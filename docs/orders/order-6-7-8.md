# Orders VI, VII & VIII — Pleadings, Amendment, Rejection of Plaint, Set-off and Counter-claim

Pleadings are the foundation of a civil trial. They define the controversy, fix the issues, limit the evidence, and bind the court. A judgment cannot travel beyond the pleadings; evidence led on an unpleaded case is no evidence at all. Everything that happens between institution and decree is constrained by what these three Orders permit you to say.

This chapter covers the three documents that constitute the pleadings — the rules that govern all of them (Order VI), the plaint (Order VII), and the written statement with its two counter-attacks, set-off and counter-claim (Order VIII).

Order VI is the grammar; Order VII is the plaintiff's sentence; Order VIII is the defendant's reply. The two most consequential rules in the chapter are **Order VI Rule 17** — which decides what you may still say — and **Order VII Rule 11** — which decides whether you get to say it at all.

## Order VI — Pleadings Generally, and Amendment

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **Rule 1. Pleading.** — "Pleading" shall mean plaint or written statement.

> **Rule 2. Pleading to state material facts and not evidence.** — **(1)** Every pleading shall contain, and contain only, a statement in a concise form of the material facts on which the party pleading relies for his claim or defence, as the case may be, but not the evidence by which they are to be proved.
> **(2)** Every pleading shall, when necessary, be divided into paragraphs, numbered consecutively, each allegation being, so far as is convenient, contained in a separate paragraph.
> **(3)** Dates, sums and numbers shall be expressed in a pleading in figures as well as in words.

> **Rule 4. Particulars to be given where necessary.** — In all cases in which the party pleading relies on any misrepresentation, fraud, breach of trust, wilful default, or undue influence, and in all other cases in which particulars may be necessary beyond such as are exemplified in the forms aforesaid, particulars (with dates and items if necessary) shall be stated in the pleading.

> **Rule 6. Condition precedent.** — Any condition precedent, the performance or occurrence of which is intended to be contested, shall be distinctly specified in his pleading by the plaintiff or defendant, as the case may be; and, subject thereto, an averment of the performance or occurrence of all conditions precedent necessary for the case of the plaintiff or defendant shall be implied in his pleading.

> **Rule 15. Verification of pleadings.** — **(1)** Save as otherwise provided by any law for the time being in force, every pleading shall be verified at the foot by the party or by one of the parties pleading or by some other person proved to the satisfaction of the Court to be acquainted with the facts of the case.
> **(2)** The person verifying shall specify, by reference to the numbered paragraphs of the pleading, what he verifies of his own knowledge and what he verifies upon information received and believed to be true.
> **(3)** The verification shall be signed by the person making it and shall state the date on and the place at which it was signed.
> **(4)** The person verifying the pleading shall also furnish an affidavit in support of his pleadings.

> **Rule 16. Striking out pleadings.** — The Court may at any stage of the proceedings order to be struck out or amended any matter in any pleading —
> **(a)** which may be unnecessary, scandalous, frivolous or vexatious, or
> **(b)** which may tend to prejudice, embarrass or delay the fair trial of the suit, or
> **(c)** which is otherwise an abuse of the process of the Court.

> **Rule 17. Amendment of pleadings.** — The Court may at any stage of the proceedings allow either party to alter or amend his pleadings in such manner and on such terms as may be just, and all such amendments shall be made as may be necessary for the purpose of determining the real questions in controversy between the parties:
> **Provided that** no application for amendment shall be allowed after the trial has commenced, unless the Court comes to the conclusion that in spite of due diligence, the party could not have raised the matter before the commencement of trial.

**Statutory mechanics.**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "shall contain, **and contain only**, a statement in a **concise form** of the **material facts** … **but not the evidence**" (R. 2(1)) | **Mandatory, exclusionary** | Three commands in one sentence: state material facts; state them concisely; do not state evidence. |
| "Dates, sums and numbers **shall** be expressed … in **figures as well as in words**" (R. 2(3)) | **Mandatory in form** | Routinely ignored; occasionally fatal where an ambiguity in a figure is exploited. |
| "**particulars** (with dates and items if necessary) **shall** be stated" (R. 4) | **Mandatory** | Fraud, misrepresentation, breach of trust, wilful default and undue influence **cannot** be pleaded generally. |
| "an averment of the performance … of all conditions precedent … **shall be implied**" (R. 6) | **Deeming provision** | The plaintiff need not plead performance; but the defendant **must distinctly specify** any condition precedent he intends to contest. |
| "The Court **may** at any stage … order to be struck out" (R. 16) | **Discretionary** | Three independent grounds: (a) unnecessary/scandalous/frivolous/vexatious; (b) prejudice, embarrass or delay; (c) abuse of process. |
| "The Court **may** at **any stage** … allow either party to alter or amend" (R. 17, main limb) | **Wide discretion** | Deliberately generous — the object is the determination of the real controversy, not the punishment of bad drafting. |
| "**all such amendments shall be made as may be necessary** for the purpose of determining the real questions in controversy" | **Mandatory, once necessity is found** | The word shifts from "may" to "shall": where the amendment *is* necessary to determine the real controversy, the court is obliged to allow it. |
| "**Provided that** no application … shall be allowed **after the trial has commenced**" | **Prohibitory bar** (inserted 2002) | The default position after commencement of trial is refusal. |
| "**unless** the Court comes to the conclusion that **in spite of due diligence**, the party **could not** have raised the matter before" | **Sole exception; heavy burden** | Note the standard: not "did not", but "**could not**", and "in spite of **due diligence**". |

**The structure of Rule 17 in one line.** Before trial commences: liberal, and the court *shall* allow what is necessary. After trial commences: prohibited, unless due diligence is affirmatively established. **The whole of amendment practice turns on when "trial commences".**

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — Material facts versus material particulars versus evidence.** Three tiers, constantly confused:

| Tier | What it is | Must it be pleaded? |
|---|---|---|
| **Material facts** | The facts which, if not proved, mean the claim or defence fails. The skeleton of the cause of action. | **Yes, always** — R. 2(1) |
| **Material particulars** | The detail that fleshes out a material fact so the opponent knows the case he must meet — dates, items, names, amounts | **Yes**, where R. 4 applies or where fairness requires |
| **Evidence** | The means by which the facts are proved — witnesses, documents, admissions | **No** — expressly excluded by R. 2(1) |

Example, in a suit on a loan: *that the defendant borrowed ₹10 lakh from the plaintiff on 3 March 2023 and agreed to repay with interest at 12% by 3 March 2024, and has failed to repay* — these are **material facts**. *That the loan was disbursed by RTGS from account no. X to account no. Y on that date* — **particulars**. *That Mr. Sharma of the bank will depose to the transfer and produce the statement* — **evidence**, and it has no place in the pleading.

**Sub-module B — Rule 4 and the pleading of fraud.** Fraud, misrepresentation, breach of trust, wilful default and undue influence are serious allegations that carry professional and personal consequences, and the Code demands that they be particularised. A pleading that alleges "the defendant fraudulently and by misrepresentation obtained the said document" and stops there is bad in law and liable to be struck out under Rule 16. What is required: **who** made the false representation, **to whom**, **when**, **in what terms**, **what was false about it**, **whether the maker knew it to be false**, and **how the plaintiff was induced to act on it**.

**Sub-module C — Rule 6 and conditions precedent.** The plaintiff gets a free pass: performance of conditions precedent is *implied* in his pleading. But the burden shifts the moment the defendant wants to contest one — he must "distinctly specify" it. A defendant who intends to say "the plaintiff never issued the notice required by clause 14" must plead that specifically; a general denial will not do.

**Sub-module D — When does "trial commence" for the Rule 17 proviso?** This is the pivotal question in amendment practice, and it is contested. The competing positions:

| View | "Trial commences" at | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Narrow** | The date on which **evidence begins to be recorded** — examination-in-chief of the first witness | Amendments remain freely available through the framing of issues and even after |
| **Wide** | The date on which **issues are framed** | The window closes much earlier |
| **Practical / prevailing approach in most courts** | The recording of evidence, but with the court weighing delay heavily as issues advance | A sliding scale rather than a cliff edge |

The safer working assumption for the practitioner is the **narrow** view for the purpose of arguing that your amendment is still in time, coupled with the recognition that the closer you are to evidence, the more you must justify. **Do not build a strategy on the point.** Amend early.

**Sub-module E — The principles governing amendment.** Distilled from a long line of authority, the court asks:

1. **Is the amendment necessary to determine the real controversy?** If yes, that is a strong reason to allow it.
2. **Can the other side be compensated in costs?** If the prejudice is curable by costs, it is not real prejudice.
3. **Does the amendment cause injustice that costs cannot cure?** The classic instance: the amendment deprives the opponent of an **accrued defence of limitation**.
4. **Does the amendment withdraw a clear admission?** Courts are reluctant, though not absolutely barred, from permitting a party to resile from an admission made in a pleading.
5. **Does the amendment change the fundamental character of the suit** — converting a suit for injunction into a suit for title, or a contract claim into a tort claim? Generally impermissible.
6. **Is the application bona fide, or is it a device to delay?**
7. **Has trial commenced?** If so, the due-diligence gate applies.

**Sub-module F — The limitation objection to an amendment.** This is the defendant's most powerful answer. If the amendment introduces a **new cause of action** that was time-barred on the date the amendment application was filed, allowing it would deprive the defendant of a vested defence. The general rule: an amendment which merely **elaborates or clarifies** what was already pleaded relates back to the date of the plaint; an amendment which introduces a **new and distinct cause of action** does not, and if that cause of action is time-barred as on the date of the application, it will not be allowed.

**Sub-module G — Amendment of the written statement is treated more liberally.** The rationale is asymmetry of consequence: an amendment to a plaint may take away a defendant's accrued right (limitation); an amendment to a written statement merely adds a defence, and the plaintiff can usually be compensated in costs. Courts therefore approach defence amendments — including inconsistent and alternative pleas — with greater latitude, though not without limit.

**Sub-module H — Rule 16, striking out.** A separate and underused weapon. Where a pleading contains matter that is scandalous (imputations of criminality or immorality irrelevant to the issues), or that will embarrass the trial (prolix, irrelevant, argumentative material), or that is an abuse of process, the court may strike it out **at any stage**. It is the answer to the pleading that is not defective enough for Rule 11 rejection but is designed to prejudice.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — the amendment that adds a time-barred cause of action.** P sues D in 2023 for recovery of ₹15 lakh advanced as a loan in 2021. In 2026, after issues are framed but before evidence, P applies to amend the plaint to add, in the alternative, a claim for the same sum as damages for breach of a distinct supply contract of 2020.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P argues that the amendment merely puts an alternative legal characterisation on the same money, that no new facts are introduced, and that the object of Rule 17 is to determine the real controversy.
- **Shield (defendant):** D answers that the supply contract of 2020 is a wholly distinct transaction requiring proof of different material facts — the contract, its terms, the breach and the loss — and is therefore a **new cause of action**. Under Article 55 of the Limitation Act it became time-barred in 2023. Allowing the amendment would deprive D of an accrued defence of limitation, which is prejudice that costs cannot compensate.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The amendment sought introduces a new and distinct cause of action which was barred by limitation on the date of the application; to allow it would be to take away from my client a defence that had already accrued, and that is prejudice which no order as to costs can repair."
:::

**Illustration 2 — the post-trial amendment and due diligence.** A specific performance suit. Evidence of PW-1 has been recorded and cross-examination is part-heard. D applies to amend the written statement to plead that the agreement was a forgery, relying on a handwriting expert's opinion obtained the previous week.

- **Sword (defendant):** D must clear the proviso. He pleads that the original agreement was produced by P only at the stage of evidence, that D had never had access to it before, that he applied for inspection under Order XI immediately upon its production, obtained the expert opinion within weeks, and moved at once. On those facts he can say that **in spite of due diligence** he could not have raised the matter before trial commenced.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** P attacks the diligence narrative: the agreement was filed with the plaint under Order VII Rule 14 and listed in the list of documents; D never sought inspection under Order XI Rule 15; D's own written statement admitted execution while disputing consideration; and D is now seeking to withdraw an admission. P also submits that allowing a forgery plea at this stage would alter the fundamental character of the defence.

::: warning Drafting Trap — the admission you cannot take back
A written statement that says "the agreement was signed but under coercion" has **admitted execution**. An application four years later to plead forgery is an application to withdraw an admission, and it will be resisted on that footing quite apart from the Rule 17 proviso. Decide the defence before the written statement is filed, and plead alternatives expressly and consistently: *"the Defendant denies execution; without prejudice and in the alternative, if execution be proved, the same was obtained by coercion in the following circumstances…"*
:::

**Illustration 3 — fraud pleaded without particulars.** P's plaint alleges: "The Defendants, in collusion with one another and by playing a fraud upon the Plaintiff, got the sale deed executed and thereby cheated the Plaintiff."

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P will resist, saying the details will emerge in evidence.
- **Shield (defendant):** D applies under **Order VI Rule 16** to strike out the paragraph as scandalous and embarrassing, and under **Order VI Rule 4** on the ground that particulars of fraud have not been given with dates and items. The pleading tells D nothing about what representation was made, by whom, when, or how P was induced. D is entitled to know the case he must meet.
- **The correct pleading:** *"On 12 April 2022 at about 11 a.m., at the office of the Sub-Registrar, [place], Defendant No. 1 represented to the Plaintiff that the document being presented for registration was an agreement of leave and licence for eleven months. The said representation was false to the knowledge of Defendant No. 1, in that the document was in fact a deed of sale. The Plaintiff, who is illiterate in English and relied upon the said representation, affixed his thumb impression thereto. The particulars of fraud are set out in Schedule C hereto."*

**Illustration 4 — striking out under Rule 16.** D's written statement in a partnership accounts suit devotes eleven paragraphs to allegations about P's personal life, his prior criminal complaint, and his alleged tax evasion, none of which bears on the accounts.

- **Sword (defendant):** Claims the material goes to P's credibility and to the conduct relevant to discretionary relief.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** P applies under **Rule 16(a) and (b)** to strike out the paragraphs as scandalous and as tending to prejudice and embarrass the fair trial of the suit. Credibility is a matter for cross-examination, not for pleading; and matters extraneous to the issues have no place in a pleading which must contain "only" material facts.

**Illustration 5 — the amendment that saves the suit.** P files a suit for a permanent injunction restraining D from interfering with P's possession. In the written statement D asserts title and denies P's possession. Before issues are framed, P applies to amend to add a prayer for declaration of title and, in the alternative, possession.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** This is the paradigm case for allowing amendment. The real controversy, as disclosed by the written statement itself, is title; without the amendment the suit would be liable to fail for want of the declaratory relief (the proviso to Section 34 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963, bars a declaration without consequential relief where the plaintiff is able to seek it). The amendment is necessary to determine the real question in controversy, trial has not commenced, and no accrued right of D is disturbed.
- **Shield (defendant):** D's realistic objections are (i) court fee — the amended plaint must bear ad valorem fee on the declaration and possession claims; and (ii) limitation, if the amendment introduces a possession claim already barred under Article 65.

### 4. Procedural Interlocking Matrix

| Order VI provision | Interlocks with | What the connection does |
|---|---|---|
| R. 2 (material facts only) | O. VII R. 11(a) — no cause of action | A plaint that pleads conclusions but no material facts discloses no cause of action and is liable to be rejected. R. 2 is the standard against which R. 11(a) is measured. |
| R. 2 (material facts) | O. XIV R. 1 — framing of issues | Issues arise only from material propositions affirmed by one party and denied by the other. A fact not pleaded generates no issue; an issue not framed generates no finding. |
| R. 4 (particulars of fraud) | S. 17, Limitation Act, 1963; O. VII R. 6 | Fraud that founds a plea for extension of time under S. 17 must be pleaded with R. 4 particulars, and the exemption from limitation must itself be pleaded under O. VII R. 6. |
| R. 6 (conditions precedent) | Contract terms; statutory notices | Performance of a condition precedent is implied and need not be pleaded; non-performance must be *specifically* pleaded by the party alleging it. Contrast S. 80 CPC notice, which the plaintiff must affirmatively plead under O. VII R. 1. |
| RR. 14, 14A (signature; address) | O. VII R. 11(f); O. VI R. 15 | Defects in signature, verification or the registered address are curable irregularities, not nullities — but persistent non-compliance with R. 14A can result in the pleading being struck off the record. |
| RR. 15, 15A (verification; affidavit) | S. 26(2) CPC | Every plaint must be supported by an affidavit under S. 26(2) read with O. VI R. 15(4). R. 15A substitutes a **statement of truth** in commercial suits, with perjury consequences. |
| R. 16 (striking out) | S. 151; O. VII R. 11 | R. 16 excises offending paragraphs; R. 11 rejects the whole plaint. Where the offending matter is severable, the court strikes out under R. 16 rather than rejecting under R. 11. |
| R. 17 (amendment) | O. I R. 10; O. II R. 2; S. 21 Limitation Act | Amendment adding a *party* runs on O. I R. 10 read with S. 21 (suit deemed instituted against the new defendant on the date of impleadment). Amendment adding a *claim* runs on R. 17 read against the O. II R. 2 bar. |
| R. 17 proviso ("due diligence") | O. XVIII R. 4 (recording of evidence) | The proviso bites once trial commences. The dominant practical marker of commencement is the filing of the examination-in-chief affidavit under O. XVIII R. 4 — not the framing of issues. |
| R. 18 (failure to amend) | Lapse of leave | Leave must be acted upon within the time fixed, failing which within **14 days** of the order. Thereafter the party cannot amend except with fresh leave. A genuine and frequently fatal trap. |

**Limitation and fee consequences of an amendment**

| Question | Position |
|---|---|
| From what date does an amended pleading speak? | Ordinarily the amendment **relates back** to the date of institution of the original plaint. |
| When does relation back *not* apply? | Where the amendment introduces a new cause of action or a claim already time-barred on the date the amendment application was made, the court will normally refuse leave, or grant it expressly subject to the limitation defence being kept alive as an issue. |
| Amendment adding a defendant | S. 21, Limitation Act, 1963 — the suit is deemed instituted against him on the date of impleadment, unless the court is satisfied the omission was due to a mistake made in good faith and directs otherwise. |
| Court fee | An amendment enhancing the valuation or adding a relief attracts ad valorem fee on the enhanced claim. Leave is routinely made conditional on payment of deficit court fee within a fixed time. |

**Applications actually filed**

- **IA under O. VI R. 17 read with S. 151** — leave to amend, supported by affidavit and accompanied by the proposed amended pleading, or by a schedule of amendments in the "existing text / proposed text / reason" tabular form.
- **IA under O. VI R. 16** — to strike out scandalous, frivolous or vexatious matter.
- **IA for further and better particulars** — where the pleading states a conclusion (fraud, undue influence, misrepresentation) without the R. 4 particulars.

::: warning The challenge route is narrow
An order granting or refusing amendment is **not** in the list of appealable orders under Order XLIII Rule 1. The only route is a revision under **Section 115** (where the State amendment permits it) or a petition under **Article 227** of the Constitution. Draft the amendment application on the footing that you will not get a second look at it.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Udhav Singh v. Madhav Rao Scindia**, (1977) 1 SCC 511 — Supreme Court of India, three-Judge Bench.

*Facts:* An election petition was attacked for omitting material facts, the pleading requirements for such petitions being governed by the CPC standard.

**Ratio decidendi:** All the primary facts which must be proved by a party to establish a cause of action or defence are "material facts". The distinction is between *material facts*, which must be pleaded, and *particulars*, which are the details of the case set up and which serve to sharpen the issues. Omission of a single material fact renders the pleading defective; omission of particulars is curable by amendment or by an order for further and better particulars.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A material fact is one which, if omitted, leaves the cause of action incomplete; a particular is one which, if omitted, merely leaves the opponent inadequately informed. The first is fatal, the second is curable."
:::

---

**Salem Advocate Bar Association (II) v. Union of India**, (2005) 6 SCC 344 — Supreme Court of India, three-Judge Bench.

*Facts:* A comprehensive challenge to the 1999 and 2002 amendments to the CPC, including the newly inserted proviso to Order VI Rule 17.

**Ratio decidendi:** The proviso to Rule 17 was introduced to curb the mischief of dilatory amendments sought after trial had begun. It is not a bar in absolute terms; the court retains power to allow amendment after commencement of trial, but only where the applicant satisfies the court that in spite of **due diligence** the matter could not have been raised before trial commenced. The requirement of due diligence is a condition precedent to the exercise of the discretion, not a factor to be weighed among others.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"After the commencement of trial the discretion under Rule 17 does not arise at all until the threshold of due diligence is crossed; the applicant must first plead and prove diligence, and only then does the court reach the merits of the amendment."
:::

---

**Revajeetu Builders and Developers v. Narayanaswamy and Sons**, (2009) 10 SCC 84 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* An application to amend the plaint was refused; the Supreme Court took the occasion to consolidate the principles governing amendment.

**Ratio decidendi:** The factors relevant to the exercise of discretion are: (i) whether the amendment is necessary for determining the real question in controversy; (ii) whether the application could have been made at an earlier stage; (iii) whether refusal would cause injustice or lead to multiplicity of proceedings; (iv) whether the amendment changes the nature of the suit; (v) whether it is used to defeat the opposite party's accrued rights; and (vi) whether it can be compensated in costs. **No straitjacket formula exists**; the overriding test is whether the amendment is necessary to do justice between the parties.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The two indispensable questions are whether the amendment is necessary to determine the real controversy, and whether it takes away a right accrued to the opposite party by lapse of time. Everything else can be met by costs."
:::

---

**Chander Kanta Bansal v. Rajinder Singh Anand**, (2008) 5 SCC 117 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* An amendment was sought after trial had commenced; the plea was that the documents in question came to the applicant's knowledge only later.

**Ratio decidendi:** "Due diligence" means the reasonable diligence a person of ordinary prudence would exercise, measured by "reasonable persistence, and an attempt to do a thing which will reasonably effect the desired result". The applicant must show what steps were actually taken and why the material could not have been obtained earlier. A bare assertion that the facts "came to knowledge subsequently", unsupported by any account of what was done, does not discharge the burden.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Due diligence is proved by narrating the steps taken, not by asserting the conclusion. The affidavit must set out dates, enquiries made, and the reason the material was unavailable despite them."
:::

---

**Rajesh Kumar Aggarwal v. K.K. Modi**, (2006) 4 SCC 385 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* An amendment to the plaint was refused on the ground that it would alter the character of the suit.

**Ratio decidendi:** At the stage of considering an amendment the court is **not** to go into the merits of the proposed amendment or to decide whether the amended plea will succeed. The merits of the amendment are irrelevant; the only questions are whether the amendment is necessary to determine the real controversy and whether it prejudices an accrued right. The court must not, at that stage, pre-judge the very plea sought to be introduced.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"An amendment cannot be refused on the ground that the plea sought to be introduced is weak. The strength of the plea is a matter for trial, not for the amendment application."
:::

---

**Bishwanath Prasad Singh v. Rajendra Prasad**, (2006) 4 SCC 432 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* Concerned the treatment of an inconsistent plea introduced by amendment of a written statement.

**Ratio decidendi:** The court is more liberal in allowing amendment of a **written statement** than of a plaint. A defendant is entitled to take inconsistent and even alternative defences, provided they are not mutually destructive in a manner that amounts to an abuse of process; the addition of a plea to the written statement does not deprive the plaintiff of any accrued right, since the plaintiff's cause of action remains as pleaded.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Adding a defence takes nothing away from the plaintiff; adding a cause of action may take a limitation defence away from the defendant. That asymmetry is why written statements are amended more freely than plaints."
:::

Citations, bench strength and the current status of each of these authorities must be checked against the official SCC/SCR report and a current citator before the judgment is relied upon in court. Bench strength in particular should be confirmed from the reported judgment itself.

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**Drafting traps in the pleading itself**

| Trap | Consequence | Cure |
|---|---|---|
| Pleading evidence instead of material facts | Prolix pleading; paragraphs liable to be struck out under R. 16; the material fact gets lost and no issue is framed on it | Draft each paragraph to answer: "is this a fact I must prove to succeed?" If no, it is evidence — move it to the affidavit of examination-in-chief. |
| Alleging "fraud", "collusion", "undue influence" without particulars | The plea is treated as non-existent; no issue is framed; no evidence is admissible on it | Every allegation under R. 4 must carry date, place, person, the exact representation, its falsity, and the reliance. Put the particulars in a numbered Schedule. |
| Vague denial in the written statement | Deemed admission under O. VIII RR. 3–5 | Deal with each paragraph of the plaint specifically; never say "the contents of paragraphs 4 to 11 are denied". |
| Omitting to plead the ground of exemption from limitation | Plaint liable to rejection under O. VII R. 11(d) | O. VII R. 6 requires the ground of exemption to be **specifically** pleaded in the plaint itself. It cannot be introduced for the first time in argument. |
| Verification not in the prescribed form; no S. 26(2) affidavit | Curable irregularity, but invites an objection at the threshold and delays numbering | Verify with paragraph numbers, distinguishing what is true to knowledge from what is true to information and belief, and state the source of the information. |

**Traps in the amendment application**

::: danger The four failure modes of an amendment application
1. **Filed after the examination-in-chief affidavit.** The proviso then applies and you must plead and prove due diligence. Once evidence has begun, an amendment application without a diligence narrative is dead on arrival.
2. **No account of diligence.** "The said facts came to the knowledge of the applicant recently" is not a plea of due diligence. Set out what enquiries were made, when, with whom, and why the material could not have been obtained earlier.
3. **The amendment is time-barred on its own facts.** If the new claim would be barred were it sued upon today, expect refusal, or expect the court to keep the limitation defence alive. Anticipate this in the application and address it.
4. **Leave granted but not carried out.** Order VI Rule 18 — if you do not amend within the time fixed, or in any event within 14 days of the order, the leave lapses. Diarise the date the moment the order is passed.
:::

**Strategic timing**

| Objective | File it when |
|---|---|
| Amend the plaint to cure a defect the written statement has exposed | **Immediately** on receipt of the written statement and before issues are framed. This is the safest window: R. 17's main limb applies, the proviso does not, and the necessity is demonstrated by the defendant's own pleading. |
| Amend to add a relief you may have forgotten | Before trial commences, and in any event before the O. II R. 2 point can be taken in a later suit. |
| Amend the written statement to add a defence | Any time before trial; and even after, the court is more liberal, since no accrued right of the plaintiff is disturbed. Still plead diligence if trial has begun. |
| Strike out scandalous matter under R. 16 | Promptly, and before framing of issues, so that the objectionable matter does not generate an issue. |

**Model prayer — application for leave to amend the plaint**

> It is therefore most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to:
>
> **(a)** Grant leave to the Plaintiff to amend the plaint in the manner set out in the Schedule of Amendments annexed hereto and marked Annexure A-1;
>
> **(b)** Permit the Plaintiff to file the amended plaint within such time as this Hon'ble Court may fix, and to pay such additional court fee as may be found payable on the amended valuation;
>
> **(c)** Direct that the Defendant be at liberty to file an additional written statement to the amended plaint within the time prescribed by Order VIII Rule 1;
>
> **(d)** Pass such other and further orders as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit in the facts and circumstances of the case, and in the interests of justice.

::: tip The Schedule of Amendments
Never describe the amendment in prose. Annex a three-column schedule — **Paragraph / Existing text / Proposed text** — with a fourth column stating the reason for each change. Judges grant applications they can read in ninety seconds.
:::

## Order VII — The Plaint, and Rejection of the Plaint

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **Rule 1. Particulars to be contained in plaint.** — The plaint shall contain the following particulars: —
> (a) the name of the Court in which the suit is brought;
> (b) the name, description and place of residence of the plaintiff;
> (c) the name, description and place of residence of the defendant, so far as they can be ascertained;
> (d) where the plaintiff or the defendant is a minor or a person of unsound mind, a statement to that effect;
> (e) the facts constituting the cause of action and when it arose;
> (f) the facts showing that the Court has jurisdiction;
> (g) the relief which the plaintiff claims;
> (h) where the plaintiff has allowed a set-off or relinquished a portion of his claim, the amount so allowed or relinquished; and
> (i) a statement of the value of the subject-matter of the suit for the purposes of jurisdiction and of court-fees, so far as the case admits.

> **Rule 2. In money suits.** — Where the plaintiff seeks the recovery of money, the plaint shall state the precise amount claimed…

> **Rule 3. Where the subject-matter of the suit is immovable property.** — Where the subject-matter of the suit is immovable property, the plaint shall contain a description of the property sufficient to identify it, and, in case such property can be identified by boundaries or numbers in a record of settlement or survey, the plaint shall specify such boundaries or numbers.

> **Rule 6. Grounds of exemption from limitation law.** — Where the suit is instituted after the expiration of the period prescribed by the law of limitation, the plaint shall show the ground upon which exemption from such law is claimed…

> **Rule 7. Relief to be specifically stated.** — Every plaint shall state specifically the relief which the plaintiff claims either simply or in the alternative, and it shall not be necessary to ask for general or other relief which may always be given as the Court may think just to the same extent as if it had been asked for. And the same rule shall apply to any relief claimed by the defendant in his written statement.

> **Rule 11. Rejection of plaint.** — The plaint shall be rejected in the following cases: —
> **(a)** where it does not disclose a cause of action;
> **(b)** where the relief claimed is undervalued, and the plaintiff, on being required by the Court to correct the valuation within a time to be fixed by the Court, fails to do so;
> **(c)** where the relief claimed is properly valued, but the plaint is written upon paper insufficiently stamped, and the plaintiff, on being required by the Court to supply the requisite stamp-paper within a time to be fixed by the Court, fails to do so;
> **(d)** where the suit appears from the statement in the plaint to be barred by any law;
> **(e)** where it is not filed in duplicate;
> **(f)** where the plaintiff fails to comply with the provisions of rule 9:
>
> *Provided that the time fixed by the Court for the correction of the valuation or supplying of the requisite stamp-paper shall not be extended unless the Court, for reasons to be recorded, is satisfied that the plaintiff was prevented by any cause of an exceptional nature…*

> **Rule 12. Procedure on rejecting plaint.** — Where a plaint is rejected the Judge shall record an order to that effect with the reasons for such order.

> **Rule 13. Where rejection of plaint does not preclude presentation of fresh plaint.** — The rejection of the plaint on any of the grounds hereinbefore mentioned shall not of its own force preclude the plaintiff from presenting a fresh plaint in respect of the same cause of action.

> **Rule 14. Production of document on which plaintiff sues or relies.** — (1) Where a plaintiff sues upon a document or relies upon document in his possession or power in support of his claim, he shall enter such documents in a list, and shall produce it in Court when the plaint is presented… (3) A document which ought to be produced in Court by the plaintiff when the plaint is presented… shall not, without the leave of the Court, be received in evidence on his behalf at the hearing of the suit.

**Statutory mechanics**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| R. 1: "**shall** contain the following particulars" | Mandatory in form, but non-compliance is generally curable | Omission of a R. 1 particular ordinarily attracts a return for amendment, not rejection — except where the omission is of the *cause of action* (R. 1(e)), which engages R. 11(a). |
| R. 1(e): "the facts constituting the cause of action **and when it arose**" | Mandatory | The *date* the cause of action arose is a distinct requirement. Its omission is the single most common route to a limitation objection at the threshold. |
| R. 1(f): "facts showing that the Court has jurisdiction" | Mandatory | Pecuniary, territorial and subject-matter jurisdiction must each be affirmatively pleaded. See ss. 15–20 CPC. |
| R. 6: "**shall** show the ground upon which exemption… is claimed" | Mandatory; condition precedent to the plea | A ground of exemption not pleaded in the plaint cannot be argued. Contrast the proviso, which permits the court to allow reliance on a ground not set out, if inconsistent with the pleaded ground, at the hearing. |
| R. 7: "**shall** state specifically the relief" | Mandatory as to specific relief; permissive as to general relief | Relief not specifically claimed is generally not granted; but "general or other relief" need not be asked for and may be granted as if asked. Alternative reliefs are expressly permitted. |
| R. 11: "**The plaint shall be rejected**" | Mandatory, not discretionary | Once a ground under R. 11 is made out, the court has **no discretion**; rejection follows. The discretion, such as it is, lies in the anterior finding that the ground exists. |
| R. 11(a): "does not disclose a cause of action" | Determined on the **plaint alone** | The written statement, the defence, and disputed questions of fact are all irrelevant. The plaint's averments are assumed true. |
| R. 11(d): "**appears from the statement in the plaint** to be barred by any law" | Determined on the plaint alone | The bar must be apparent on the face of the plaint. A bar requiring evidence to establish it cannot ground rejection under R. 11(d). |
| RR. 11(b), (c): "on being required by the Court… fails to do so" | **Condition precedent** | Rejection for undervaluation or deficit stamp is unlawful unless the court has first fixed a time and the plaintiff has then failed. There is no rejection without a prior opportunity. |
| R. 13: "shall not **of its own force** preclude" | Saving | Rejection is not res judicata. A fresh plaint on the same cause of action is competent — subject to limitation, which continues to run. |
| R. 14(3): "shall **not**, without the leave of the Court, be received in evidence" | Prohibitory, with a discretionary relaxation | A document not filed with the plaint is inadmissible unless leave is obtained. Leave is regularly granted, but it must be asked for. |

::: tip Rule 11 in one line
Rejection under Rule 11 is decided on a demurrer — read the plaint, assume every word of it is true, ignore the defence entirely, and ask whether even then the suit must fail.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — what a "cause of action" actually is.**

A cause of action is the bundle of facts which the plaintiff must prove, if traversed, in order to obtain the relief he claims. It is not the relief; it is not the legal label; it is not the evidence. Two working tests:

1. **The subtraction test** — remove any one pleaded fact. If the plaintiff can still succeed, that fact was not part of the cause of action. If he cannot, it was.
2. **The completeness test** — read the plaint alone, assume every averment true, and ask whether a decree could be passed. If not, no cause of action is disclosed.

For a suit on a contract the bundle is: the contract, its terms, the plaintiff's performance or readiness and willingness, the defendant's breach, the loss. For a suit for possession on title: the plaintiff's title, the defendant's possession, the absence of any right in the defendant to remain.

**Sub-module B — Rule 11(a): "does not disclose a cause of action".**

The four rules of engagement:

| Rule | Content |
|---|---|
| Plaint alone | The court reads the plaint and the documents filed with it. Nothing else. |
| Averments assumed true | The court does not test whether the pleaded facts are believable, only whether, if true, they yield a decree. |
| Written statement irrelevant | A strong defence, an admission of the defence, or a plainly meritorious plea in the written statement is wholly beside the point at the R. 11 stage. |
| No partial rejection | The plaint is rejected as a whole or not at all. A plaint cannot be rejected against one defendant and retained against another, nor rejected as to one relief. |

The clever pleader's antidote is the "clever drafting" doctrine: where the plaint has been artfully drafted to create an illusion of a cause of action, the court may look through the drafting device and reject. But this is a narrow exception — it addresses camouflage, not weakness.

**Sub-module C — Rule 11(d): "barred by any law".**

"Any law" is genuinely wide, and includes:

- **Limitation Act, 1963** — where the plaint itself discloses dates showing the suit is time-barred, and no ground of exemption is pleaded under R. 6.
- **Section 11 CPC** — res judicata, but only where the previous judgment is on the record and the bar is apparent without evidence.
- **Order II Rule 2** — but note that this bar cannot ordinarily be established without producing the earlier plaint, which normally puts it beyond R. 11(d).
- **Specific Relief Act, 1963**, proviso to s. 34 — a bare declaration where consequential relief could have been sought.
- **Statutory bars on jurisdiction** — s. 9 CPC read with the ouster provision in the special statute.
- **Section 80 CPC** — suit against Government without notice and without leave under s. 80(2).
- **Section 12A, Commercial Courts Act, 2015** — pre-institution mediation, where no urgent interim relief is contemplated.

The controlling words are "**appears from the statement in the plaint**". If the bar requires the court to receive evidence, take a written statement into account, or resolve a disputed fact, R. 11(d) is not available; the point must be taken as a preliminary issue under Order XIV Rule 2 instead.

**Sub-module D — Rules 11(b) and (c): valuation and stamp.**

These two grounds are conditional, and the condition is a prior order. The sequence is fixed:

1. Court forms the view that the relief is undervalued (b) or the paper insufficiently stamped (c);
2. Court **requires** the plaintiff to correct the valuation or supply the stamp paper, **fixing a time**;
3. Plaintiff fails within that time;
4. **Then** — and only then — the plaint is rejected.

The proviso adds a further constraint: the time so fixed cannot be extended unless the court records reasons and is satisfied that the plaintiff was prevented by a cause of an **exceptional nature**, and refusal to extend would cause grave injustice.

**Sub-module E — the consequences of rejection.**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Is an order of rejection a decree? | **Yes.** Section 2(2) expressly includes the rejection of a plaint within the definition of "decree". |
| Is it therefore appealable? | Yes — as a decree, under Section 96, not under Order XLIII. This is why R. 11 is the defendant's most valuable weapon: success ends the suit with a decree, not merely an order. |
| Does it bar a fresh suit? | **No.** Rule 13 — rejection does not "of its own force" preclude a fresh plaint on the same cause of action. |
| Practically, then, is rejection worth anything? | Yes, because limitation does not stop running. A plaint rejected in year six of a three-year limitation period cannot in practice be re-presented. |

**Sub-module F — Rule 10 and Rule 10A: return, distinguished from rejection.**

| | **Return** (R. 10) | **Rejection** (R. 11) |
|---|---|---|
| Ground | Plaint filed in the wrong court — no jurisdiction | One of the six R. 11 grounds |
| Nature of order | Order, appealable under O. XLIII R. 1(a) | **Decree** (s. 2(2)), appealable under s. 96 |
| Effect on the suit | Plaint is handed back for presentation to the proper court | Suit is at an end in that court |
| Limitation | The fresh presentation is a **fresh institution**; time is not saved (contrast s. 24(5) on transfer, where the suit continues) | Fresh plaint is a fresh institution |
| R. 10A | Where the plaint is returned after the defendant has appeared, the court must fix a date for appearance in the transferee court and give notice — a procedural softener | Not applicable |

**Sub-module G — Rule 7: pleading the relief.**

Relief must be stated **specifically**. Two practical corollaries:

- **Alternative relief is expressly permitted** and should be used. "Possession, or in the alternative, mesne profits" costs nothing and preserves everything.
- **General relief need not be asked for.** The final "such other and further orders as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit" clause is not the source of the court's power — the court may grant general relief as if it had been asked. But it is invariably included, and it does no harm.

The trap is the proviso to **Section 34 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963**: a plaintiff entitled to consequential relief who omits to claim it cannot obtain a bare declaration. A suit for declaration of title, where the plaintiff is out of possession, must also claim possession.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — the limitation bar apparent on the face of the plaint.** P files a suit in 2025 for recovery of ₹18,00,000 due under an invoice. Paragraph 4 of the plaint states that the goods were delivered on 3 March 2019 and payment fell due on 2 April 2019. Paragraph 9 states the cause of action arose on 2 April 2019. There is no plea of acknowledgment and no plea under Order VII Rule 6.

- **Sword (defendant):** Application under **R. 11(d)**. The suit is governed by Article 14 or 15 of the Limitation Act, 1963 (three years). The plaint's own dates show the suit is barred by more than three years. No ground of exemption is pleaded, as R. 6 requires. The bar appears from the statement in the plaint; no evidence is needed.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** The only real answer is an acknowledgment in writing under **s. 18** or a part-payment under **s. 19** of the Limitation Act. But this must be *pleaded*, not argued — hence an immediate application under Order VI Rule 17 to amend the plaint to plead the acknowledgment letter of, say, 12 January 2021 and to annex it. Once pleaded, the bar no longer "appears from the statement in the plaint" and R. 11(d) fails.

::: warning The sequencing point
Where a Rule 11 application and an amendment application are both pending, the court ordinarily decides the **amendment application first**, because Rule 11 operates on the plaint as it stands, and the plaint as it stands may be about to change. File the amendment the moment the Rule 11 application is served.
:::

**Illustration 2 — the bare declaration.** P, who was dispossessed from a shop in 2021, files a suit in 2023 seeking only a declaration that he is the owner of the shop. He does not claim possession.

- **Sword (defendant):** Application under **R. 11(d)** relying on the proviso to **s. 34 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963**. P is able to seek the further relief of possession and has omitted to do so; the court "shall not make any such declaration". The bar is apparent from the plaint, which itself pleads dispossession.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** Amend to add a prayer for possession, and pay the ad valorem court fee on the market value. Note the second-order risk: if the amendment is allowed in 2026 and the dispossession was in 2021, the possession claim under Article 65 (twelve years) survives; but had the dispossession been under Article 64 (dispossession — twelve years from dispossession) the analysis changes. Check the article before conceding.

**Illustration 3 — Rule 11(a) where the plaint pleads conclusions.** P's plaint against a bank reads: "The Defendant has acted illegally, arbitrarily and in bad faith, and has caused the Plaintiff loss of ₹2 crore. The Plaintiff is entitled to damages." There is no averment of any contract, any duty, any specific act, or any date.

- **Sword (defendant):** Application under **R. 11(a)**. The plaint pleads conclusions of law and epithets, not material facts. Assuming every word true, no decree can follow, because the plaint does not identify what the defendant did, when, or under what obligation. It fails Order VI Rule 2 and therefore Order VII Rule 11(a).
- **Shield (plaintiff):** There is no answer on the pleading as it stands. The plaintiff must apply to amend, setting out the account, the instructions, the date of the unauthorised debit, and the loss.

**Illustration 4 — Rule 11 raised as a defence to injunction proceedings.** P sues for a permanent injunction restraining D from executing an award, and obtains an ex parte order under Order XXXIX Rules 1 and 2. D applies under R. 11(d), contending that s. 5 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 bars the civil court's intervention.

- **Sword (defendant):** The bar is statutory and appears from the plaint, which itself pleads the existence of the arbitral award. R. 11(d) is squarely attracted, and its success produces a **decree**, extinguishing the injunction with it.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** The plaintiff's answer must be that the suit is not one "in respect of matters governed by Part I" of the 1996 Act — for example, that he is a stranger to the arbitration agreement. If that is so, it must be visible on the face of the plaint.

**Illustration 5 — undervaluation.** In a suit for specific performance of an agreement to sell for ₹95,00,000, P values the suit at ₹95,00,000 and pays fee accordingly. D contends the market value is ₹4 crore and that fee is payable on market value.

- **Sword (defendant):** Application under **R. 11(b)/(c)**.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** Two answers. First, on the merits — under the Court Fees Act as applied in most States, fee in a specific performance suit is payable on the **consideration stated in the agreement**, not on market value. Second, and decisively, on procedure: **rejection cannot follow immediately**. The court must first require correction within a fixed time. Even if D is right, P is entitled to an opportunity to pay the deficit, and the suit is rejected only if he then fails.

**Illustration 6 — the partial rejection that cannot be granted.** P sues four defendants: D1 on a contract, and D2–D4 as guarantors. The plaint discloses a cause of action against D1 but pleads nothing whatever against D2–D4 beyond describing them as guarantors.

- **Sword (defendants 2–4):** They apply under R. 11(a) to reject the plaint as against them.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** The plaint cannot be rejected **in part**. Rule 11 operates on the plaint as a whole; if a cause of action is disclosed against D1, the plaint stands. The correct remedy for D2–D4 is not R. 11 but an application to be struck out as parties under **Order I Rule 10(2)**, or a preliminary issue under Order XIV Rule 2.

### 4. Procedural Interlocking Matrix

| Order VII provision | Interlocks with | What the connection does |
|---|---|---|
| R. 1(e) — cause of action and when it arose | Limitation Act, 1963, Schedule | The pleaded date of accrual fixes the starting point under the relevant Article. Pleading it carelessly hands the defendant a Rule 11(d) application. |
| R. 1(f) — facts showing jurisdiction | Ss. 15–20 CPC | Pecuniary (s. 15), territorial (ss. 16–20) and subject-matter (s. 9) jurisdiction must each be affirmatively pleaded, with the Explanation to s. 20 addressed where a corporation is a defendant. |
| R. 1(i) — valuation | Suits Valuation Act, 1887; State Court Fees Acts | Valuation for jurisdiction and valuation for court fee are separate exercises and may produce different figures. Both must be stated. |
| R. 6 — grounds of exemption from limitation | Ss. 5, 12–24, Limitation Act, 1963 | Acknowledgment (s. 18), part payment (s. 19), fraud (s. 17), disability (s. 6) — each must be pleaded in the plaint to be available. |
| R. 7 — relief specifically stated | S. 34 proviso, Specific Relief Act, 1963 | A declaration without consequential relief, where such relief could be claimed, is barred. Directly grounds a Rule 11(d) application. |
| R. 9 — procedure on admitting plaint | O. V R. 1 — issue of summons | Failure to comply with R. 9 (supplying copies and process fee) is itself a ground of rejection under R. 11(f). |
| R. 10 — return of plaint | S. 15–20; O. XLIII R. 1(a) | Return for want of jurisdiction. Appealable as an order. Time is **not** saved — contrast s. 24(5) on transfer, where the suit continues. |
| R. 10A — notice on return | O. VII R. 10 | Where the defendant has appeared, the court fixes a date for appearance before the proper court, avoiding a fresh round of service. |
| R. 11(a) | O. VI R. 2 | Material facts are the currency of a cause of action; R. 11(a) is R. 2 enforced. |
| R. 11(d) | S. 11 CPC; O. II R. 2; s. 80 CPC; s. 12A Commercial Courts Act, 2015 | Each is a "law" capable of barring the suit — but only where the bar is apparent on the plaint without evidence. |
| R. 11 (order of rejection) | **S. 2(2) CPC**; S. 96 | Rejection is a **decree**. It carries a first appeal, not a Rule 1 Order XLIII appeal. This single fact makes R. 11 the most valuable defence application in the Code. |
| R. 13 | Limitation Act | A fresh plaint is competent, but limitation runs throughout. Rejection is only as valuable as the time that has passed. |
| R. 14 — production of documents | O. XIII R. 1; O. VIII R. 1A (defendant's documents) | Documents not filed with the plaint are inadmissible without leave. The mirror obligation on the defendant is O. VIII R. 1A. |
| R. 18 (as amended) | O. XIII R. 1 | Post-2002, the consolidated position is that documents must accompany the pleading; belated production requires leave and the court's reasons. |

**The interim applications that actually accompany a plaint**

| Application | Provision | When |
|---|---|---|
| Application for interim injunction | O. XXXIX RR. 1, 2 | Filed with the plaint; supported by affidavit; ex parte relief requires compliance with R. 3 proviso (reasons to be recorded, notice and papers to be served forthwith). |
| Application for attachment before judgment | O. XXXVIII R. 5 | Requires specific pleading of intent to defeat or delay the decree. |
| Application for appointment of receiver | O. XL R. 1 | Where the property requires preservation and management. |
| Application to condone delay | S. 5, Limitation Act, 1963 | Note: s. 5 does **not** apply to suits, only to appeals and applications. The plaint's own answer to delay must be under O. VII R. 6. |
| Application to sue as an indigent person | O. XXXIII R. 1 | Substitutes for payment of court fee at institution. |
| Application under s. 12A, Commercial Courts Act, 2015 | Pre-institution mediation | Mandatory for commercial suits **not** contemplating urgent interim relief. Non-compliance is a ground for rejection under Order VII Rule 11. |

::: warning The s. 12A trap in commercial suits
In a commercial dispute of a Specified Value, a plaint that does not contemplate urgent interim relief and is filed without exhausting pre-institution mediation under Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 is liable to be rejected. The urgency must be genuine and apparent from the pleading — an interim application filed only to bypass s. 12A invites the objection rather than answering it.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**T. Arivandandam v. T.V. Satyapal**, (1977) 4 SCC 467 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* A litigant filed a succession of vexatious proceedings over the same property; the plaint was drafted so as to appear to raise a triable claim.

**Ratio decidendi:** Where a plaint is manifestly vexatious and meritless and does not disclose a clear right to sue, the trial judge must exercise his power under Order VII Rule 11 and reject it. The court must read the plaint **meaningfully, not formally**; if "clever drafting has created the illusion of a cause of action", the plaint must be nipped in the bud at the first hearing.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The court is required to read the plaint meaningfully, and not merely to count whether the necessary words appear in it. Where clever drafting has created an illusion of a cause of action, the plaint must be rejected."
:::

---

**Saleem Bhai v. State of Maharashtra**, (2003) 1 SCC 557 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* The trial court directed the defendants to file a written statement before deciding a pending Order VII Rule 11 application.

**Ratio decidendi:** For the purposes of Order VII Rule 11 the averments **in the plaint alone** are germane; the pleas taken by the defendant in the written statement are wholly irrelevant. The direction to file a written statement before deciding a Rule 11 application is not warranted, and the trial court may exercise the power under Rule 11 **at any stage of the suit**, including before the written statement is filed and before issues are framed.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A Rule 11 application is decided on the plaint alone and can be taken up at any stage of the suit; the defendant need not first file his written statement, and if he has filed one, it is to be disregarded."
:::

---

**Popat and Kotecha Property v. State Bank of India Staff Association**, (2005) 7 SCC 510 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* A plaint was attacked as time-barred on its own averments.

**Ratio decidendi:** Rule 11(d) applies only where the bar is apparent **from the statement in the plaint**; it does not permit the court to travel beyond the plaint or to determine a mixed question of fact and law. Further, the plaint cannot be rejected in part — Rule 11 does not contemplate rejection of a portion of the plaint or rejection against one of several defendants. Where limitation is a mixed question of fact and law, it must be tried as an issue and not disposed of under Rule 11.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Rule 11(d) is confined to a bar visible on the face of the plaint. A limitation plea which requires evidence is a triable issue under Order XIV, not a ground of rejection."
:::

---

**Church of Christ Charitable Trust and Educational Charitable Society v. Ponniamman Educational Trust**, (2012) 8 SCC 706 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* A plaint alleging an agreement to sell was challenged as disclosing no cause of action.

**Ratio decidendi:** The court reaffirmed the two-part discipline: (i) in deciding an application under Rule 11(a), only the averments in the plaint are to be looked at; and (ii) the plaint must disclose the **material facts** giving rise to the cause of action, and mere pleading of a bundle of facts which does not, if proved, entitle the plaintiff to relief will not suffice. A clever device to camouflage the absence of a cause of action must be seen through.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A plaint discloses a cause of action only if, taking every averment as proved, the court could pass the decree prayed for. Anything less is a Rule 11(a) rejection."
:::

---

**Dahiben v. Arvindbhai Kalyanji Bhanusali**, (2020) 7 SCC 366 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* A suit filed some five and a half years after a registered sale deed sought its cancellation on the ground that the full sale consideration had not been paid.

**Ratio decidendi:** Order VII Rule 11 is an independent and special remedy enabling the court to summarily terminate a suit which is a **dead letter** without proceeding to trial. The word "shall" makes rejection mandatory once a ground is made out. The court must ascertain whether, on a meaningful reading of the plaint and the documents filed with it, the suit is manifestly vexatious and without merit. Non-payment of a part of the sale consideration does not by itself invalidate a completed sale, and the suit was in any event barred by limitation on its own averments.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Order VII Rule 11 exists to terminate at the threshold a suit which, on its own showing, must fail — so that a defendant is not dragged through a trial that can have only one outcome."
:::

---

**Srihari Hanumandas Totala v. Hemant Vithal Kamat**, (2021) 9 SCC 99 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* A plaint was sought to be rejected under Rule 11(d) on the ground that the suit was barred by res judicata.

**Ratio decidendi:** A plea of **res judicata cannot ordinarily be the basis for rejection under Order VII Rule 11(d)**, because it necessarily requires consideration of the pleadings, issues and decision in the previous suit — that is, material beyond the plaint. Res judicata is a mixed question of fact and law and must be decided as an issue after the parties have filed their pleadings and adduced evidence, save in the rarest case where the entire foundation is apparent on the plaint itself.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Res judicata is a mixed question of fact and law requiring examination of the record of the earlier suit; it is therefore a matter for a preliminary issue under Order XIV Rule 2, not for rejection under Order VII Rule 11(d)."
:::

---

**Sopan Sukhdeo Sable v. Assistant Charity Commissioner**, (2004) 3 SCC 137 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* The scope of the enquiry under Order VII Rule 11 was in issue.

**Ratio decidendi:** In considering Rule 11 the court must look only at the plaint; the question is whether a "clear right to sue" is shown. The averments in the plaint as a whole are to be seen, and a mere assertion or a stray sentence cannot be picked out. If on a meaningful — as opposed to a formal — reading the plaint is manifestly vexatious and meritless, the power must be exercised.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The plaint is read as a whole. Neither the defendant's best paragraph nor the plaintiff's worst sentence decides a Rule 11 application."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Every citation, bench strength and current status above must be confirmed against the official report and a current citator before the judgment is placed before a court. Several of these authorities have been considered in subsequent decisions on the interaction between Rule 11 and specific statutory bars.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**The plaint-drafting checklist — the twelve things that get a plaint rejected or returned**

| # | Requirement | Provision | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Correct court named, with pecuniary and territorial jurisdiction pleaded | R. 1(a), (f); ss. 15–20 | Return under R. 10 |
| 2 | Full description and address of every party; minority or unsoundness of mind stated | R. 1(b)–(d) | Objection at numbering; O. XXXII defects |
| 3 | Material facts constituting the cause of action, in chronological paragraphs | R. 1(e); O. VI R. 2 | Rejection under R. 11(a) |
| 4 | **The date the cause of action arose**, stated expressly | R. 1(e) | Rejection under R. 11(d) on limitation |
| 5 | Ground of exemption from limitation, if the suit is prima facie late | R. 6 | Rejection under R. 11(d) |
| 6 | Description of immovable property by boundaries and survey/settlement numbers | R. 3 | Decree unexecutable for want of identification |
| 7 | Precise amount claimed in a money suit; approximation only where permitted | R. 2 | Objection on court fee |
| 8 | Valuation for jurisdiction **and** for court fee, separately stated | R. 1(i) | Rejection under R. 11(b)/(c) after opportunity |
| 9 | Reliefs stated specifically, with alternatives; consequential relief not omitted | R. 7; s. 34 proviso, Specific Relief Act | Rejection under R. 11(d) |
| 10 | Set-off allowed or claim relinquished, stated | R. 1(h) | O. II R. 2 consequences |
| 11 | List of documents, with the documents produced | R. 14; O. XIII R. 1 | Inadmissibility at trial without leave |

**Advocate's toolkit — for the defendant, running a Rule 11 application**

::: danger The five ways a Rule 11 application is lost
1. **Relying on the written statement.** The moment your argument requires the judge to look at your defence, you have lost. Confine yourself to the plaint and the documents filed with it.
2. **Asking for partial rejection.** "Reject the plaint as against Defendant No. 3" is not available. Use Order I Rule 10(2) instead.
3. **Raising res judicata or Order II Rule 2 under R. 11(d).** Both normally require the earlier record. Take them as a preliminary issue under Order XIV Rule 2.
4. **Seeking rejection for undervaluation without a prior order.** Rules 11(b) and (c) require the court first to fix a time for correction. Ask for that order, then move for rejection on failure.
5. **Filing too late to matter.** Rule 11 can be invoked at any stage, but a rejection in year seven merely produces a fresh plaint. File it at the first hearing, before the plaintiff has a limitation-safe opportunity to re-present.
:::

**Advocate's toolkit — for the plaintiff, resisting a Rule 11 application**

| Line of defence | How it is run |
|---|---|
| The bar is not apparent on the plaint | Show that establishing it requires evidence, a document not filed, or a finding of fact. Then the point belongs to Order XIV Rule 2, not Rule 11. |
| The plaint must be read as a whole | Resist the extraction of a single sentence. Read the narrative paragraphs together and identify the complete bundle of facts. |
| Amend | File an Order VI Rule 17 application at once. Rule 11 operates on the plaint as it stands; change the plaint. Press for the amendment to be heard first. |
| Limitation is a mixed question | Where acknowledgment, part payment, fraud or continuing wrong is in play, limitation is triable, not summary. |
| Even if rejected, Rule 13 saves a fresh plaint | Useful only if time remains. Check the residual limitation before conceding anything. |

**Strategic timing**

| Step | Timing |
|---|---|
| Rule 11 application (defendant) | **First hearing**, before or with the written statement. Delay costs the application its practical value. |
| Rule 17 amendment (plaintiff, in response) | Immediately on service of the Rule 11 application, and in any event before trial commences. |
| Objection to court fee/valuation | Before issues; the court must fix a time for correction, and that time must be allowed to run. |
| Application for leave to file documents not filed with the plaint | Before the plaintiff's evidence, under R. 14(3). Do not wait until the document is tendered in the witness box. |

**Model prayer — application under Order VII Rule 11**

> It is therefore most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to:
>
> **(a)** Reject the plaint in Suit No. ____ of 20__ under Order VII Rule 11(a) and (d) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, on the ground that the plaint discloses no cause of action and that the suit appears from the statement in the plaint to be barred by [the Limitation Act, 1963 / Section ____ of ____];
>
> **(b)** Pending the hearing and final disposal of this application, stay all further proceedings in the suit, including the framing of issues;
>
> **(c)** Award the costs of this application to the Applicant;
>
> **(d)** Pass such other and further orders as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit and proper in the interests of justice.

::: tip The one thing to remember about Rule 11
Rejection is a **decree** under Section 2(2). That is why it is the most valuable interlocutory application in the Code — and why the court will not grant it on anything less than a bar visible on the four corners of the plaint.

## Order VIII — Written Statement, Set-off and Counter-claim

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **Rule 1. Written statement.** — The defendant shall, within thirty days from the date of service of summons on him, present a written statement of his defence:
>
> *Provided that where the defendant fails to file the written statement within the said period of thirty days, he shall be allowed to file the same on such other day, as may be specified by the Court, for reasons to be recorded in writing and on payment of such costs as the Court deems fit, but which shall not be later than one hundred twenty days from the date of service of summons and on expiry of one hundred twenty days from the date of service of summons, the defendant shall forfeit the right to file the written statement and the Court shall not allow the written statement to be taken on record.*

> **Rule 1A. Duty of defendant to produce documents upon which relief is claimed or relied upon by him.** — (1) Where the defendant bases his defence upon a document or relies upon any document in his possession or power, in support of his defence or claim for set-off or counter-claim, he shall enter such document in a list, and shall produce it in Court when the written statement is presented…

> **Rule 2. New facts must be specially pleaded.** — The defendant must raise by his pleading all matters which show the suit not to be maintainable, or that the transaction is either void or voidable in point of law, and all such grounds of defence as, if not raised, would be likely to take the opposite party by surprise, or would raise issues of fact not arising out of the plaint, as, for instance, fraud, limitation, release, payment, performance, or facts showing illegality.

> **Rule 3. Denial to be specific.** — It shall not be sufficient for a defendant in his written statement to deny generally the grounds alleged by the plaintiff, but the defendant must deal specifically with each allegation of fact of which he does not admit the truth, except damages.

> **Rule 3A. Denial by the defendant in suits before the Commercial Division of High Court or Commercial Court.** — (1) Denial shall be in the manner provided in sub-rules (2), (3), (4) and (5) of this Rule. (2) The defendant in his written statement shall state which of the allegations in the particulars of plaint he denies, which allegations he is unable to admit or deny, but which he requires the plaintiff to prove, and which allegations he admits…

> **Rule 4. Evasive denial.** — Where a defendant denies an allegation of fact in the plaint, he must not do so evasively, but answer the point of substance. Thus, if it is alleged that he received a certain sum of money, it shall not be sufficient to deny that he received that particular amount, but he must deny that he received that sum or any part thereof, or else set out how much he received. And if an allegation is made with diverse circumstances, it shall not be sufficient to deny it along with those circumstances.

> **Rule 5. Specific denial.** — (1) Every allegation of fact in the plaint, if not denied specifically or by necessary implication, or stated to be not admitted in the pleading of the defendant, shall be taken to be admitted except as against a person under disability:
>
> *Provided that the Court may in its discretion require any fact so admitted to be proved otherwise than by such admission.*
>
> (2) Where the defendant has not filed a pleading, it shall be lawful for the Court to pronounce judgment on the basis of the facts contained in the plaint, except as against a person under a disability, but the Court may, in its discretion, require any such fact to be proved…

> **Rule 6. Particulars of set-off to be given in written statement.** — (1) Where in a suit for the recovery of money the defendant claims to set-off against the plaintiff's demand any ascertained sum of money legally recoverable by him from the plaintiff, not exceeding the pecuniary limits of the jurisdiction of the Court, and both parties fill the same character as they fill in the plaintiff's suit, the defendant may, at the first hearing of the suit, but not afterwards unless permitted by the Court, present a written statement containing the particulars of the debt sought to be set-off.
> (2) **Effect of set-off.** — The written statement shall have the same effect as a plaint in a cross-suit so as to enable the Court to pronounce a final judgment in respect both of the original claim and of the set-off…

> **Rule 6A. Counter-claim by defendant.** — (1) A defendant in a suit may, in addition to his right of pleading a set-off under rule 6, set up, by way of counter-claim against the claim of the plaintiff, any right or claim in respect of a cause of action accruing to the defendant against the plaintiff either before or after the filing of the suit but before the defendant has delivered his defence or before the time limited for delivering his defence has expired, whether such counter-claim is in the nature of a claim for damages or not:
>
> *Provided that such counter-claim shall not exceed the pecuniary limits of the jurisdiction of the Court.*
>
> (2) Such counter-claim shall have the effect of a cross-suit so as to enable the Court to pronounce a final judgment in the same suit, both on the original claim and on the counter-claim.
> (3) The plaintiff shall be at liberty to file a written statement in answer to the counter-claim of the defendant within such period as may be fixed by the Court.
> (4) The counter-claim shall be treated as a plaint and governed by the rules applicable to plaints.

> **Rule 6B.** — Where any defendant seeks to rely upon any ground as supporting a right of counter-claim, he shall, in his written statement, state specifically that he does so by way of counter-claim.
>
> **Rule 6C. Exclusion of counter-claim.** — Where a defendant sets up a counter-claim and the plaintiff contends that the claim thereby raised ought not to be disposed of by way of counter-claim but in an independent suit, the plaintiff may, at any time before issues are settled in relation to the counter-claim, apply to the Court for an order that such counter-claim may be excluded…
>
> **Rule 6D.** — If in any case in which the defendant sets up a counter-claim, the suit of the plaintiff is stayed, discontinued or dismissed, the counter-claim may nevertheless be proceeded with.
>
> **Rule 6E.** — If the plaintiff makes default in putting in a reply to the counter-claim, the Court may pronounce judgment against the plaintiff in relation to the counter-claim or make such order in relation to the counter-claim as it thinks fit.
>
> **Rule 6F. Relief to defendant where counter-claim succeeds.** — Where in any suit a set-off or counter-claim is established as a defence against the plaintiff's claim, and any balance is found due to the plaintiff or the defendant, as the case may be, the Court may give judgment to the party entitled to such balance.
>
> **Rule 6G.** — The rules relating to a written statement by a defendant shall apply to a written statement filed in answer to a counter-claim.

> **Rule 9. Subsequent pleadings.** — No pleading subsequent to the written statement of a defendant other than by way of defence to set-off or counter-claim shall be presented except by the leave of the Court and upon such terms as the Court thinks fit; but the Court may at any time require a written statement or additional written statement from any of the parties and fix a time of not more than thirty days for presenting the same.

> **Rule 10. Procedure when party fails to present written statement called for by Court.** — Where any party from whom a written statement is required under rule 1 or rule 9 fails to present the same within the time permitted or fixed by the Court, as the case may be, the Court shall pronounce judgment against him, or make such order in relation to the suit as it thinks fit and on the pronouncement of such judgment a decree shall be drawn up.

**Statutory mechanics**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| R. 1: "**shall**, within thirty days" | Mandatory in terms; but the main period is treated as directory in ordinary suits | Thirty days is the norm; the court may extend for recorded reasons and on costs. |
| R. 1 proviso: "**shall forfeit the right**… and the Court **shall not allow**" | Mandatory and, in commercial suits, **absolute** | For **commercial suits** governed by the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, the 120-day outer limit is a hard bar: the right is forfeited and the court has no power to take the written statement on record. In ordinary civil suits the outer limit has been read as directory, the court retaining a residual power in exceptional cases. **This distinction is the single most important thing on this page.** |
| R. 2: "**must** raise by his pleading all matters" | Mandatory | Limitation, fraud, release, payment, performance, illegality — each is an affirmative defence that is lost if not pleaded. It cannot be introduced in argument or in cross-examination. |
| R. 3: "must deal **specifically** with each allegation" | Mandatory | A general denial is not a denial at all. |
| R. 4: "must not do so **evasively**, but answer the point of substance" | Mandatory | Denying the precise figure while remaining silent on receipt of any sum is an evasive denial and is treated as no denial. |
| R. 5(1): "**shall be taken to be admitted**" | Deeming provision, with a discretionary escape | Facts not specifically denied, not denied by necessary implication, and not stated to be not admitted, are **deemed admitted** — except against a person under disability. The proviso lets the court still require proof. |
| R. 5(2): "it shall be **lawful** for the Court to pronounce judgment" | Enabling, discretionary | Even on total default the court is not bound to decree; it *may* require proof. In practice a court will insist on proof where title, status or a serious claim is involved. |
| R. 6: "**ascertained** sum of money legally recoverable… both parties fill the same character" | Four cumulative conditions | Legal set-off requires: (i) a money suit; (ii) an ascertained sum; (iii) legally recoverable, and within the court's pecuniary limits; (iv) the same character of the parties. |
| R. 6A: "any right or claim… **whether such counter-claim is in the nature of a claim for damages or not**" | Wide and enabling | A counter-claim need not be for an ascertained sum, need not arise from the same transaction, and may be for any relief. It is far wider than set-off. |
| R. 6A(1): cause of action accruing "**before the defendant has delivered his defence**" | Temporal limit | The counter-claim's cause of action must have accrued before the defence was delivered, or before the time for it expired. |
| R. 6A(4): "**treated as a plaint**" | Deeming | Court fee, limitation, verification, particulars and Order VII Rule 11 all apply to a counter-claim as to a plaint. |
| R. 6D: "may **nevertheless** be proceeded with" | Saving | Dismissal or withdrawal of the suit does not kill the counter-claim. This is what makes a counter-claim safer than a set-off. |
| R. 9: "**No** pleading subsequent… except by the leave of the Court" | Prohibitory | A replication or rejoinder requires leave. A reply to a counter-claim does not. |
| R. 10: "the Court **shall** pronounce judgment against him" | Mandatory in form; read with discretion | Read with R. 5(2), the court retains the power to require proof; but a defendant who does not file a written statement after being required to do so risks a decree. |

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — the time limit, and the two regimes.**

This is the most heavily litigated procedural question in the Order, and the answer differs according to the kind of suit.

| | **Ordinary civil suit** | **Commercial suit** (Specified Value, Commercial Courts Act, 2015) |
|---|---|---|
| Normal period | 30 days from service of summons | 30 days from service |
| Extension | Court may extend for reasons recorded and on costs | Court may extend for reasons recorded and on costs |
| Outer limit | 120 days | 120 days |
| Is the outer limit absolute? | **No** — read as directory. The court retains a residual power in exceptional circumstances, though it is to be used sparingly and never as a matter of routine. | **Yes** — the right to file is **forfeited** and the court "shall not allow the written statement to be taken on record". There is no residual discretion. |
| Source of the difference | Rule 1 as it stands in the body of the Code | The amended Order VIII Rule 1 in the Schedule to the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, read with the express forfeiture language |

The practical rule: **in a commercial suit, day 120 is the end of the world.** Diarise the date of service, not the date of the summons; count 120 days; and treat that date as immovable.

**Sub-module B — the architecture of denial (Rules 3, 4 and 5).**

The Code builds a ratchet in three steps:

1. **Rule 3 — denial must be specific.** Deal with each allegation of fact separately. "The contents of paragraphs 5 to 14 are denied" is not compliance.
2. **Rule 4 — denial must not be evasive.** Answer the point of substance. If the plaint says "the defendant received ₹10,00,000", it is not enough to deny receipt of ₹10,00,000 — the defendant must deny receipt of that sum *or any part of it*, or state what he did receive.
3. **Rule 5 — what is not denied is admitted.** Any allegation not specifically denied, not denied by necessary implication, and not stated to be "not admitted" is **deemed admitted**.

The three permissible responses to any pleaded fact are therefore: **admit**, **deny specifically**, or **state that it is not admitted and put the plaintiff to proof**. There is no fourth option, and silence is the worst of all — it is an admission.

::: warning The deemed admission is the defendant's commonest self-inflicted wound
A well-drafted written statement is a paragraph-by-paragraph mirror of the plaint. Every paragraph of the plaint must be met by a corresponding paragraph of the written statement. When paragraph 11 of the plaint has no counterpart in the written statement, paragraph 11 has been admitted, and no amount of evidence will unsay it.
:::

**Sub-module C — Rule 3A: the commercial-suit standard.**

For commercial suits the Schedule inserts Rule 3A, which requires the defendant to classify every allegation into one of three baskets — **denied**, **not admitted (plaintiff put to proof)**, or **admitted** — and, crucially, requires that where an allegation is denied, the defendant must **state his reasons** for doing so and, if he intends to put forward a different version of events, state his own version. This is a materially higher standard than Rule 3 and closes off the bare traverse.

**Sub-module D — Rule 2: affirmative defences that are lost if unpleaded.**

Rule 2 lists, by way of example, fraud, limitation, release, payment, performance and illegality. The list is illustrative, not exhaustive. The unifying principle is that any defence which introduces facts not arising out of the plaint, or which would take the plaintiff by surprise, must be pleaded.

Common affirmative defences that must be expressly pleaded:

- Limitation (though a court is bound to dismiss a time-barred suit under s. 3 of the Limitation Act even if not pleaded, the *facts* founding the plea must be on record)
- Res judicata under s. 11 CPC, with particulars of the earlier suit
- The bar under Order II Rule 2 — **and the earlier plaint must be produced**
- Estoppel, waiver, acquiescence
- Payment, accord and satisfaction, release
- Want of jurisdiction, including a pecuniary or territorial objection under s. 21 (which must be taken at the earliest possible opportunity and before settlement of issues)
- Non-joinder or misjoinder of parties (Order I Rule 13 — must be taken at the earliest possible opportunity)
- Illegality of the contract, s. 23 of the Contract Act, 1872

**Sub-module E — set-off: legal and equitable.**

| | **Legal set-off** (R. 6) | **Equitable set-off** (not in the Code; a rule of equity applied by courts) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of the sum | Must be an **ascertained** sum of money | May be an **unascertained** sum, including damages |
| Relation to the plaintiff's claim | Need **not** arise out of the same transaction | **Must** arise out of the same transaction, or transactions so connected that it would be inequitable to drive the defendant to a separate suit |
| Same character of parties | Required | Required |
| Pecuniary limit | Must not exceed the court's pecuniary jurisdiction | Same |
| Court fee | Payable as on a plaint | Payable as on a plaint |
| Limitation | Must be within time on the date of the **suit** (the written statement is treated as a plaint in a cross-suit) | Same |
| Claimed as of right? | Yes, if the conditions are met | No — it is discretionary and equitable |

**Sub-module F — counter-claim, and why it beats set-off.**

Rule 6A is materially wider than Rule 6 on every axis:

| Axis | Set-off (R. 6) | Counter-claim (R. 6A) |
|---|---|---|
| Type of suit it can be raised in | Only a suit for **recovery of money** | **Any** suit |
| Nature of the claim | Ascertained sum of money only | Any right or claim, including damages, specific performance, injunction, possession |
| Same transaction required? | No (legal set-off) | No |
| Can it exceed the plaintiff's claim? | It operates only as a defence up to the plaintiff's claim; the balance requires R. 6F | Yes — expressly, subject only to the court's pecuniary limits |
| Survives dismissal of the suit? | No | **Yes** — Rule 6D |
| Treated as a plaint? | Has the effect of a plaint in a cross-suit (R. 6(2)) | Expressly treated as a plaint (R. 6A(4)) |

**When must a counter-claim be filed?** The cause of action must have accrued before the defence was delivered or before the time for it expired. As to the *filing* of the counter-claim itself, the settled practical rule is that it should be filed with the written statement, and in any event **before issues are framed**; a counter-claim sought to be introduced after issues, and certainly after trial has substantially progressed, will ordinarily be refused, because the defendant then has the alternative of an independent suit.

**Sub-module G — Rule 6C: the plaintiff's escape hatch.**

A plaintiff faced with a sprawling counter-claim that will delay his own suit may apply under Rule 6C for an order excluding the counter-claim, so that it must be pursued as an independent suit. The application must be made **before issues are settled in relation to the counter-claim**. This is a short and easily missed window.

**Sub-module H — Rule 9: replication and additional written statement.**

There is no right of reply to a written statement. A replication or rejoinder requires the **leave of the court** under Rule 9. Two exceptions and one power:

- No leave is needed for a written statement in **answer to a set-off or counter-claim** — that is a matter of right, and Rule 6A(3) contemplates the court fixing the period.
- The court may **of its own motion** require an additional written statement from any party, fixing a period not exceeding thirty days.
- A replication cannot be used to introduce a new cause of action or to alter the plaint. That requires an amendment under Order VI Rule 17.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — the 121st day in a commercial suit.** P, a supplier, files a commercial suit for ₹3.4 crore. Summons is served on D on 1 February. D's counsel seeks and obtains two extensions, and tenders the written statement on 5 June — 124 days after service.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** Object at the threshold. Under the proviso to Order VIII Rule 1 as it applies to commercial suits, D has **forfeited** the right to file, and the court "shall not allow the written statement to be taken on record". This is not a matter of discretion or of costs; there is no power to condone. P should press for the suit to proceed under Order VIII Rule 10, and for judgment on the plaint's averments.
- **Shield (defendant):** The only genuine arguments are (i) that service was not effective on 1 February — attack the date of service itself, since the 120 days runs from **service**, not from filing or from the summons; or (ii) that the suit is not in fact a commercial dispute of a Specified Value, so the ordinary regime and its residual discretion apply. Both are factual and must be raised immediately.

::: danger Diarise the date of service
In a commercial suit, calculate the 120-day date on the day service is effected and put it in the file. Everything else in Order VIII is recoverable; this is not.
:::

**Illustration 2 — the deemed admission.** P's plaint pleads in paragraph 7 that D executed a promissory note dated 4 May 2022 for ₹22,00,000, and in paragraph 8 that D received the sum in cash. D's written statement traverses paragraphs 1 to 6 and 9 to 15, but is silent on paragraphs 7 and 8.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** Apply under **Order XII Rule 6** for judgment on admissions. Paragraphs 7 and 8 stand admitted under Order VIII Rule 5(1). Execution and receipt being admitted, the plaintiff's case is complete on the pleadings.
- **Shield (defendant):** Two responses, in this order. First, apply immediately under **Order VI Rule 17** to amend the written statement to introduce a specific denial — amendments to a written statement are treated liberally, and adding a defence deprives the plaintiff of no accrued right. Second, invoke the **proviso to Rule 5(1)**, which permits the court in its discretion to require a fact so admitted to be proved otherwise than by the admission — particularly apt where the transaction alleged is a large cash payment.

**Illustration 3 — the evasive denial.** P pleads that D received ₹10,00,000 on 3 March. D's written statement says: "It is denied that the Defendant received a sum of ₹10,00,000 on 3 March as alleged."

- **Sword (plaintiff):** This is precisely the mischief Rule 4 addresses. D has denied the exact figure and the exact date, and said nothing about receiving any sum at all. Under Rule 4 the denial is evasive and D must be treated as not having denied receipt; the substance of the allegation — that money passed — stands admitted.
- **Shield (defendant):** The written statement must be redrafted to say: "It is denied that the Defendant received a sum of ₹10,00,000, or any part thereof, on 3 March or at all," or, if a lesser sum was received, "The Defendant received ₹2,50,000 on 3 March, and no more."

**Illustration 4 — legal set-off in a rent suit.** P sues D, his tenant, for arrears of rent of ₹6,00,000. D claims that P owes him ₹4,20,000 under a decree obtained in an earlier suit between the same parties in the same capacities.

- **Sword (defendant):** A textbook **legal set-off** under Rule 6. The suit is for recovery of money; the sum is ascertained (a decretal amount); it is legally recoverable and within the court's pecuniary limits; and both parties fill the same character. D must plead the particulars of the debt in the written statement, at the first hearing, and pay court fee as on a plaint.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** Attack the conditions. Is the sum genuinely ascertained? Is the decree still executable, or has it become time-barred under Article 136 of the Limitation Act? Do the parties fill the same character — is the decree in D's individual capacity while the tenancy is held by a firm?

**Illustration 5 — equitable set-off.** P, a contractor, sues D for ₹80,00,000 as the unpaid balance under a works contract. D says the work was defective and that he has spent ₹35,00,000 rectifying it, and claims to set this off.

- **Sword (defendant):** The sum is unascertained, so legal set-off under Rule 6 is unavailable. But the claim arises out of the very same contract, and it would be inequitable to compel D to pay in full and sue separately. This is the paradigm **equitable set-off**.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** Equitable set-off is discretionary, not a right. P argues that the rectification cost is a disputed damages claim requiring its own evidence, that allowing it will convert a straightforward money suit into a construction trial, and that the proper course is a counter-claim under Rule 6A on which court fee is paid and issues are framed.

**Illustration 6 — counter-claim in a suit for injunction.** P sues for a permanent injunction restraining D from entering a plot. D, in his written statement, sets up a counter-claim for a declaration of title and for possession, and for mesne profits.

- **Sword (defendant):** Rule 6A permits exactly this. The counter-claim need not be for money, need not arise from the same transaction, and may exceed the plaintiff's claim in value. Its great advantage is **Rule 6D**: if P, sensing defeat, withdraws the suit or allows it to be dismissed for default, the counter-claim survives and proceeds to judgment. D obtains an adjudication of title without filing a separate suit.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** Apply under **Rule 6C**, before issues are settled on the counter-claim, for an order excluding it, on the ground that a title suit ought not to be tried within an injunction suit. Also examine (i) whether court fee has been paid on the counter-claim as on a plaint — it must be, under Rule 6A(4); (ii) whether the counter-claim's cause of action accrued before the defence was delivered; and (iii) whether it is within limitation as on the date of filing.

**Illustration 7 — the counter-claim filed too late.** In a suit filed in 2021, issues were framed in 2023 and P's evidence concluded in 2025. D then applies to file a counter-claim for damages.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** Resist. Although Rule 6A fixes the temporal limit by reference to the accrual of the cause of action, the settled practice is that a counter-claim will not be entertained after issues have been framed, and certainly not after the plaintiff's evidence is closed. D has a complete alternative remedy by way of an independent suit; permitting the counter-claim now would derail a trial that is nearly over.
- **Shield (defendant):** The only realistic answer is that the cause of action accrued and was discovered late, and that limitation on an independent suit has expired — but that second point, being the reason the application is being made, tends to defeat rather than assist it.

### 4. Procedural Interlocking Matrix

| Order VIII provision | Interlocks with | What the connection does |
|---|---|---|
| R. 1 (30 days / 120 days) | O. V R. 1 (summons); Commercial Courts Act, 2015, Schedule | Time runs from **service of summons**, not from institution or from the date of the summons. In commercial suits the 120-day limit is absolute. |
| R. 1A (documents with the written statement) | O. VII R. 14 (plaintiff's mirror obligation); O. XIII R. 1 | A document not filed with the written statement is not receivable in evidence without leave. |
| R. 2 (new facts specially pleaded) | O. XIV R. 1 (issues); s. 3 Limitation Act | An unpleaded defence generates no issue. Limitation is the partial exception — s. 3 obliges the court to dismiss a time-barred suit — but the facts must still be on record. |
| RR. 3, 4, 5 (denial) | **O. XII R. 6** — judgment on admissions | A deemed admission under R. 5(1) is an admission on which the plaintiff can move for judgment without trial. This is the sharpest consequence in the Order. |
| R. 3A (commercial suits) | Commercial Courts Act, 2015 | Requires denial with reasons and a positive counter-version. A bare traverse is non-compliant. |
| R. 5(2), R. 10 (default) | O. IX R. 6 (ex parte); s. 2(2) | Judgment on default under R. 10 results in a decree. Distinguish an ex parte decree under Order IX (non-appearance) from a R. 10 judgment (appearance, but no written statement) — the setting-aside routes differ. |
| R. 6 (set-off) | S. 15 CPC (pecuniary jurisdiction); Court Fees Act | Set-off must be within the court's pecuniary limits and bears court fee as on a plaint. |
| R. 6 (limitation on set-off) | Limitation Act, 1963 | The set-off must have been within time on the **date of institution of the suit**, the written statement being treated as a plaint in a cross-suit. |
| R. 6A (counter-claim) | O. VII R. 11; O. VI R. 15; Court Fees Act | Treated as a plaint: it can itself be attacked under Order VII Rule 11, must be verified, and must bear ad valorem court fee. |
| R. 6A (limitation on counter-claim) | Limitation Act, 1963 | Limitation for a counter-claim is computed as on the **date the counter-claim is filed** — not the date of the suit. Contrast set-off. |
| R. 6C (exclusion) | O. XIV R. 1 (settlement of issues) | The application must be made **before issues are settled on the counter-claim**. The window closes early. |
| R. 6D (survival) | O. XXIII R. 1 (withdrawal); O. IX R. 8 (dismissal for default) | Withdrawal or dismissal of the plaintiff's suit does not abate the counter-claim. |
| R. 6E (default on reply) | R. 6A(3) | Failure by the plaintiff to answer the counter-claim exposes him to judgment on it. |
| R. 6F (balance) | S. 2(2) | The court may decree the net balance in favour of whichever party it is due to — a single decree, not two. |
| R. 9 (subsequent pleadings) | O. VI R. 17 | A replication cannot enlarge the plaint. New facts require amendment, not a rejoinder. |

**Set-off and counter-claim compared on limitation and fee**

| | **Set-off (R. 6)** | **Counter-claim (R. 6A)** |
|---|---|---|
| Relevant date for limitation | Date of institution of the **plaintiff's suit** | Date of **filing of the counter-claim** |
| Court fee | As on a plaint, on the amount set off | As on a plaint, on the value of the counter-claim |
| If the suit is dismissed or withdrawn | Falls with the suit | **Survives** (R. 6D) |
| Can it be attacked under O. VII R. 11? | Not directly | **Yes** — it is treated as a plaint |
| Appeal | Part of the decree in the suit | Part of the same decree; separately appealable in substance |

**Applications that arise around the written statement**

| Application | Provision | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Application for extension of time to file the written statement | O. VIII R. 1 proviso | Must state reasons; costs are usually imposed. Useless past 120 days in a commercial suit. |
| Application for judgment on admissions | O. XII R. 6 | Deployed against deemed admissions under R. 5(1). Can be made at any stage. |
| Application under R. 6C | O. VIII R. 6C | To exclude a counter-claim and relegate it to an independent suit. |
| Application for leave to file a replication | O. VIII R. 9 | Required; a rejoinder filed without leave is liable to be taken off the record. |
| Application to amend the written statement | O. VI R. 17 | The standard cure for a deemed admission or an unpleaded defence. Treated liberally. |
| Application under O. VII R. 11 against a counter-claim | O. VII R. 11 read with O. VIII R. 6A(4) | The plaintiff's answer to a counter-claim that discloses no cause of action or is time-barred on its face. |

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Kailash v. Nanhku**, (2005) 4 SCC 480 — Supreme Court of India, three-Judge Bench.

*Facts:* A written statement in an election petition was filed beyond the ninety-day period then prescribed by the proviso to Order VIII Rule 1.

**Ratio decidendi:** The provision fixing the outer limit for filing a written statement in an **ordinary civil suit** is **procedural and directory**, not mandatory. The use of "shall" is not decisive; a procedural law is the handmaid of justice, and the court retains the power to extend time in exceptional cases, for reasons to be recorded, and ordinarily on costs. The extension is not to be granted routinely — it is an exception, to be used where the defendant demonstrates a genuine impediment.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"In an ordinary civil suit the outer limit under Order VIII Rule 1 is directory; the court retains a residual discretion to extend time in an exceptional case, for reasons recorded and on costs. That discretion is the exception, not the rule."
:::

---

**SCG Contracts (India) (P) Ltd. v. K.S. Chamankar Infrastructure (P) Ltd.**, (2019) 12 SCC 210 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* A written statement in a commercial suit was tendered beyond 120 days from service of summons; the defendant relied on the earlier line of authority treating the period as directory.

**Ratio decidendi:** The proviso to Order VIII Rule 1 as amended by the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 is **mandatory and absolute** in its application to commercial suits. The words "shall forfeit the right to file the written statement" and "the Court shall not allow the written statement to be taken on record" leave no room for discretion. The line of cases treating the period as directory has no application to commercial suits.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"In a commercial suit, on the expiry of 120 days from the date of service of summons the defendant forfeits the right to file a written statement and the court has no power to take it on record. There is no discretion to condone."
:::

---

**Badat and Co. v. East India Trading Co.**, AIR 1964 SC 538 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* The distinction between legal and equitable set-off arose in a suit on foreign awards.

**Ratio decidendi:** A **legal set-off** under Order VIII Rule 6 requires an ascertained sum legally recoverable, in the same character, within the pecuniary jurisdiction; it need not arise out of the same transaction and is claimable as of right. An **equitable set-off** is not governed by Rule 6 at all; it is allowed where the cross-demands arise out of the same transaction or are so connected in their nature and circumstances that it would be inequitable to drive the defendant to a separate suit, and it may be for an unascertained sum. Equitable set-off is discretionary.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Where the cross-demand is unascertained, the defendant must found it in equity by showing that it arises out of the same transaction; Rule 6 will not carry an unliquidated claim."
:::

---

**Ramesh Chand Ardawatiya v. Anil Panjwani**, (2003) 7 SCC 350 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* A counter-claim was sought to be raised after the stage of the written statement had passed.

**Ratio decidendi:** A counter-claim may be raised in the written statement, or by way of an amendment incorporated with the leave of the court, or in a subsequent pleading filed under Order VIII Rule 9 with leave. But the right to file a counter-claim is **not available after issues have been framed and the trial has begun**; thereafter the defendant must be relegated to an independent suit. A counter-claim cannot be inferred from a mere denial or from stray averments in the written statement — Rule 6B requires it to be stated **specifically** to be a counter-claim.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A counter-claim must be pleaded expressly as such under Rule 6B, and must be filed before issues are framed; after that stage the defendant's remedy is an independent suit."
:::

---

**Ashok Kumar Kalra v. Wing Cdr. Surendra Agnihotri**, (2020) 2 SCC 394 — Supreme Court of India, three-Judge Bench.

*Facts:* The question referred was whether a counter-claim can be filed after the written statement has been filed.

**Ratio decidendi:** Order VIII Rule 6A does not put an embargo on filing a counter-claim after the written statement; the embargo is only on the **accrual of the cause of action**, which must be before the defence is delivered or before the time for delivering it expires. However, the court's discretion to permit a belated counter-claim is to be exercised having regard to the stage of the proceedings, and the **outer limit** for entertaining a counter-claim is the **framing of issues**. Relevant factors include the nature of the claim, whether it would delay the trial, and whether the defendant has an efficacious alternative remedy.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A counter-claim may be filed after the written statement, but not after issues are framed; and the cause of action must have accrued before the defence was delivered."
:::

---

**Rajni Rani v. Khairati Lal**, (2015) 2 SCC 682 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* The court considered the exercise of discretion in permitting a written statement beyond the prescribed period in an ordinary suit.

**Ratio decidendi:** The residual discretion recognised in *Kailash* is to be exercised sparingly and on cogent material; the defendant must show a real and sufficient explanation for the delay. Routine extensions defeat the object of the 1999 and 2002 amendments, which was to compress the pre-trial stage.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The discretion to permit a late written statement in an ordinary suit is not to be exercised as a matter of course; the defendant must place cogent material explaining the entire period of delay."
:::

---

**Modula India v. Kamakshya Singh Deo**, (1988) 4 SCC 619 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* A defendant whose defence had been struck out sought to participate in the trial.

**Ratio decidendi:** Even where a defendant has not filed a written statement or his defence has been struck out, he is not wholly excluded from the proceedings: he may **cross-examine the plaintiff's witnesses** and address argument, though he cannot lead evidence in support of a defence he has not pleaded. The plaintiff must still prove his case; the absence of a written statement does not relieve him of that burden where the court, in its discretion under Rule 5(2), requires proof.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A defendant without a written statement may still cross-examine and argue; he cannot lead affirmative evidence, but the plaintiff is not thereby excused from proving his case."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Citations, bench strength and current status must be confirmed against the official report and a current citator. The interaction between *Kailash* and *SCG Contracts* — directory in ordinary suits, mandatory in commercial suits — has been the subject of continuing consideration, and the position under any applicable State amendment or High Court Original Side Rules must be checked separately.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**The written statement drafting protocol**

::: tip Draft the written statement as a mirror, then as a case
A competent written statement has two halves, and both are indispensable.

**Part A — Preliminary objections.** Every objection that could dispose of the suit, taken at the threshold: jurisdiction (pecuniary, territorial, subject-matter), limitation, res judicata with particulars of the earlier suit, Order II Rule 2 with the earlier plaint annexed, non-joinder of a necessary party, want of statutory notice under s. 80, non-compliance with s. 12A of the Commercial Courts Act, cause of action.

**Part B — Parawise reply.** One paragraph of the written statement for each paragraph of the plaint, in the same numbering. No gaps. No "paragraphs 6 to 12 are denied".

**Part C — Additional pleas.** The defendant's own version of events, pleaded affirmatively, with dates.

**Part D — Set-off or counter-claim**, if any, expressly labelled as such under Rule 6B.

**Part E — Verification** under Order VI Rule 15 and the affidavit or statement of truth.
:::

**The failure modes**

| Trap | Consequence | Cure |
|---|---|---|
| Missing the 120-day limit in a commercial suit | Right forfeited; no discretion to condone; suit proceeds under R. 10 | None after the event. The only cure is the diary entry made on the day of service. |
| Gap in the parawise reply | Deemed admission under R. 5(1); exposure to Order XII Rule 6 | Amend under Order VI Rule 17 at once, and invoke the proviso to R. 5(1). |
| Bare or omnibus denial | Treated as no denial (R. 3) | Deal with each allegation separately. |
| Evasive denial of a figure | The substance is treated as admitted (R. 4) | "…or any part thereof, or at all", or state the actual amount received. |
| Affirmative defence not pleaded | No issue framed; evidence inadmissible on it | R. 2 — plead limitation, payment, release, fraud, illegality, estoppel expressly. |
| Counter-claim not labelled as such | Treated as a mere defence; no decree can be passed on it | R. 6B — say in terms "the Defendant sets up the following claim **by way of counter-claim**". |
| Counter-claim without court fee | Liable to be rejected as a plaint would be | R. 6A(4) — pay ad valorem fee on the value of the counter-claim. |
| Counter-claim after issues | Refused; relegated to an independent suit | File it with the written statement. |
| Replication filed without leave | Taken off the record | Apply under R. 9. |
| Documents not filed with the written statement | Inadmissible without leave (R. 1A) | File the list and the documents with the written statement. |

**Strategic timing — the defendant's calendar**

| Day | Step |
|---|---|
| Day 0 | Service of summons. **Calculate and diarise Day 30 and Day 120.** |
| By Day 30 | File the written statement, with the list of documents (R. 1A), and any set-off or counter-claim. |
| With the written statement | Take every preliminary objection. A pecuniary or territorial objection under s. 21 and a non-joinder objection under Order I Rule 13 must be taken **at the earliest possible opportunity and before settlement of issues**, or they are lost. |
| Before issues | Last window for a counter-claim; last window for the plaintiff's Rule 6C application. |
| Any time | Order VII Rule 11 against the plaint; Order XII Rule 6 against admissions. |

**Model prayer — counter-claim**

> The Defendant therefore prays that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to:
>
> **(a)** Dismiss the suit of the Plaintiff with costs;
>
> **(b)** Decree the counter-claim of the Defendant and declare that the Defendant is the absolute owner of the suit property more particularly described in Schedule A hereto;
>
> **(c)** Direct the Plaintiff to deliver vacant and peaceful possession of the suit property to the Defendant;
>
> **(d)** Direct an enquiry into mesne profits under Order XX Rule 12 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 from the date of the counter-claim until delivery of possession, and decree the amount so found due;
>
> **(e)** Award the costs of the suit and of the counter-claim to the Defendant;
>
> **(f)** Pass such other and further orders as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit in the interests of justice.

**Model prayer — application under Order VIII Rule 6C**

> It is therefore most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to order that the counter-claim raised by the Defendant in paragraphs ____ to ____ of the written statement be excluded from this suit and be left to be disposed of, if at all, by way of an independent suit, and to proceed with the trial of the Plaintiff's claim; and to pass such other orders as may be just.

::: danger The three sentences a defendant cannot afford to get wrong
1. **"Service was effected on ____; the 120-day period expires on ____."** In a commercial suit this sentence decides the case.
2. **"Paragraph ____ of the plaint is denied."** Say it about every paragraph, or the paragraph is admitted.
3. **"The Defendant sets up the following claim by way of counter-claim."** Without those words there is no counter-claim, only a defence.
:::

## Chapter consolidation — the life of a pleading

**The pleading timeline, end to end**

| Stage | Provision | What must happen | What is lost if it does not |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drafting the plaint | O. VI RR. 1–6, 15; O. VII RR. 1–7 | Material facts, date of accrual, jurisdiction, valuation, specific reliefs, verification, s. 26(2) affidavit | Rejection under O. VII R. 11 |
| Filing the plaint | O. VII R. 14; O. XIII R. 1 | List of documents; documents produced | Inadmissibility without leave |
| Threshold attack | O. VII R. 11 | Reject on the plaint alone; rejection is a **decree** (s. 2(2)) | The defendant's best single application |
| Wrong forum | O. VII R. 10, 10A | Return for presentation to the proper court; appealable under O. XLIII R. 1(a) | Fresh institution; time not saved |
| Written statement | O. VIII R. 1 | 30 days; outer limit 120 days from **service** | In a commercial suit, the right is **forfeited** |
| Denials | O. VIII RR. 3, 4, 5, 3A | Parawise, specific, non-evasive | Deemed admission; O. XII R. 6 judgment |
| Affirmative defences | O. VIII R. 2 | Limitation, payment, release, fraud, illegality, estoppel pleaded expressly | No issue framed; evidence inadmissible |
| Cross-claim | O. VIII RR. 6, 6A–6G | Set-off or counter-claim, expressly labelled, court fee paid | No decree on the defendant's claim |
| Replication | O. VIII R. 9 | Leave of the court, except in answer to set-off or counter-claim | Taken off the record |
| Amendment | O. VI R. 17 | Before trial: liberal. After trial commences: due diligence must be proved | Refusal; no appeal, only s. 115 / Art. 227 |
| Carrying out the amendment | O. VI R. 18 | Within the time fixed, else within 14 days | Leave lapses |
| Issues | O. XIV R. 1 | Issues arise only from pleaded material propositions | An unpleaded fact yields no finding |

**The three rejection-versus-return-versus-striking-out distinctions**

| | **Striking out** (O. VI R. 16) | **Return** (O. VII R. 10) | **Rejection** (O. VII R. 11) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Target | Offending paragraphs | The whole plaint, for want of jurisdiction | The whole plaint, on a R. 11 ground |
| Nature of order | Interlocutory order | Order, appealable under O. XLIII R. 1(a) | **Decree** under s. 2(2), appealable under s. 96 |
| Suit continues? | Yes, shorn of the offending matter | In the proper court, on fresh presentation | No |
| Fresh suit? | N/A | Same plaint, re-presented | Fresh plaint competent under R. 13, subject to limitation |

**Set-off against counter-claim, in one table**

| | **Set-off** | **Counter-claim** |
|---|---|---|
| Available in | Money suits only | Any suit |
| Claim | Ascertained sum (legal); same-transaction unascertained sum (equitable) | Any right or claim |
| Exceeds the plaintiff's claim? | Operates as a defence; balance under R. 6F | Yes, subject to pecuniary jurisdiction |
| Survives dismissal of the suit? | No | **Yes** (R. 6D) |
| Limitation computed as on | Date of the suit | Date of filing the counter-claim |
| Attackable under O. VII R. 11? | No | **Yes** (R. 6A(4)) |

::: tip The four sentences worth memorising from this chapter
1. **"A plaint is rejected on a demurrer."** Read the plaint, assume it true, ignore the defence, and ask whether a decree could follow. That is the whole of Order VII Rule 11.
2. **"Rejection is a decree."** Section 2(2) makes Order VII Rule 11 the most valuable interlocutory application in the Code — and explains why courts insist on a bar visible on the four corners of the plaint.
3. **"What is not denied is admitted."** Order VIII Rule 5(1). One missing paragraph in a written statement can decide a suit under Order XII Rule 6.
4. **"After trial commences, diligence comes before merits."** Under the proviso to Order VI Rule 17 the court does not reach the merits of an amendment until due diligence is established.
:::

::: warning Before you file
Order VI Rule 15A and Order VIII Rule 3A apply only to commercial suits; the 120-day forfeiture under Order VIII Rule 1 is absolute only in commercial suits. Before applying anything in this chapter, identify whether your suit is a commercial dispute of a Specified Value, and check the applicable State amendment and the High Court Rules on the Original Side. The procedural regime, not merely the forum, changes with the answer.
:::
