# Orders IX, X & XI — Appearance, Examination of Parties and Discovery

These three Orders govern the stretch between service of summons and the framing of issues — the phase in which a suit either acquires shape or quietly collapses. Order IX is about attendance: who turns up, what happens when nobody does, and how a decree passed in a party's absence can be undone. Order X is the court's own inquisitorial power to pin the parties down to what is genuinely in dispute, and since 2002 it also carries the mandatory reference to alternative dispute resolution. Order XI is the most under-used part of the Code — discovery, interrogatories and inspection, the machinery that narrows a case before a witness is ever called.

| Order | Rules | One-line function |
|---|---|---|
| **IX** | RR. 1–5 | Attendance on the day fixed; dismissal where the plaintiff's default has defeated service |
| **IX** | RR. 6–7 | Where only the plaintiff appears — the suit may proceed *ex parte*; the defendant's route back in before decree |
| **IX** | RR. 8–9 | Where only the defendant appears — dismissal for default, and the bar on a fresh suit |
| **IX** | RR. 10–12 | Non-attendance of one of several plaintiffs or defendants; failure of a party ordered to appear in person |
| **IX** | RR. 13–14 | **Setting aside an *ex parte* decree** — the single most litigated rule in the Order |
| **X** | RR. 1, 2–4 | Ascertainment of admissions and denials; oral examination of parties by the court |
| **X** | RR. 1A–1C | Mandatory direction to opt for a section 89 mode of settlement, and the return to court on failure |
| **XI** | RR. 1–11, 22 | Interrogatories — leave, form, objections, answer on affidavit, use at trial |
| **XI** | RR. 12–20 | Discovery and inspection of documents; notice to produce; premature discovery |
| **XI** | R. 21 | **Non-compliance** — suit dismissed for want of prosecution, or defence struck out |

::: info The architecture in one breath
Order IX is built on a single asymmetry: when the **plaintiff** defaults, the suit is dismissed and Rule 9 bars a fresh one, so his only road back is an application to set aside; when the **defendant** defaults, the suit proceeds *ex parte* and he has not one but two remedies — Rule 13 in the trial court, and a first appeal under section 96(2). Order X is the only place in the Code where the judge, not the parties, drives the narrowing of the dispute, and Rule 1A converts that moment into a compulsory pause for settlement. Order XI supplies the teeth: a party who will not answer interrogatories or produce documents can be struck out under Rule 21 and left to face judgment as though he had never defended.
:::

## Order IX — appearance and the consequences of non-appearance

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **Rule 1. Parties to appear on day fixed in summons for defendant to appear and answer.** — On the day fixed in the summons for the defendant to appear and answer, the parties shall be in attendance at the Court-house in person or by their respective pleaders, and the suit shall then be heard unless the hearing is adjourned to a future day fixed by the Court.

> **Rule 2. Dismissal of suit where summons not served in consequence of plaintiff's failure to pay costs.** — Where on the day so fixed it is found that the summons has not been served upon the defendant in consequence of the failure of the plaintiff to pay the court-fee or postal charges, if any, chargeable for such service, or to present copies of the plaint or concise statements, as required by rule 9 of Order VII, the Court may make an order that the suit be dismissed:
>
> *Provided that no such order shall be made, if, notwithstanding such failure, the defendant attends in person or by agent when he is allowed to appear by agent.*

> **Rule 3. Where neither party appears, suit to be dismissed.** — Where neither party appears when the suit is called on for hearing, the Court may make an order that the suit be dismissed.

> **Rule 4. Plaintiff may bring fresh suit or Court may restore suit to file.** — Where a suit is dismissed under rule 2 or rule 3, the plaintiff may (subject to the law of limitation) bring a fresh suit; or he may apply for an order to set the dismissal aside, and if he satisfies the Court that there was sufficient cause for such failure as is referred to in rule 2, or for his non-appearance, as the case may be, the Court shall make an order setting aside the dismissal and shall appoint a day for proceeding with the suit.

> **Rule 5. Dismissal of suit where plaintiff, after summons returned unserved, fails for one month to apply for fresh summons.** — (1) Where after a summons has been issued to the defendant, or to one of several defendants, and returned unserved, the plaintiff fails, for a period of seven days from the date of the return made to the Court by the officer ordinarily certifying to the Court returns made by the serving officers, to apply for the issue of a fresh summons the Court shall make an order that the suit be dismissed as against such defendant, unless the plaintiff has within the said period satisfied the Court that —
> **(a)** he has failed after using his best endeavours to discover the residence of the defendant, who has not been served, or
> **(b)** such defendant is avoiding service of process, or
> **(c)** there is any other sufficient cause for extending the time,
>
> in which case the Court may extend the time for making such application for such period as it thinks fit.
>
> (2) In such case the plaintiff may (subject to the law of limitation) bring a fresh suit.

> **Rule 6. Procedure when only plaintiff appears.** — (1) Where the plaintiff appears and the defendant does not appear when the suit is called on for hearing, then —
> **(a)** *When summons duly served.* — If it is proved that the summons was duly served, the Court may make an order that the suit be heard ex parte;
> **(b)** *When summons not duly served.* — If it is not proved that the summons was duly served, the Court shall direct a second summons to be issued and served on the defendant;
> **(c)** *When summons served but not in due time.* — If it is proved that the summons was served on the defendant, but not in sufficient time to enable him to appear and answer on the day fixed in the summons, the Court shall postpone the hearing of the suit to a future day to be fixed by the Court, and shall direct notice of such day to be given to the defendant.

> **Rule 7. Procedure where defendant appears on day of adjourned hearing and assigns good cause for previous non-appearance.** — Where the Court has adjourned the hearing of the suit ex parte, and the defendant, at or before such hearing, appears and assigns good cause for his previous non-appearance, he may, upon such terms as the Court directs as to costs or otherwise, be heard in answer to the suit as if he had appeared on the day fixed for his appearance.

> **Rule 8. Procedure where defendant only appears.** — Where the defendant appears and the plaintiff does not appear when the suit is called on for hearing, the Court shall make an order that the suit be dismissed, unless the defendant admits the claim, or part thereof, in which case the Court shall pass a decree against the defendant upon such admission, and, where part only of the claim has been admitted, shall dismiss the suit so far as it relates to the remainder.

> **Rule 9. Decree against plaintiff by default bars fresh suit.** — (1) Where a suit is wholly or partly dismissed under rule 8, the plaintiff shall be precluded from bringing a fresh suit in respect of the same cause of action. But he may apply for an order to set the dismissal aside, and if he satisfies the Court that there was sufficient cause for his non-appearance when the suit was called on for hearing, the Court shall make an order setting aside the dismissal upon such terms as to costs or otherwise as it thinks fit, and shall appoint a day for proceeding with the suit.
>
> (2) No order shall be made under this rule unless notice of the application has been served on the opposite party.

> **Rule 13. Setting aside decree ex parte against defendant.** — In any case in which a decree is passed ex parte against a defendant, he may apply to the Court by which the decree was passed for an order to set it aside; and if he satisfies the Court that the summons was not duly served, or that he was prevented by any sufficient cause from appearing when the suit was called on for hearing, the Court shall make an order setting aside the decree as against him upon such terms as to costs, payment into Court or otherwise as it thinks fit, and shall appoint a day for proceeding with the suit:
>
> *Provided that where the decree is of such a nature that it cannot be set aside as against such defendant only it may be set aside as against all or any of the other defendants also:*
>
> *Provided further that no Court shall set aside a decree passed ex parte merely on the ground that there has been an irregularity in the service of summons, if it is satisfied that the defendant had notice of the date of hearing and had sufficient time to appear and answer the plaintiff's claim.*
>
> **Explanation.** — Where there has been an appeal against a decree passed ex parte under this rule, and the appeal has been disposed of on any ground other than the ground that the appellant has withdrawn the appeal, no application shall lie under this rule for setting aside that ex parte decree.

> **Rule 14. No decree to be set aside without notice to opposite party.** — No decree shall be set aside on any such application as aforesaid unless notice thereof has been served on the opposite party.

**Statutory mechanics**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| R. 2, R. 3: "the Court **may** make an order that the suit be dismissed" | Discretionary | Dismissal for non-service or for neither party appearing is not automatic. The court may adjourn instead, and an order of dismissal passed mechanically on the first default is open to attack. |
| R. 5: "the Court **shall** make an order that the suit be dismissed" | Mandatory, subject to escape clauses | The only mandatory dismissal in the Order — but clauses (a) to (c) give the plaintiff three separate routes to an extension, and the seven days run from the *return*, not from the date of issue. |
| R. 6(1)(a): "**if it is proved** that the summons was duly served" | **Condition precedent** | Proof of due service is jurisdictional to an *ex parte* order. Without it the court must go to clause (b) and issue a second summons. An *ex parte* decree passed without this finding is liable to be set aside as of course. |
| R. 8: "the Court **shall** make an order that the suit be dismissed" | Mandatory | Where the defendant appears and the plaintiff does not, the court has no discretion to adjourn in the plaintiff's favour — subject only to the admission carve-out. |
| R. 9(1): "the plaintiff **shall be precluded** from bringing a fresh suit" | **Statutory bar** | A dismissal under Rule 8 is not a dismissal simpliciter; it destroys the cause of action for the purposes of a fresh suit. Contrast Rules 2 and 3, where Rule 4 expressly preserves the fresh suit. |
| R. 13: "**if he satisfies the Court**" | Condition precedent; burden on the applicant | Two alternative gateways: summons not duly served, **or** sufficient cause for non-appearance. Either suffices; the applicant need not establish both. |
| R. 13: "the Court **shall** make an order setting aside the decree" | Mandatory on satisfaction | Once either gateway is crossed, setting aside is not discretionary. The discretion survives only as to *terms* — costs, payment into court. |
| R. 13, second proviso: "**merely** on the ground that there has been an irregularity in the service of summons" | Limiting proviso | Irregularity is not enough where the defendant in fact had notice and time. Distinguish *irregular* service (curable) from *no* service at all (fatal — the first gateway remains open). |
| R. 13, Explanation | **Bar on election** | Once an appeal against the *ex parte* decree has been disposed of on any ground other than withdrawal, Rule 13 is closed. The defendant must choose his sequence with care. |
| R. 14: "**No** decree shall be set aside … unless notice" | Mandatory, negative form | Setting aside without notice to the decree-holder is a nullity, not an irregularity. |

::: warning The distinction Rule 4 and Rule 9 turn on
Dismissal under Rules 2 or 3 → **fresh suit permitted** (Rule 4), subject only to limitation.
Dismissal under Rule 8 → **fresh suit barred** (Rule 9), and restoration is the only route.
The difference is that in Rule 8 the defendant has actually appeared and been put to the trouble of attending; the Code protects him from being vexed a second time.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**(a) The two kinds of absence.** The Order deals separately with the party who never engages at all and the party who engages and then fails to attend on a particular day. Rules 2, 3, 5 and 6 belong to the first phase — the summons stage. Rules 7, 8 and 13 belong to the second — the hearing stage. The remedies differ because the prejudice differs.

**(b) "Ex parte" is a mode of proceeding, not a species of decree.** When the court orders under Rule 6(1)(a) that the suit be heard *ex parte*, it is not giving judgment for the plaintiff. The plaintiff must still prove his case; the court must still be satisfied on evidence. An *ex parte* decree is a decree on the merits, reached without the defendant's participation. This matters practically: the plaintiff who leads no evidence and simply asks for a decree because the defendant is absent should not get one.

**(c) Rule 7 versus Rule 13 — the timing distinction.** Rule 7 operates *before* decree. The defendant who turns up at an adjourned hearing, shows good cause for his earlier absence and is let back in is heard "as if he had appeared on the day fixed" — but he cannot reopen what has already been done in his absence. Rule 13 operates *after* decree, and if it succeeds the decree goes and the suit is restored. A defendant who appears mid-trial should be applying under Rule 7; one who appears after judgment must be under Rule 13.

**(d) The defendant's two doors after an *ex parte* decree.** He may apply under Rule 13 to the court that passed the decree, or he may appeal under section 96(2). The two are concurrent, and they test different things: Rule 13 asks whether he was properly served or had sufficient cause for absence; the appeal asks whether the decree is right on the merits and the material on record. The Explanation to Rule 13 forecloses the Rule 13 route once the appeal has been disposed of — but not the other way round.

**(e) "Sufficient cause" is judged at the moment of absence.** The enquiry is directed to why the defendant did not appear on the day the suit was called on and the decree followed. A history of laxity earlier in the proceedings is not, by itself, an answer to a good explanation for the operative date, though it bears on the terms the court imposes.

**(f) Service and knowledge are not the same thing.** The second proviso to Rule 13 addresses the case where service was technically defective but the defendant nonetheless had notice of the hearing date and time to respond. That defendant cannot succeed on the irregularity. But a defendant who was never served at all — a false report, service on a stranger, a wrong address — falls within the first gateway, and no amount of subsequent knowledge cures it.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — the substituted-service *ex parte* decree.** A money suit is filed against a defendant who has moved house. The process server reports refusal; the court orders substituted service by publication in a newspaper the defendant has never read; the suit proceeds *ex parte* and is decreed. The defendant learns of it when his bank account is attached in execution eight months later.

- *As a shield (defendant):* Apply under Rule 13 pleading that the summons was not duly served — the first gateway, on which sufficient cause need not be shown at all. Attach the process server's report and demonstrate that the address used was not the address in the plaintiff's own correspondence. On limitation, invoke Article 123 of the Limitation Act: where the summons was not duly served, time runs from **knowledge of the decree**, not its date. Depose to the date of knowledge with precision — the attachment order is the natural peg.
- *As a sword (plaintiff/decree-holder):* Resist on the second proviso. Establish that the defendant had actual notice: a reply notice sent from the new address, an appearance in a connected proceeding, a WhatsApp acknowledgement. Push the court to record a finding on the date of knowledge, because that finding governs limitation and is the ground on which the application can be thrown out without reaching the merits.

**Illustration 2 — the partition suit dismissed in the plaintiff's absence.** In a partition suit, the plaintiff's counsel is engaged before another court on the date of final hearing. The defendant is present. The suit is dismissed under Rule 8.

- *As a shield (plaintiff):* A fresh suit is barred by Rule 9(1). The only route is restoration. Apply within thirty days (Article 122), supported by an affidavit of counsel explaining the clash, and serve the application — Rule 9(2) makes notice mandatory, and an order made without it is a nullity. Offer costs; courts restore readily where the default is counsel's and the party is blameless.
- *As a sword (defendant):* Oppose on the ground that the absence was part of a pattern of delay and that the explanation is bare. Where restoration is nonetheless likely, do not waste the opportunity — press for real costs and for a peremptory direction that the plaintiff close evidence on the restored date.

**Illustration 3 — the injunction defendant who elects the wrong remedy.** An *ex parte* decree of permanent injunction is passed. The defendant, advised to be thorough, files a first appeal under section 96(2) **and** an application under Rule 13. The appeal is heard first and dismissed on merits.

- *Consequence:* The Explanation to Rule 13 now bars the application, and it must be dismissed as not maintainable. The defendant has lost the cheaper and more effective remedy — restoration of the suit and a trial — by pursuing the appeal to disposal first.
- *The correct sequence:* File the Rule 13 application first and have it decided. If it fails, the first appeal remains available, and in that appeal the correctness of the order refusing to set aside cannot be reagitated, but the merits of the decree can.

### 4. Procedural Interlocking Matrix

| Trigger | Provision | Application / relief | Limitation | Appeal or revision |

| Suit dismissed for non-service caused by plaintiff's default | O. IX R. 2 | Application under O. IX R. 4 to set aside, **or** fresh suit | 30 days (Art. 122, Limitation Act) | Order rejecting the application — appealable, O. XLIII R. 1(c) |
| Neither party appears | O. IX R. 3 | Application under O. IX R. 4 | 30 days (Art. 122) | O. XLIII R. 1(c) |

| Defendant absent; summons duly served | O. IX R. 6(1)(a) | Court proceeds *ex parte* | — | — |
| Defendant appears before decree, at adjourned hearing | O. IX R. 7 | Application to be let in on good cause | No prescribed article; promptness governs | Refusal is not separately appealable; ground in the appeal from the decree |
| Suit dismissed for plaintiff's absence, defendant present | O. IX R. 8 | Restoration under O. IX R. 9 — **fresh suit barred** | 30 days (Art. 122) | Order rejecting — O. XLIII R. 1(c) |
| *Ex parte* decree passed | O. IX R. 13 | Application to set aside | 30 days from decree, or from knowledge where summons not duly served (Art. 123) | Order rejecting — O. XLIII R. 1(d) |
| *Ex parte* decree passed | s. 96(2) | First appeal on the merits | 30 days to District Court / 90 days to High Court (Arts. 116, 117) | Second appeal, s. 100, on a substantial question of law |
| Setting aside sought without notice | O. IX R. 14 | Bar — order made without notice is a nullity | — | Attackable in execution and in appeal |

**Court fee and stamp.** An application under Rules 4, 9 or 13 bears the fixed court fee prescribed by the applicable State Court Fees Act for a miscellaneous application; it is not valued on the subject-matter of the suit. A first appeal under section 96(2), by contrast, bears **ad valorem** fee on the decretal amount or the value of the relief. This asymmetry is a real factor in choosing the remedy where the decree is large.

**Interlocking with Order XLIII.** Both refusals — Rule 9 and Rule 13 — carry an appeal under Order XLIII Rule 1, clauses (c) and (d) respectively. Note the asymmetry: an order **setting aside** a dismissal or an *ex parte* decree is **not** appealable. The aggrieved decree-holder's remedy is Article 227, and the threshold there is jurisdictional error, not error of appreciation.

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Arjun Singh v. Mohindra Kumar**, AIR 1964 SC 993 — **Bench: 3 Judges**
*Facts:* The defendant was absent on a date to which the hearing had been adjourned *ex parte*, and the question was whether his application fell under Rule 7 or Rule 13, and whether an earlier refusal operated as res judicata.

**Ratio decidendi:** Rule 7 and Rule 13 occupy different fields. Rule 7 applies where the suit is still pending and the defendant seeks to be relieved of the consequences of his earlier absence for the future; it does not entitle him to set aside anything already done. Rule 13 applies only once a decree has been passed. An order under Rule 7 does not attract the principle of res judicata in the strict sense, but the court's earlier finding on the same absence binds it at the later stage.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"My application is under Rule 7 and not Rule 13, because no decree has yet been passed; on the authority of *Arjun Singh*, all I seek is to be heard from this date forward, and I do not ask this Court to reopen the evidence already recorded."
:::

**G.P. Srivastava v. R.K. Raizada**, (2000) 3 SCC 54 — **Bench: 2 Judges**
*Facts:* An *ex parte* decree was set aside by the trial court on the defendant's explanation; the High Court reversed, relying on the defendant's earlier dilatory conduct.

**Ratio decidendi:** Under Rule 13 the expression "sufficient cause" must receive a liberal construction so as to advance substantial justice. The enquiry is confined to the cause for non-appearance **on the date when the suit was called on for hearing and the decree passed**. Prior negligence or delay in the conduct of the suit is not, by itself, a ground to refuse relief, though it may justify the imposition of costs.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The conduct my learned friend complains of relates to earlier dates. *G.P. Srivastava* confines the enquiry under Rule 13 to the cause for absence on the date the decree was passed, and on that date the cause shown is unrebutted."
:::

**Rani Choudhury v. Suraj Jit Choudhury**, (1982) 2 SCC 596 — **Bench: 2 Judges**
*Facts:* A defendant against whom an *ex parte* decree had been passed pursued an appeal, and after its disposal applied under Rule 13.

**Ratio decidendi:** Where an appeal against an *ex parte* decree has been preferred and disposed of on merits, the decree of the trial court merges in the appellate decree, and an application under Order IX Rule 13 to set aside the trial court's decree no longer lies. This decision is the source of the Explanation later inserted into Rule 13.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Having carried the *ex parte* decree in appeal to disposal, the defendant is met by the Explanation to Rule 13, which on the principle of *Rani Choudhury* closes this forum to him altogether."
:::

**Bhanu Kumar Jain v. Archana Kumar**, (2005) 1 SCC 787 — **Bench: 2 Judges**
*Facts:* An application under Rule 13 was dismissed; the defendant then pursued a first appeal under section 96(2) and sought in it to reagitate the question of service.

**Ratio decidendi:** A defendant suffering an *ex parte* decree has two distinct and concurrent remedies — Rule 13 and an appeal under section 96(2) — and the pendency of one does not bar the other. But where the Rule 13 application has been dismissed and that dismissal has become final, the defendant cannot in the first appeal reopen the correctness of the order refusing to set aside; that question is concluded. He remains entitled to challenge the decree on the merits on the material on record.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The appellant is not shut out of this appeal by the dismissal of his Rule 13 application; *Bhanu Kumar Jain* preserves his right to assail the decree on the merits, and confines the bar to the question of service alone."
:::

**Parimal v. Veena alias Bharti**, (2011) 3 SCC 545 — **Bench: 2 Judges**
*Facts:* A matrimonial decree was passed *ex parte* after the summons was returned with an endorsement of refusal; the defendant pleaded non-service.

**Ratio decidendi:** "Sufficient cause" means a cause beyond the control of the party, and the applicant must show that he was not negligent and did not act with want of *bona fides*. A refusal to accept service, duly endorsed, amounts to due service. The second proviso to Rule 13 bars relief where the defendant in fact had notice of the date of hearing and sufficient time to appear, notwithstanding an irregularity in the mode of service.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The endorsement of refusal on the summons is, on *Parimal*, due service; and the defendant having had notice and time, the second proviso to Rule 13 bars him from relying on any irregularity in the manner of service."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
State amendments to Order IX are numerous, and several High Courts have altered the periods in Rules 5 and 13 and the consequences of default. The Limitation Act articles cited above (Articles 122 and 123) should be checked against the current bare Act, and the status of each judgment confirmed, before any of this is put to a court.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

::: danger Five ways an application under Rule 13 fails
1. **Filed out of time with no application under section 5 of the Limitation Act.** Thirty days from the decree is short. If you are relying on knowledge rather than the decree date, plead the date of knowledge as a fact, with the document that establishes it — and file a section 5 application in the alternative regardless.
2. **Pleading "sufficient cause" in the abstract.** "The defendant could not appear due to unavoidable circumstances" is not a cause. Name the illness, produce the certificate, name the counsel and the court he was detained in, exhibit the travel record.
3. **Conflating irregular service with no service.** If you plead only that service was irregular, the second proviso answers you. If the truth is that you were never served, plead *that* — and plead it first, because it is a self-contained gateway requiring no proof of sufficient cause.
4. **Pursuing the appeal to disposal first.** The Explanation then bars the application. Where both remedies are open, have the Rule 13 application decided first.
5. **Omitting notice under Rule 14.** An order of setting aside made without notice to the decree-holder is a nullity and will be undone on his application, usually after you have already lost the benefit of the passage of time.
:::

::: warning Strategic timing
- **Rule 7 before decree, Rule 13 after.** The moment of decree is the dividing line, and an application under the wrong rule invites dismissal on that ground alone. If you appear midway through an *ex parte* trial, move under Rule 7 the same day.
- **Do not let the restoration application go stale.** Where a suit has been dismissed under Rule 8, every day of delay strengthens the defendant's plea of acquiescence, and Rule 9 leaves no fresh suit to fall back on.
- **Watch the seven days in Rule 5.** They run from the *return* of the unserved summons, which is a date on the record you may never see unless you inspect. Diarise inspection of the process register.
- **Where the decree is large, weigh the court fee.** A Rule 13 application costs a fixed fee; a first appeal under section 96(2) costs *ad valorem*. For a substantial money decree this difference alone can decide the sequence.
:::

**Model prayer — application under Order IX Rule 13**

> It is therefore most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to —
> **(a)** set aside the *ex parte* decree dated ______ passed in Suit No. ______ of ______ as against the applicant/defendant;
> **(b)** restore the said suit to its original number and file, and permit the applicant to file his written statement and contest the suit on merits;
> **(c)** stay the execution of the said decree in Execution Petition No. ______ pending disposal of this application;
> **(d)** pass such further or other order as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit in the facts and circumstances of the case.

**Checklist before filing**

| # | Item | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Date of decree and date of knowledge, both pleaded | Governs which limb of Article 123 applies |
| 2 | Section 5 application in the alternative | Costless insurance; its absence is fatal if the date of knowledge is disbelieved |
| 3 | Certified copy of the decree and judgment | Required to be filed with the application |
| 4 | Specific denial of due service, with the process server's report | The first gateway requires no proof of sufficient cause |
| 5 | Affidavit particularising the cause for absence | The second gateway |
| 6 | Notice to the decree-holder | Mandatory under Rule 14 |
| 7 | Separate application for stay of execution | The application itself does not stay the decree |
| 8 | Draft written statement ready | Courts frequently restore on the condition that it be filed the same day |

## Order X — examination of parties by the Court and the section 89 reference

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **Rule 1. Ascertainment whether allegations in pleadings are admitted or denied.** — At the first hearing of the suit the Court shall ascertain from each party or his pleader whether he admits or denies such allegations of fact as are made in the plaint or written statement (if any) of the opposite party, and as are not expressly or by necessary implication admitted or denied by the party against whom they are made. The Court shall record such admissions and denials.

> **Rule 1A. Direction of the Court to opt for any one mode of alternative dispute resolution.** — After recording the admissions and denials, the Court shall direct the parties to the suit to opt either mode of the settlement outside the Court as specified in sub-section (1) of section 89. On the option of the parties, the Court shall fix the date of appearance before such forum or authority as may be opted by the parties.

> **Rule 1B. Appearance before the conciliatory forum or authority.** — Where a suit is referred under rule 1A, the parties shall appear before such forum or authority for conciliation of the suit.

> **Rule 1C. Appearance before the Court consequent to the failure of efforts of conciliation.** — Where a suit is referred under rule 1A and the presiding officer of conciliation forum or authority is satisfied that it would not be proper in the interest of justice to proceed with the matter further, then, it shall refer the matter again to the Court and direct the parties to appear before the Court on the date fixed by it.

> **Rule 2. Oral examination of party, or companion of party.** — (1) At the first hearing of the suit, the Court —
>
> *(a)* shall, with a view to elucidating matters in controversy in the suit, examine orally such of the parties to the suit appearing in person or present in Court, as it deems fit; and
>
> *(b)* may orally examine any person, able to answer any material question relating to the suit, by whom any party appearing in person or present in Court or his pleader is accompanied.
>
> (2) At any subsequent hearing, the Court may orally examine any party appearing in person or present in Court, or any person, able to answer any material question relating to the suit, by whom such party or his pleader is accompanied.
>
> (3) The Court may, if it thinks fit, put in the course of an examination under this rule questions suggested by either party.

> **Rule 3. Substance of examination to be written.** — The substance of the examination shall be reduced to writing by the Judge, and shall form part of the record.

> **Rule 4. Consequence of refusal or inability of pleader to answer.** — (1) Where the pleader of any party who appears by a pleader or any such person accompanying a pleader as is referred to in rule 2, refuses or is unable to answer any material question relating to the suit which the Court is of opinion that the party whom he represents ought to answer, and is likely to be able to answer if interrogated in person, the Court may postpone the hearing of the suit to a future day and direct that such party shall appear in person on such day.
>
> (2) If such party fails without lawful excuse to appear in person on the day so appointed, the Court may pronounce judgment against him, or make such order in relation to the suit as it thinks fit.

#### Statutory mechanics

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| R. 1 — "the Court **shall** ascertain" | Mandatory duty on the judge | The narrowing exercise is judicial, not adversarial; it cannot be waived by consent |
| R. 1 — "**shall record** such admissions and denials" | Mandatory | An unrecorded admission cannot later ground a Order XII Rule 6 judgment |
| R. 1 — "as are **not** expressly or by necessary implication admitted or denied" | Limiting clause | Rule 1 targets only the gaps left by the pleadings, not the whole case |
| R. 1A — "the Court **shall** direct the parties to opt" | Mandatory direction | Failure to make the reference is an irregularity; it does not by itself vitiate the decree |
| R. 1A — "**On the option of the parties**" | Condition precedent to fixing a date | The Court directs the *choice*; the parties make it (except arbitration and conciliation, which require consent) |
| R. 1C — "**it shall refer the matter again to the Court**" | Mandatory return | The suit is never dead while it sits before the ADR forum; it is only paused |
| R. 2(1)(a) — "**shall** … examine orally" | Mandatory | The court is bound to attempt elucidation where a party is personally present |
| R. 2(1)(b) and 2(2) — "**may** orally examine" | Discretionary | Examination of the accompanying person, and all examination at later hearings, is at the judge's option |
| R. 2(1)(a) — "**with a view to elucidating matters in controversy**" | Purpose limitation | The power is to clarify pleadings, not to take evidence or extract a confession |
| R. 3 — "**shall** be reduced to writing by the Judge" | Mandatory | An oral examination that is not recorded is of no forensic value whatever |
| R. 4(2) — "the Court **may** pronounce judgment against him" | Discretionary, drastic | The only place in the Code where a judgment can follow from a failure to answer questions |

::: warning What "first hearing" means, and why it matters
Rules 1, 1A and 2(1) are all pegged to "the first hearing of the suit". That is **not** the return date on the summons, and it is not the date the written statement is filed. It is the first date on which the court applies its judicial mind to the pleadings — ordinarily the date fixed for the framing of issues under Order XIV Rule 1(5). Every subsequent limitation calculation that runs "from the first hearing" (including Order VIII Rule 1's outer limit, and the Order XI Rule 1 window in commercial suits) is affected by this.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**(a) Order X is the Code's only inquisitorial moment.** Everywhere else the Code is adversarial: the parties plead, the parties lead evidence, the court decides. Order X reverses that briefly. The judge questions the parties directly, in open court, to find out what is genuinely disputed. The theory is that pleadings are drafted by lawyers and are therefore evasive, while a party answering a judge's question on his feet is not.

**(b) Rule 1 and Rule 2 do different jobs.** Rule 1 is a *paper* exercise: the judge compares the plaint against the written statement and asks the parties to say, on the record, whether each unaddressed allegation is admitted or denied. Rule 2 is an *oral* exercise: the judge questions the party (or the person who came with him — a manager, an accountant, a family member) to elucidate what the pleadings have left murky. Rule 1 produces admissions; Rule 2 produces clarity.

**(c) The examination under Rule 2 is not evidence.** This is the single most common confusion. A statement recorded under Order X Rule 2 is not deposition, is not on oath in the sense of section 5 of the Oaths Act, and cannot be used as substantive proof of a fact. It can be used to identify the controversy, to found an admission for the purposes of Order XII, and to test the credibility of a witness who later says something different. It cannot be used to decide the suit.

**(d) Rule 1A is the enforcement arm of section 89.** Section 89 of the Code says that where the court finds elements of a settlement, it shall formulate the terms, give them to the parties, and refer the dispute to arbitration, conciliation, judicial settlement (including Lok Adalat) or mediation. Order X Rule 1A converts that from a discretionary judicial instinct into a fixed step in the calendar of every suit: after admissions and denials are recorded, the court **must** put the parties to their election. Rules 1B and 1C then handle the mechanics — the parties appear before the chosen forum, and if the presiding officer of that forum concludes the exercise is futile, the matter comes back to court on a fixed date.

**(e) The section 89 drafting anomaly.** Section 89(1) as enacted required the court to "formulate the terms of settlement" *before* the reference and then "reformulate" them after receiving the parties' observations. Read literally, that would require the judge to draft the settlement before the parties had even agreed to talk — an absurdity, and a duplication of the mediator's function. The Supreme Court has read the requirement down: the court need only formulate a summary of the disputes, not the terms of a settlement, and the detailed formulation belongs to the ADR forum. The same judgment corrected the interchange of the definitions of "judicial settlement" and "mediation" in clauses (c) and (d) of section 89(2).

**(f) Consent, and when it is needed.** Of the five modes, **arbitration and conciliation** cannot be imposed — they need the agreement of both sides, because they end in an award or settlement that displaces the court's jurisdiction. **Lok Adalat, judicial settlement and mediation** can be referred without consent, because none of them can conclude the dispute without the parties' agreement anyway; a failed mediation simply returns the suit to the docket.

**(g) Rule 4 — the sanction nobody uses.** If the pleader cannot answer a material question and the court thinks the client could, the court may adjourn and direct the client's personal attendance. If the client then stays away without lawful excuse, the court "may pronounce judgment against him". It is a formidable power and it is almost never exercised, because the alternative — "such order in relation to the suit as it thinks fit" — usually means costs. But the power exists, and it is worth invoking against a corporate defendant that sends only counsel to every hearing.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

::: tip Illustration 1 — the evasive denial that Rule 1 exposes
A suit for recovery of ₹42 lakh on a running account. The plaint pleads twelve specific invoices with dates and amounts. The written statement says: "The contents of paragraphs 4 to 9 of the plaint are denied. The defendant puts the plaintiff to strict proof."

**Sword (plaintiff).** At the first hearing counsel invites the court to exercise its Rule 1 duty and ask the defendant, invoice by invoice, whether the goods were received. The defendant's manager, who is present in court, is examined under Rule 2(1)(b). He concedes that ten of the twelve consignments were received and that only two are disputed on quality. The court records this under Rule 3. The plaintiff immediately applies under Order XII Rule 6 for a decree on the admitted ten invoices, and the trial narrows to a quality dispute over ₹6 lakh.

**Shield (defendant).** Counsel resists the Rule 2 examination on the ground that the manager is not a party and that his answers would in substance be evidence taken before issues are framed. He asks that any examination be confined to elucidation and that the record note expressly that the answers are not deposition. He also objects that the plaintiff is using Order X to circumvent Order XI — if he wants admissions he should serve a notice to admit under Order XII Rule 2, on which costs consequences follow, rather than ambushing a witness at the first hearing.
:::

::: tip Illustration 2 — the section 89 reference in a partition suit
A partition suit among four siblings over an ancestral house and two agricultural plots. The pleadings disclose no dispute about the genealogy or the shares — only about who occupies what and who has spent what on repairs.

**Sword (the plaintiff seeking a reference).** After admissions and denials are recorded, counsel points out that title and shares are admitted and that what remains is allotment and equities — classically mediable. He invokes Rule 1A and asks that the parties be put to their election, proposing mediation before the court-annexed centre. Because mediation does not require consent, the reluctant sibling cannot block it. The mediation produces a memorandum of allotment which is placed before the court and made a decree.

**Shield (the sibling resisting).** Counsel does not resist the reference — that would be futile — but insists that the order under Rule 1A record that the reference is without prejudice to his contention that one plot is self-acquired, and that the fixed date under Rule 1C be short, so that the suit is not parked for a year. He also asks that the court not "formulate terms of settlement", because any formulation touching the self-acquired plot would prejudge the very issue he wants tried.
:::

::: tip Illustration 3 — Rule 4 against an absent corporate defendant
A suit against a company for wrongful termination of a distributorship. At the first hearing the court asks whether the termination letter of 14 March was preceded by any show-cause notice. Counsel says he has no instructions. The court asks whether the company received the plaintiff's letter of 2 March. Counsel again has no instructions.

**Sword (plaintiff).** Counsel invokes Rule 4(1), submits that these are material questions which the company's authorised signatory is plainly able to answer, and asks the court to postpone the hearing and direct his personal attendance. The court does so. If the signatory does not appear without lawful excuse, Rule 4(2) opens.

**Shield (defendant).** The company files an application supported by an affidavit explaining that the signatory is abroad, offering an alternative officer with knowledge of the file, and undertaking to file a supplementary affidavit answering the two questions within a week. Rule 4(2) bites only on failure "without lawful excuse"; a substantiated excuse plus a substitute usually ends the matter, sometimes with costs.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

#### *Siraj Ahmad Siddiqui v. Prem Nath Kapoor*, (1993) 4 SCC 406 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A tenant's eviction proceeding turned on when the "first hearing" occurred, because the statutory deposit had to be made by that date.

**Ratio decidendi:** The "first hearing of the suit" is **not** the date fixed in the summons for the defendant's appearance, nor the date on which the written statement is filed. It is the date on which the court applies its mind to the case — ordinarily the date on which issues are framed or the case is otherwise taken up for consideration of the pleadings. Steps that are purely ministerial do not constitute the first hearing.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The first hearing within the meaning of Order X Rule 1 is the date on which the Court applies its judicial mind to the pleadings, which in the ordinary course is the date fixed for settlement of issues under Order XIV Rule 1(5), and not the return date of the summons."
:::

#### *Salem Advocate Bar Association, Tamil Nadu v. Union of India (II)*, (2005) 6 SCC 344 — 3-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A challenge to the working of the 1999 and 2002 amendments to the Code, in which the Court considered a committee report on section 89 and the amended Orders.

**Ratio decidendi:** The amended provisions, including Order X Rule 1A and section 89, are constitutionally valid and are to be worked purposively. The court's obligation at the Rule 1A stage is to direct the parties to opt for a mode of settlement; the model rules framed by the committee were approved as guidance. The Court also held that time-limits in the amended Code (notably Order VIII Rule 1) are directory rather than mandatory, but that the discretion to extend must be exercised for reasons recorded.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Order X Rule 1A imposes a mandatory procedural step: after recording admissions and denials the Court is bound to direct the parties to elect a mode under section 89, and a decree passed without that step, while not a nullity, reflects a departure from a mandated stage of the suit."
:::

#### *Afcons Infrastructure Ltd. v. Cherian Varkey Construction Co. (P) Ltd.*, (2010) 8 SCC 24 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A dispute over whether a court could refer parties to arbitration under section 89 in the absence of an arbitration agreement, and over the internally inconsistent drafting of the section.

**Ratio decidendi:** Section 89 must be read down. (i) The court is not required to formulate the *terms of settlement*; it need only formulate a **summary of the disputes**, the detailed working being for the ADR forum. (ii) The definitions in clauses (c) and (d) of section 89(2) are **interchanged** — what is described as "judicial settlement" is mediation and vice versa — and must be read as corrected. (iii) **Arbitration and conciliation** require the consent of all parties; **Lok Adalat, judicial settlement and mediation** may be referred without consent. (iv) The Court set out categories of cases ordinarily unsuited to ADR (suits involving public interest, serious fraud, prosecution for criminal offences, protection of courts' jurisdiction over minors and persons of unsound mind) and categories ordinarily suited to it (trade, matrimonial, partition, tenancy, consumer and strictly personal disputes).

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Under *Afcons*, a reference to arbitration under section 89 requires the written consent of all parties; but a reference to mediation, judicial settlement or Lok Adalat may be made notwithstanding the objection of a party, since none of those modes can conclude the dispute without agreement."
:::

#### *Kapil Corepacks (P) Ltd. v. Harbans Lal*, (2010) 8 SCC 452 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A statement made by a party during an Order X examination was relied on as an admission binding the party at trial.

**Ratio decidendi:** An examination under Order X Rule 2 is for the limited purpose of elucidating matters in controversy. It is **not a substitute for evidence** and a statement recorded under it is not deposition on oath. A concession made in the course of such an examination can operate as an admission only if it is clear, unambiguous and recorded as such under Rule 3; it cannot be stretched to concede a case that the pleadings do not concede.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A statement recorded under Order X Rule 2 is not evidence and cannot be treated as proof of a fact in issue; it may found an admission only where the concession is clear and unambiguous and is recorded under Rule 3."
:::

#### *K. Srinivas Rao v. D.A. Deepa*, (2013) 5 SCC 226 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A matrimonial dispute in which the Court considered the systemic role of mediation in family litigation.

**Ratio decidendi:** Courts should, at the earliest stage, direct parties in matrimonial and family disputes to mediation, and the reference should ordinarily precede a contested trial. The Court issued directions to criminal and family courts to refer such disputes to mediation centres before proceeding, reinforcing the Order X Rule 1A stage as an active rather than formal step.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"In matrimonial and family disputes the Order X Rule 1A direction is not a formality; the settled practice endorsed by the Supreme Court is that such disputes be referred to a mediation centre before the suit is set down for trial."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
*Salem Advocate Bar Association (II)* was decided on a set of model rules that individual High Courts have since adopted with local variations — always check your own High Court's ADR Rules and the mediation-centre roster before drafting the Rule 1A order. The Mediation Act, 2023 has since created a statutory framework for pre-litigation and court-annexed mediation which overlaps with section 89; check its commencement and the applicability of its section 7 to your dispute before assuming section 89 alone governs.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

::: danger Five ways the Order X stage is wasted
1. **Treating it as a formality.** Counsel appears, says "no admissions", and the court moves to issues. The single most cost-effective narrowing opportunity in the whole suit is thrown away.
2. **Not getting the admission recorded.** A concession made across the bar and not reduced to writing under Rule 3 does not exist. If the opposite party admits execution of the document, ask the court in terms to record it, and check the order sheet before you leave.
3. **Treating the Rule 2 answer as evidence.** Building a case on a Rule 2 statement, without leading the fact in evidence, guarantees a finding against you on that issue at trial.
4. **Consenting to arbitration casually.** A section 89 reference to arbitration ends the court's jurisdiction over the merits and produces an award challengeable only under section 34 of the 1996 Act. Do not tick that box to be agreeable.
5. **Ignoring the refund.** Failing to apply for the certificate for refund of court fee after a settlement leaves the client's money with the State.
:::

::: warning Strategic timing
- **Bring your client, or the person who knows the file, to the first hearing.** Rule 2(1)(b) lets the court examine the person accompanying the party. If your accountant is in court, favourable clarifications go on the record; if only counsel appears, the opponent gets to shape the narrative.
- **Use Order X to set up Order XII Rule 6, not to win the case.** The purpose of the exercise is a partial decree on the admitted portion and a shorter list of issues. Frame your questions to the court with that endpoint in view.
- **Ask for a short Rule 1C date.** ADR references become graveyards when the return date is four months away. Ask for six weeks, with liberty to extend.
- **If you want the reference, ask for mediation, not arbitration.** Mediation needs no consent, costs nothing, and returns the suit intact if it fails.
- **Object to arbitration in writing** if you do not want it. Consent under *Afcons* must be that of all parties; a recorded objection forecloses the argument that you acquiesced.
:::

**Model order sought under Rule 1A (to be pressed at the first hearing):**

> It is respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to:
>
> **(a)** record, under Order X Rule 1 read with Rule 3, the admissions of the defendant in respect of the execution of the agreement dated \_\_\_\_ and the receipt of the consignments covered by invoices Nos. \_\_\_\_ to \_\_\_\_;
>
> **(b)** direct the parties, under Order X Rule 1A read with section 89(1)(d) of the Code, to appear before the \_\_\_\_ Mediation Centre, and fix a date of appearance before that forum;
>
> **(c)** direct the said Centre to report the outcome of the mediation to this Hon'ble Court on or before \_\_\_\_, and fix \_\_\_\_ as the date of appearance of the parties before this Hon'ble Court under Order X Rule 1C;
>
> **(d)** record that this reference is without prejudice to the rights and contentions of the parties on the merits of the suit;
>
> **(e)** pass such further or other order as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit.

## Order XI — discovery, interrogatories and inspection

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **Rule 1. Discovery by interrogatories.** — In any suit the plaintiff or defendant by leave of the Court may deliver interrogatories in writing for the examination of the opposite parties or any one or more of such parties; and such interrogatories when delivered shall have a note at the foot thereof stating which of such interrogatories each of such persons is required to answer:
>
> *Provided that no party shall deliver more than one set of interrogatories to the same party without an order for that purpose:*
>
> *Provided also that interrogatories which do not relate to any matters in question in the suit shall be deemed irrelevant, notwithstanding that they might be admissible on the oral cross-examination of a witness.*

> **Rule 2. Particular interrogatories to be submitted.** — On an application for leave to deliver interrogatories, the particular interrogatories proposed to be delivered shall be submitted to the Court. In deciding upon such application, the Court shall take into account any offer, which may be made by the party sought to be interrogated, to deliver particulars, or to make admissions, or to produce documents relating to the matters in question, or any of them, and leave shall be given as to such only of the interrogatories submitted as the Court shall consider necessary either for disposing fairly of the suit or for saving costs.

> **Rule 4. Form of interrogatories.** — Interrogatories shall be in the form No. 2 in Appendix C, with such variations as circumstances may require.

> **Rule 5. Corporations.** — Where any party to a suit is a corporation or a body of persons, whether incorporated or not, empowered by law to sue or be sued, whether in its own name or in the name of any officer or other person, any opposite party may apply for an order allowing him to deliver interrogatories to any member or officer of such corporation or body, and an order may be made accordingly.

> **Rule 6. Objections to interrogatories by answer.** — Any objection to answering any interrogatory on the ground that it is scandalous or irrelevant or not exhibited *bona fide* for the purpose of the suit, or that the matters inquired into are not sufficiently material at that stage, or on the ground of privilege or any other ground may be taken in the affidavit in answer.

> **Rule 7. Setting aside and striking out interrogatories.** — Any interrogatories may be set aside on the ground that they have been exhibited unreasonably or vexatiously, or struck out on the ground that they are prolix, oppressive, unnecessary or scandalous; and any application for this purpose may be made within seven days after service of the interrogatories.

> **Rule 8. Affidavit in answer, filing.** — Interrogatories shall be answered by affidavit to be filed within ten days, or within such other time as the Court may allow.

> **Rule 11. No exception to be taken to affidavit in answer.** — No exceptions shall be taken to any affidavit in answer, but the sufficiency or otherwise of any such affidavit objected to as insufficient shall be determined by the Court.

> **Rule 12. Application for discovery of documents.** — Any party may, without filing any affidavit, apply to the Court for an order directing any other party to any suit to make discovery on oath of the documents which are or have been in his possession or power, relating to any matter in question therein. On the hearing of such application the Court may either refuse or adjourn the same, if satisfied that such discovery is not necessary, or not necessary at that stage of the suit, or make such order, either generally or limited to certain classes of documents, as may, in its discretion be thought fit:
>
> *Provided that discovery shall not be ordered when and so far as the Court shall be of opinion that it is not necessary either for disposing fairly of the suit or for saving costs.*

> **Rule 14. Production of documents.** — It shall be lawful for the Court, at any time during the pendency of any suit, to order the production by any party thereto, upon oath, of such of the documents in his possession or power, relating to any matter in question in such suit, as the Court shall think right; and the Court may deal with such documents, when produced, in such manner as shall appear just.

> **Rule 15. Inspection of documents referred to in pleadings or affidavits.** — Every party to a suit shall be entitled at any time to give notice to any other party, in whose pleadings or affidavits reference is made to any document, or who has entered any document in any list annexed to his pleadings, to produce such document for the inspection of the party giving such notice, or of his pleader, and to permit him or them to take copies thereof; and any party not complying with such notice shall not afterwards be at liberty to put any such document in evidence on his behalf in such suit unless he shall satisfy the Court that such document relates only to his own title, he being a defendant to the suit, or that the reason for not complying with such notice was sufficient.

> **Rule 18. Order for inspection.** — (1) Where the party served with notice under rule 15 omits to give such notice of a time for inspection or objects to give inspection, the Court may, on the application of the party desiring it, make an order for inspection in such place and in such manner as it may think fit:
>
> *Provided that the order shall not be made when and so far as the Court shall be of opinion that it is not necessary either for disposing fairly of the suit or for saving costs.*

> **Rule 20. Premature discovery.** — Where the party from whom discovery of any kind or inspection is sought objects to the same, or any part thereof, the Court may, if satisfied that the right to the discovery or inspection sought depends on the determination of any issue or question in dispute in the suit, or that for any other reason it is desirable that any issue or question in dispute in the suit should be determined before deciding upon the right to the discovery or inspection, order that such issue or question be determined first, and reserve the question as to the discovery or inspection.

> **Rule 21. Non-compliance with order for discovery.** — (1) Where any party fails to comply with any order to answer interrogatories, or for discovery or inspection of documents, he shall, if a plaintiff, be liable to have his suit dismissed for want of prosecution, and, if a defendant, to have his defence, if any, struck out, and to be placed in the same position as if he had not defended, and the party interrogating or seeking discovery or inspection may apply to the Court for an order to that effect and an order may be made on such application accordingly, after notice to the parties and after giving them a reasonable opportunity of being heard.
>
> (2) Where an order is made under sub-rule (1) dismissing any suit, the plaintiff shall be precluded from bringing a fresh suit on the same cause of action.

> **Rule 22. Using answers to interrogatories at trial.** — Any party may, at the trial of a suit, use in evidence any one or more of the answers or any part of an answer of the opposite party to interrogatories without putting in the others or the whole of such answer: provided always that in such case the Court may look at the whole of the answers, and if it shall be of opinion that any other of them is so connected with those put in that the last-mentioned answers ought not to be used without them, it may direct them to be put in.

#### Statutory mechanics

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| R. 1 — "**by leave of the Court**" | Condition precedent | Interrogatories delivered without leave are a nullity and can be ignored |
| R. 1, first proviso — "**no** party shall deliver more than one set" | Prohibitory | A second set requires a separate order; plan the first set exhaustively |
| R. 1, second proviso — "**deemed irrelevant, notwithstanding** that they might be admissible on … cross-examination" | Deeming provision | The credit-only question that is fair in cross is impermissible as an interrogatory |
| R. 2 — "leave **shall** be given as to such only … as the Court shall consider **necessary** either for disposing fairly of the suit or for saving costs" | Mandatory limiting test | The single test that governs all of Order XI |
| R. 2 — "the Court **shall take into account any offer** … to deliver particulars, or to make admissions" | Mandatory consideration | A well-timed offer of particulars can defeat the whole application |
| R. 6 — objections "**may be taken in the affidavit in answer**" | Enabling | Privilege and relevance are pleaded in the answer, not by refusing to answer |
| R. 7 — "within **seven days** after service" | Fixed period | The window to strike out interrogatories as oppressive is short |
| R. 8 — "**shall** be answered by affidavit … within **ten days**" | Mandatory, extendable | Time may be enlarged; the obligation to answer on affidavit may not |
| R. 11 — "**No exceptions shall be taken**" | Prohibitory | You do not file a reply to the answer; you move the court on sufficiency |
| R. 12 and R. 18, provisos — "**shall not** be ordered … not necessary either for disposing fairly of the suit or for saving costs" | Jurisdictional limit | The proviso is the shield against fishing expeditions |
| R. 15 — "**shall not afterwards be at liberty** to put any such document in evidence" | Statutory bar with escape | Non-compliance with a notice to produce forfeits the document, subject to sufficient cause |
| R. 21(1) — "**shall** … be liable to have his suit dismissed / defence struck out" | Drastic, discretionary in application | The only real sanction in the Order |
| R. 21(1) — "**after notice** … and after giving them a **reasonable opportunity of being heard**" | Condition precedent | An order under R. 21 passed without hearing is liable to be set aside |
| R. 21(2) — "**shall be precluded** from bringing a fresh suit" | Statutory bar | Mirrors Order IX Rule 9; dismissal under R. 21 is terminal |

::: warning Commercial suits are governed by a different Order XI
For a "commercial dispute of a Specified Value" within the meaning of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, Order XI **as it stands in the Code is substituted in its entirety** by the Order XI in the Schedule to that Act. That version requires a **statement of truth and a list of all documents in a party's power, possession, control or custody to be filed with the plaint and the written statement**, permits inspection within thirty days, and provides for a case management hearing at which the court fixes the discovery calendar. Do not cite the Code's Order XI in a commercial suit, and do not cite the Commercial Courts Act's Order XI in an ordinary civil suit. Check the Specified Value threshold in force in your State.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**(a) Two different machineries live in one Order.** Rules 1 to 11 and Rule 22 deal with **interrogatories** — written questions that the opposite party must answer on affidavit. Rules 12 to 20 deal with **discovery and inspection of documents** — compelling the other side to disclose what it holds and let you look at it. Rule 21 supplies the sanction for both. They are independent: you can seek documents without interrogating, and vice versa.

**(b) The purpose is to narrow, not to win.** Discovery exists so that neither side is ambushed and so that the trial is confined to what is genuinely contested. Rule 2 and the provisos to Rules 12 and 18 all impose the same test — is this **necessary either for disposing fairly of the suit or for saving costs**? A request that satisfies neither limb is refused, however interesting the answer might be.

**(c) "Fishing and roving" is the standard objection, and it has a precise meaning.** A fishing enquiry is one where the party does not know that a fact exists and is using discovery to find out whether it might. That is impermissible. It is quite different from a party who knows the fact exists and is using discovery to compel its admission or to obtain the document that proves it — which is exactly what the Order is for. The line is drawn by the pleadings: discovery must relate to "any matter in question in the suit", and the matters in question are fixed by what has been pleaded.

**(d) What may be interrogated about.** Facts, not evidence, and not law. You may ask whether the defendant received a letter, whether he was a director on a given date, what the terms of an oral agreement were as he understands them, whether a payment was made. You may not ask him what his witnesses will say, what his counsel advises, or questions going purely to credit — the second proviso to Rule 1 forecloses exactly that, and says so in terms.

**(e) Objecting the right way.** Rule 6 is often misread. A party served with interrogatories does **not** simply refuse to answer. He files the affidavit in answer and takes his objections in it — that the question is scandalous, irrelevant, not *bona fide*, immaterial at this stage, or privileged. Separately, and within **seven days** of service, he may move under Rule 7 to have the interrogatories set aside as vexatious or struck out as prolix or oppressive. Refusing to answer without doing either exposes him to Rule 21.

**(f) Privilege survives Order XI.** Communications between a client and his legal adviser (sections 126–129 of the Evidence Act, 1872; sections 132–135 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023), affairs of State (section 123), and official communications (section 124) are all outside discovery. The claim of privilege is made in the affidavit in answer, and the court may inspect the document to test the claim without disclosing it to the other side.

**(g) The document you did not disclose, you may not use.** Rule 15 is the practitioner's most useful lever. If your opponent has referred to a document in his pleadings or affidavits, or listed it, you can serve a notice to produce it for inspection. If he does not comply, Rule 15 bars him from putting that document in evidence at all — subject to the two escapes: a defendant's own title deeds, and sufficient cause for non-compliance.

**(h) Premature discovery, and why Rule 20 exists.** Sometimes the right to discovery itself depends on an issue in the suit — a party seeking partnership accounts must first be a partner. Rule 20 lets the court try that anterior question first and reserve the discovery. It is the answer to the objection "he is not entitled to see our books unless and until he establishes he is a shareholder".

**(i) Rule 21 is a last resort with a hard edge.** The rule empowers dismissal of the suit or striking out of the defence. Because it decides a case without a trial, courts insist on wilful and contumacious default, not mere delay, and Rule 21(1) itself requires notice and a reasonable opportunity of being heard. But once a suit is dismissed under Rule 21(1), sub-rule (2) bars a fresh suit on the same cause of action — precisely as Order IX Rule 9 does.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

::: tip Illustration 1 — interrogatories in a suit on an oral agreement
A suit for specific performance of an oral agreement to sell, resisted on the ground that no agreement was ever made and that the payment of ₹15 lakh relied on was a loan.

**Sword (plaintiff).** With his application under Rule 2 he submits eight interrogatories: whether the defendant received ₹15 lakh on 4 June; whether it was received by cheque; the number and bank of that cheque; whether the defendant treated it as a loan in his books; whether any loan agreement was executed; whether interest was ever charged or paid; whether the sum was disclosed in his income-tax return for the relevant year; and if so, under what head. Each is a fact within the defendant's knowledge, each relates to a matter in question, and the answers will either establish the plaintiff's case or destroy the loan defence. He simultaneously applies under Rule 12 for discovery of the defendant's books of account and returns for the relevant year.

**Shield (defendant).** Within seven days he moves under Rule 7 to strike out the interrogatories about income-tax returns as oppressive and as directed to a collateral matter, and to set aside the last two as not *bona fide*. In his affidavit in answer under Rule 6 he objects to the interrogatory on his books as immaterial at this stage, discovery of accounts being premature before the existence of the agreement is decided — inviting the court to invoke Rule 20. He simultaneously offers, under Rule 2, to admit receipt of ₹15 lakh on 4 June by cheque, which removes the necessity for the first three interrogatories altogether.
:::

::: tip Illustration 2 — Rule 15 and the document that vanished
A suit for damages for breach of a distributorship. The written statement pleads that the plaintiff was terminated after an internal audit report dated 12 January found stock diversion, and refers to that report by date.

**Sword (plaintiff).** He serves a notice under Rule 15 to produce the audit report for inspection. The defendant does not respond. The plaintiff applies under Rule 18 for an order for inspection. The defendant then claims the report is privileged as prepared for the purpose of litigation. The plaintiff points out that the report predates the termination and could not have been prepared in contemplation of litigation, and that in any event the defendant has pleaded on it — a party who deploys a document in his pleadings cannot then shelter it. He asks in the alternative for the Rule 15 bar to be recorded: the defendant may not put the report in evidence.

**Shield (defendant).** He must choose. If he maintains the privilege, he loses the report as evidence and the pleaded justification for termination becomes unsupported. The tactically correct course is usually to produce it, redact any genuinely privileged legal advice appended to it, and file an affidavit explaining the redaction so that the court may inspect.
:::

::: tip Illustration 3 — Rule 21 against a stonewalling defendant
A partnership dissolution suit. The court orders the managing partner to make discovery on oath of the firm's books for six years. He files an affidavit saying the books "are not traceable". Three further orders follow over fourteen months, each met with the same answer, while he continues to operate the firm's bank account.

**Sword (plaintiff).** He applies under Rule 21(1) to have the defence struck out, placing on record the bank statements obtained from the bank showing that the firm continued to trade, and the GST returns filed during the same period which must have been drawn from books. The default is not inability but contumacy, and the material shows it.

**Shield (defendant).** He resists on the settled principle that Rule 21 is penal and must be reserved for wilful default; he files a police complaint about the missing records, offers a chartered accountant's certificate reconstructing the accounts from bank statements and returns, and asks that the court accept the reconstruction with liberty to the plaintiff to seek an audit under Order XXVI Rule 11, rather than shut out the defence altogether. Courts almost always take the middle course — costs and a further peremptory direction — before they strike out.
:::

::: info Discovery and the Limitation Act
There is no limitation period for an application under Order XI — it is an interlocutory step in a pending suit. What is time-sensitive is Rule 7's **seven days** to strike out interrogatories and Rule 8's **ten days** to answer. Note also that discovery cannot be used to cure a limitation defect in the plaint: if the suit is time-barred on its own averments, Order VII Rule 11(d) operates regardless of what discovery might reveal.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

#### *M.L. Sethi v. R.P. Kapur*, (1972) 2 SCC 427 — 3-Judge Bench

*Facts:* An order refusing discovery of documents in a suit for damages for malicious prosecution was challenged, raising the scope of the discovery jurisdiction and the reach of revision under section 115.

**Ratio decidendi:** The object of discovery is to enable a party to obtain from his adversary information about facts material to the case, so that the issues may be narrowed and surprise at trial avoided. Discovery is confined to matters in question in the suit as disclosed by the pleadings; it is not a licence for a **fishing or roving enquiry** into the opponent's case. The governing test is whether the discovery is necessary either for disposing fairly of the suit or for saving costs, and the court retains a discretion which will not be interfered with merely because another view was possible.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Discovery under Order XI is confined to matters in question in the suit as defined by the pleadings; it may not be used as a fishing or roving enquiry to ascertain whether a case exists, and leave will be granted only to the extent necessary for disposing fairly of the suit or for saving costs."
:::

#### *Maganlal v. Church of North India*, (2005) 10 SCC 484 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A party sought production of documents relating to the internal affairs of a society in a suit concerning possession of property.

**Ratio decidendi:** An order for production under Order XI must be tested against relevance to the matters in issue. Documents which do not bear on any issue arising from the pleadings will not be ordered to be produced merely because they may be of general interest or because they might yield some collateral advantage.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"An application for production under Order XI Rule 12 must identify the issue in the suit to which each class of documents is said to relate; a request framed by reference to categories of records rather than to issues is liable to be refused as irrelevant."
:::

#### *Rasiklal Manikchand Dhariwal v. M.S.S. Food Products*, (2012) 2 SCC 196 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A trade mark passing-off action in which interrogatories were sought on matters said to relate to the defendant's own case.

**Ratio decidendi:** Interrogatories may be administered on any matter in question in the suit, including facts that support the case of the party interrogating even though they also form part of the opponent's case. They may **not** be administered to obtain discovery of the **evidence** by which the opponent proposes to prove his case, nor on matters going only to credit. The test is whether the answer will serve to maintain the case of the interrogating party or destroy that of his adversary.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"An interrogatory is permissible if the answer will either support the case of the party administering it or destroy the case of his adversary; it is impermissible if it seeks the evidence by which the adversary proposes to prove his case, or goes merely to credit."
:::

#### *Babbar Sewing Machine Co. v. Trilok Nath Mahajan*, (1978) 4 SCC 188 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A defence was struck out under Order XI Rule 21 for failure to comply with an order for discovery.

**Ratio decidendi:** The penalty under Rule 21 is of a **drastic character** and must be applied only in extreme cases where there is **contumacious or wilful default** and a want of *bona fides*. Mere failure or delay in compliance does not suffice; there must be an obstinate refusal such that a fair trial has become impossible. The order is a last resort and the court must be satisfied that the default was deliberate.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"An order under Order XI Rule 21 striking out a defence or dismissing a suit is penal and may be made only where the default is wilful and contumacious and the party has shown an obstinate refusal to comply, not where there has been mere delay or inability."
:::

#### *Sudhir Kumar @ S. Baliyan v. Vinay Kumar G.B.*, (2021) 13 SCC 71 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* An application to place additional documents on record in a commercial suit after the pleadings had closed, under Order XI Rule 1(4) and (5) of the Commercial Courts Act version of Order XI.

**Ratio decidendi:** In a commercial suit, documents in a party's power, possession, control or custody must be disclosed with the pleading, and later production requires the party to establish **reasonable cause** for the earlier non-disclosure. The discipline of the Commercial Courts Act's Order XI is stricter than the Code's and is to be enforced, since the whole scheme of that Act is directed at time-bound disposal.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"In a commercial dispute of a Specified Value, a document not disclosed with the plaint or written statement can be brought on record only on establishing reasonable cause for non-disclosure; the Code's more liberal practice under Order XIII Rule 1 does not apply."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
The Evidence Act, 1872 has been replaced by the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023. Privilege provisions have been renumbered (ss. 126–129 → ss. 132–135; ss. 123–124 → ss. 129–130), and older judgments cite the 1872 numbering. Cite the current numbering with the old in parenthesis. The Commercial Courts Act's Specified Value threshold was reduced to ₹3 lakh by the 2018 amendment — confirm the figure and the notified commercial courts in your State before assuming which Order XI applies.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

::: danger Six ways a discovery application fails
1. **No leave.** Interrogatories delivered without an order under Rule 1 are waste paper, and the opposite party is entitled to ignore them.
2. **Framed by category, not by issue.** "All correspondence, books, records and documents relating to the transaction" invites refusal. Identify each class of document and the issue it goes to.
3. **Asking for the opponent's evidence.** "State the names of the witnesses you propose to examine" and "state what your witness will depose to about the meeting of 4 June" are both impermissible.
4. **Interrogating on credit.** The second proviso to Rule 1 deems irrelevant anything that would be admissible only in cross-examination. Previous convictions, unrelated litigation and general character are all out.
5. **Missing the seven-day window.** A party wanting interrogatories struck out as oppressive who lets Rule 7's seven days pass is left with only the Rule 6 objections in his answer.
6. **Moving under Rule 21 too early.** One default and an application to strike out the defence will be dismissed with costs and will make the court unsympathetic when the real contumacy arrives. Build the record: order, default, peremptory order, default, then apply.
:::

::: warning Strategic timing
- **File the discovery application before issues are framed, not after.** Rule 2's necessity test is easiest to satisfy while the shape of the trial is still being decided, and the answers may themselves affect the issues.
- **Serve a notice to admit under Order XII Rule 2 first.** It costs nothing, and Rule 2 of Order XI expressly requires the court to take into account any offer to make admissions — so a refused notice to admit strengthens the interrogatories application considerably.
- **Use Rule 15 before Rule 12.** If the document has been referred to in the pleadings, a notice to produce is available as of right and needs no application at all. Save the Rule 12 application for documents whose existence you must first establish.
- **Anticipate Rule 20.** If your entitlement to the documents depends on a contested status — partner, shareholder, member — ask the court to try that issue first as a preliminary issue under Order XIV Rule 2, rather than have the discovery reserved indefinitely.
- **Convert answers into a decree.** The moment the affidavit in answer contains a clear admission, file an Order XII Rule 6 application. Discovery that is not converted into a procedural gain is an expensive detour.
- **In a commercial suit, do your disclosure with the pleading.** Under the Commercial Courts Act's Order XI, the document you leave out is the document you will fight to bring in.
:::

## Chapter consolidation — Orders IX, X and XI together

These three Orders form the pre-trial spine of the suit, and they interlock more tightly than their separation in the Code suggests.

| Stage | Order | The question the court asks | The sanction if the answer is unsatisfactory |
|---|---|---|---|
| The day fixed in the summons | IX RR. 1–8 | Who has appeared? | Dismissal (plaintiff) or *ex parte* proceeding (defendant) |
| After a decree in absence | IX RR. 9, 13 | Was there sufficient cause? | R. 9 bars a fresh suit; R. 13 restores the defence |
| First hearing | X RR. 1, 3 | What is actually admitted? | Recorded admission founds an O. XII R. 6 decree |
| First hearing | X R. 1A | Will you attempt settlement? | Compulsory election; full court-fee refund on settlement |
| First hearing | X RR. 2, 4 | Can you explain what your pleading means? | Personal attendance; judgment under R. 4(2) |
| Before issues | XI RR. 1–11 | What do you say to these questions on oath? | Answers usable at trial under R. 22 |
| Before issues | XI RR. 12–20 | What documents do you hold? | R. 15 bars the undisclosed document from evidence |
| Persistent default | XI R. 21 | Is this contumacy? | Suit dismissed or defence struck out; fresh suit barred |

::: tip The eight sentences worth memorising
1. *Ex parte* is a mode of proceeding, not a species of decree — an *ex parte* decree is a decree on the merits and is appealable under section 96(2).
2. Order IX Rule 13's Explanation forecloses election: a defendant who has appealed and lost on the merits cannot then apply to set aside.
3. Order IX Rule 9's bar is on a fresh suit, not on an application to restore — and the bar operates only where the dismissal was for the plaintiff's default in appearance.
4. "Sufficient cause" under Rules 9 and 13 is to be construed liberally where the party has not been negligent, but the defendant must show the summons was not duly served *or* that he was prevented by sufficient cause.
5. The "first hearing" is the date the court applies its mind to the pleadings, ordinarily the date of framing issues — not the summons return date.
6. A statement under Order X Rule 2 is not evidence; it can found an admission only if clear and recorded under Rule 3.
7. Discovery is confined to matters in question on the pleadings; there is no fishing or roving enquiry, and no discovery of the opponent's evidence or of matters going to credit.
8. Order XI Rule 21 is penal — it requires wilful and contumacious default, notice, and a reasonable opportunity of being heard.
:::

::: warning Before you file
- Check whether your suit is a **commercial dispute of a Specified Value**. If it is, the Commercial Courts Act's Order XI displaces the Code's Order XI entirely, and disclosure obligations attach from the moment the plaint is filed.
- Check your **High Court's Original Side Rules** and any State amendment. Several High Courts have amended Order IX Rules 5 and 13 and Order XI Rule 8 (time to answer) and have local rules on court-annexed mediation under Order X Rule 1A.
- Diarise the **thirty days** under Article 123 of the Limitation Act, 1963 for an application under Order IX Rule 13, and the **thirty days** under Article 122 for restoration under Rule 9, both running from the date of the decree or dismissal (or, under the proviso to Article 123, from knowledge where the summons was not duly served).
- Remember that Order XI has no limitation period of its own but two short internal clocks: **seven days** under Rule 7 and **ten days** under Rule 8.
:::
