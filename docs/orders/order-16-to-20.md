# Orders XVI, XVII, XVIII, XIX & XX — Witnesses, Adjournments, Evidence, Affidavits and Judgment

This is the trial itself. Order XVI governs the machinery for getting witnesses into the witness box — the list, the summons, the expenses, and what happens when a summoned witness does not come. Order XVII contains the most abused provision in the Code: the power to adjourn, capped since 2002 at three adjournments per party, and the drastic Rule 3 power to decide the suit against a party who has had time and wasted it. Order XVIII fixes who begins, how evidence is recorded, and since 2002 permits examination-in-chief to be given on affidavit. Order XIX deals with affidavits generally and the court's power to order proof by affidavit. Order XX is the endpoint — the judgment, its contents, the time within which it must be pronounced, the decree that follows from it, and the special forms of decree in partition, possession, mesne profits, accounts and dissolution.

::: info The architecture in one breath
Order XVIII decides who speaks first and how the evidence is taken; Order XVI is the machinery for compelling the witnesses to be there; Order XVII is what happens when they are not. Order XIX allows the court to short-circuit oral evidence altogether where a point can safely be proved on affidavit — the same idea that Order XVIII Rule 4 generalised in 2002 by making the examination-in-chief an affidavit as a matter of course. Order XX then converts everything that has happened into a judgment that must deal with **each issue separately**, and a decree that must **agree with the judgment** and specify precisely what the court has granted. Every enforcement problem in execution can be traced back to a decree that was drawn imprecisely under Order XX Rule 6.
:::

## Order XVI — summoning and attendance of witnesses

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **Rule 1. List of witnesses and summons to witnesses.** — (1) On or before such date as the Court may appoint, and **not later than fifteen days after the date on which the issues are settled**, the parties shall present in Court a list of witnesses whom they propose to call either to give evidence or to produce documents and obtain summonses to such persons for their attendance in Court.
>
> (2) A party desirous of obtaining any summons for the attendance of any person shall file in Court an application stating therein the purpose for which the witness is proposed to be summoned.
>
> (3) The Court may, for reasons to be recorded, permit a party to call, whether by summoning through Court or otherwise, any witness, other than those whose names appear in the list referred to in sub-rule (1), if such party shows sufficient cause for the omission to mention the name of such witness in the said list.
>
> (4) Subject to the provisions of sub-rule (2), summonses referred to in this rule may be obtained by the parties on an application to the Court or to such officer as may be appointed by the Court in this behalf within five days of presenting the list of witnesses under sub-rule (1).

> **Rule 1A. Production of witnesses without summons.** — Subject to the provisions of sub-rule (3) of rule 1, any party to the suit may, without applying for summons under rule 1, bring any witness to give evidence or to produce documents.

> **Rule 2. Expenses of witness to be paid into Court on applying for summons.** — (1) The party applying for a summons shall, before the summons is granted and within a period to be fixed, pay into Court such a sum of money as appears to the Court to be sufficient to defray the travelling and other expenses of the person summoned in passing to and from the Court in which he is required to attend, and for one day's attendance.

> **Rule 6. Summons to produce document.** — Any person may be summoned to produce a document, without being summoned to give evidence; and any person summoned merely to produce a document shall be deemed to have complied with the summons if he causes such document to be produced instead of attending personally to produce the same.

> **Rule 7. Power to require persons present in Court to give evidence or produce document.** — Any person present in Court may be required by the Court to give evidence or to produce any document then and there in his possession or power.

> **Rule 10. Procedure where witness fails to comply with summons.** — (1) Where a person to whom a summons has been issued either to attend to give evidence or to produce a document fails to attend or to produce the document in compliance with such summons, the Court —
>
> *(a)* shall, if the certificate of the serving officer has not been verified by the affidavit, or if service of the summons has been effected by a party or his agent, or
>
> *(b)* may, if the certificate of the serving officer has been so verified,
>
> examine on oath the serving officer or the party or his agent, as the case may be, who has effected service, or cause him to be so examined by any Court, touching the service or non-service of the summons.
>
> (2) Where the Court sees reason to believe that such evidence or production is material, and that such person has, without lawful excuse, failed to attend or to produce the document in compliance with such summons or has intentionally avoided service, it may issue a proclamation requiring him to attend to give evidence or to produce the document at a time and place to be named therein; and a copy of such proclamation shall be affixed on the outer door or other conspicuous part of the house in which he ordinarily resides.
>
> (3) In lieu of or at the time of issuing such proclamation, or at any time afterwards, the Court may, in its discretion, issue a warrant, either with or without bail, for the arrest of such person, and may make an order for the attachment of his property to such amount as it thinks fit, not exceeding the amount of the costs of attachment and of any fine which may be imposed under rule 12:
>
> *Provided that no Court of Small Causes shall make an order for the attachment of immovable property.*

> **Rule 12. Procedure if witness fails to appear.** — (1) The Court may, where such person does not appear, or appears but fails to satisfy the Court that there was a lawful excuse, impose upon him such fine not exceeding five hundred rupees as it thinks fit, having regard to his condition in life and all the circumstances of the case, and may order his property, or any part thereof, to be attached and sold or, if already attached under rule 10, to be sold for the purpose of satisfying all costs of such attachment, together with the amount of the said fine, if any.

> **Rule 14. Court may of its own accord summon as witnesses strangers to suit.** — Subject to the provisions of this Code as to attendance and appearance and to any law for the time being in force, where the Court at any time thinks it necessary to examine any person, including a party to the suit, and not called as a witness by a party to the suit, the Court may, of its own motion, cause such person to be summoned as a witness to give evidence, or to produce any document in his possession, on a day to be appointed, and may examine him as a witness or require him to produce such document.

> **Rule 19. No witness to be ordered to attend in person unless resident within certain limits.** — No one shall be ordered to attend in person to give evidence unless he resides —
>
> *(a)* within the local limits of the Court's ordinary original jurisdiction, or
>
> *(b)* without such limits but at a place less than one hundred or (where there is railway or steamer communication or other established public conveyance for five-sixths of the distance between the place where he resides and the place where the Court is situate) less than five hundred kilometres distance from the Court-house.

> **Rule 21. Rules as to witnesses to apply to parties summoned.** — Where any party to a suit is required to give evidence or to produce a document, the provisions as to witnesses shall apply to him so far as they are applicable.

#### Statutory mechanics

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| R. 1(1) — "**not later than fifteen days** after the date on which the issues are settled" | Fixed outer limit | Ties the witness list to Order XIV; late listing needs R. 1(3) leave |
| R. 1(1) — "the parties **shall** present … a list" | Mandatory | The list defines who may be summoned through court |
| R. 1(2) — application "**stating therein the purpose**" | Condition precedent | Prevents summonses being taken out to harass |
| R. 1(3) — "**for reasons to be recorded** … if such party shows **sufficient cause**" | Discretionary escape | The route to a witness omitted from the list |
| R. 1(4) — summons obtainable "**within five days** of presenting the list" | Fixed period | Short; diarise it with the list |
| R. 1A — "**without applying for summons** … may bring any witness" | Enabling | A witness who will come voluntarily needs no process at all |
| R. 2(1) — expenses "**before the summons is granted**" | Condition precedent | An unfunded summons will not issue |
| R. 6 — "**deemed to have complied**" by causing the document to be produced | Deeming provision | The custodian need not attend personally |
| R. 7 — "**Any person present in Court may be required**" | Immediate compulsion | No summons, no expenses, no notice |
| R. 10(1)(a) — "**shall** … examine on oath the serving officer" where service was by a party | Mandatory | Private service must be judicially verified before coercion |
| R. 10(2) — "**without lawful excuse** … or has **intentionally avoided service**" | Condition precedent to proclamation | Requires a finding, not an assumption |
| R. 10(3) — warrant "**with or without bail**"; attachment capped at costs plus fine | Discretionary, capped | Arrest of a witness is the Code's bluntest instrument |
| R. 12(1) — fine "**not exceeding five hundred rupees** … having regard to his condition in life" | Capped penalty | The cap is unrevised and largely symbolic |
| R. 14 — "the Court **may, of its own motion**, cause such person to be summoned" | Court's independent power | Extends to a party not called by either side |
| R. 19 — "**No one shall be ordered** to attend in person unless he resides…" | Prohibitory, jurisdictional | 100 km, or 500 km where public conveyance covers five-sixths |
| R. 21 — provisions as to witnesses apply to a party | Extension | A party summoned as a witness faces the same coercive machinery |

::: warning Rule 19 is a real limit, and Rule 1A is the answer to it
A court cannot order a person to attend **in person** if he lives beyond the Rule 19 limits. The remedies are a commission under Order XXVI Rule 4 (examination of a witness residing beyond the limits), evidence on affidavit under Order XIX, or — most simply — Rule 1A: nothing prevents a party from **bringing** a distant witness voluntarily. Rule 19 restrains the court's power to compel, not a witness's willingness to appear.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**(a) Two routes into the witness box.** A witness either comes because the court has summoned him under Rule 1, or because a party has brought him under Rule 1A. The summons route is for the reluctant witness, the official custodian and the third party with documents. The Rule 1A route is for your own client, your own employees, and anyone who will attend willingly — and it is faster, cheaper and free of the service risks that Rules 10 to 12 exist to solve.

**(b) The list is a disclosure obligation with a deadline tied to Order XIV.** Within fifteen days of the settlement of issues each party must file a list of the witnesses it proposes to call, to give evidence or to produce documents. The purpose is the same as Order XIII Rule 1's for documents: to close the record and prevent ambush. A witness not on the list can still be called, but only under Rule 1(3), on sufficient cause shown for the omission, with reasons recorded.

**(c) Summons to give evidence and summons to produce are different instruments.** Rule 6 makes the point expressly: a person may be summoned merely to produce a document, and he complies by **causing the document to be produced** — sending it by a clerk with a covering letter. He need not attend. This is how bank records, revenue records, hospital records and company registers are ordinarily brought on record, and issuing a summons to give evidence to a bank manager when a summons to produce would do is a common and avoidable discourtesy.

**(d) Producing a document is not proving it.** A document produced under a Rule 6 summons arrives on the file. It still has to be tendered through a competent witness, ruled admissible and exhibited under Order XIII Rule 4. For bank records specifically, the Bankers' Books Evidence Act, 1891 provides a shortcut: a certified copy of an entry, accompanied by the statutory certificate, is admissible without producing the original book or calling the officer.

**(e) Expenses are a condition precedent, not a formality.** Rule 2 requires the applying party to deposit, before the summons is granted, an amount sufficient for the witness's travel and one day's attendance. Courts do not issue unfunded summonses, and the commonest reason a witness summons "was not issued" is that the diet money was never deposited. Deposit it with the application.

**(f) The escalation ladder for a witness who does not come.** Rule 10 sets out a sequence, and each rung requires the previous one. First, verify service — and where the summons was served **privately by a party**, sub-rule (1)(a) makes judicial examination of the server **mandatory**, because the court will not coerce on an unverified private return. Second, if the evidence is material and the witness has no lawful excuse or has evaded service, issue a **proclamation**, affixed at his residence. Third, and only in the court's discretion, issue a **warrant** with or without bail, and attach property up to the costs of attachment and any fine. Fourth, under Rule 12, impose a fine not exceeding five hundred rupees and sell the attached property to realise the costs and fine.

**(g) Rule 7 is the sharpest tool in the Order.** Any person **present in court** may be required, then and there, to give evidence or to produce a document in his possession. No list, no summons, no expenses, no notice. If the opposite party's manager is sitting in the back of the courtroom holding the file, Rule 7 reaches him. It is used far less than it could be.

**(h) Rule 14 puts the court in the arena, carefully.** The court may of its own motion summon anyone — including a party not called by either side — to give evidence or produce a document. The power exists because the court is not a passive umpire where the record is incomplete. But it must be exercised sparingly and never to fill a gap in one side's case: the party who has failed to prove a fact cannot ask the court to summon the witness he neglected.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

::: tip Illustration 1 — proving the bank trail in a suit on a loan
A suit to recover ₹28 lakh advanced by cheque. The defendant denies receipt. The plaintiff needs the defendant's bank statement showing the credit.

**Sword (plaintiff).** He does **not** summon the branch manager to give evidence. He applies under Rule 6 for a summons to the bank to produce the certified copy of the account statement for the relevant period, and separately relies on sections 4 and 6 of the Bankers' Books Evidence Act, 1891, under which a certified copy accompanied by the statutory certificate is admissible without production of the original ledger or the attendance of an officer. He deposits the Rule 2 expenses with the application, files the witness list within fifteen days of the settlement of issues, and specifies in the Rule 1(2) application that the purpose is to prove the credit entry of 4 June.

**Shield (defendant).** He objects that the account is his personal account and that a wholesale summons for two years of statements is a fishing exercise; he asks that the summons be confined to the single entry in issue. He also reserves the objection under Order XIII: production is not proof, and the certified copy must still be tendered and exhibited, with the statutory certificate in the correct form. If the certificate is defective the shortcut fails and the bank officer must be called.
:::

::: tip Illustration 2 — the witness who evades service
A suit for specific performance in which the attesting witness to the agreement — the defendant's own former employee — is critical. Summonses are returned three times with the endorsement "not found". The plaintiff's process server says the witness is being kept away.

**Sword (plaintiff).** He applies under Rule 10. Because the last service was attempted through his own agent, sub-rule (1)(a) obliges the court to examine the server on oath. The server deposes that the witness was seen at the premises and refused to receive the summons. On that material the plaintiff asks for a finding that the witness has intentionally avoided service, and for a **proclamation** under sub-rule (2) affixed at his residence. If that fails he applies under sub-rule (3) for a bailable warrant and attachment limited to the costs of attachment.

**Shield (defendant).** He is not the witness and cannot resist directly, but he opposes the material-evidence finding — arguing that the attesting witness's evidence is not necessary because execution is not denied — and he objects to a warrant on a third party as disproportionate where a commission under Order XXVI or evidence on affidavit under Order XIX would serve. That objection often succeeds: courts issue warrants against witnesses reluctantly.
:::

::: tip Illustration 3 — Rule 7 and the officer in the back row
A suit for damages against a construction company. The company's site engineer, who signed the daily progress reports, is sitting in court with a bundle of papers, having accompanied counsel. He is not in anyone's witness list.

**Sword (plaintiff).** He rises immediately and invokes Rule 7: the engineer is a person present in court and may be required then and there to give evidence and to produce the documents in his possession. No list, no summons, no notice and no expenses are required. The timing is the whole value of the application — the papers are in the room and the witness has not been prepared.

**Shield (defendant).** He resists on two grounds. First, that Rule 7 is not to be used to circumvent the Rule 1 list and the disclosure discipline, and that the plaintiff had every opportunity to list the engineer. Second, that the documents in the engineer's hands are counsel's brief and are privileged. He asks in the alternative that if the engineer is examined, the defendant be permitted to lead evidence in answer and that the examination be deferred to the next date so that the engineer may consult the record — a request the court will weigh against the obvious prejudice of allowing exactly the preparation that Rule 7 is designed to prevent.
:::

::: info The three ways to reach a witness you cannot compel
Where a witness lives beyond the Order XVI Rule 19 limits, is in custody, is aged or infirm, or is otherwise unable to attend, the Code offers three alternatives to a summons: (i) a **commission** under Order XXVI Rules 4 and 4A, where a commissioner records the evidence at the witness's location and returns the deposition to the court; (ii) evidence **on affidavit** under Order XIX Rule 1, where the court is satisfied that the point may properly be proved that way and the other side does not *bona fide* require cross-examination; and (iii) production from **prison** under Order XVI-A. In each case the applicant bears the cost, and in each case the right to cross-examine must be preserved or the evidence will carry little weight.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

#### *Mange Ram v. Brij Mohan*, (1983) 4 SCC 36 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A party's evidence was closed because he had failed to take steps to summon his witnesses within the time allowed, and the question was whether the consequence flowed automatically.

**Ratio decidendi:** The provisions of Order XVI regulating the filing of the list and the taking out of summonses are **procedural**, and non-compliance does not automatically deprive a party of the right to examine his witnesses. The court retains discretion, and the object of the rules is to ensure the orderly conduct of the trial, not to defeat a genuine case. Where a party seeks to examine a witness not summoned in time, the court should weigh the reason for the default and the materiality of the evidence, and may impose costs rather than shut out the witness.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Non-compliance with the timelines in Order XVI Rule 1 does not automatically forfeit the right to examine a witness; the court retains a discretion to permit the examination on terms, and the rule is directory in its operation rather than a bar."
:::

#### *Vidyadhar v. Manikrao*, (1999) 3 SCC 573 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A party to a suit for specific performance did not enter the witness box himself, and the effect of that omission was in question.

**Ratio decidendi:** Where a party to a suit does not appear in the witness box and offer himself for cross-examination on facts within his personal knowledge, a **presumption arises that the case set up by him is not correct**. The failure of a party to appear as a witness is a serious infirmity and may be treated as a circumstance against him. This is distinct from the failure to call an available witness, which attracts the adverse inference under section 114(g) of the Evidence Act.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A party who withholds himself from the witness box on facts within his personal knowledge invites the presumption that his case is not true; the omission is a serious infirmity and is to be weighed against him."
:::

#### *Ved Parkash Kharbanda v. Vimal Bindal*, 2013 SCC OnLine Del 3021 — Delhi High Court

*Facts:* A comprehensive review of the practice under Orders XVI and XVIII following the 2002 amendments, including the interaction between the witness list, the affidavit of evidence and the right to summon.

**Ratio decidendi:** A party is required to file the list of witnesses within the time prescribed by Order XVI Rule 1(1), and a witness not so listed can be examined only on sufficient cause shown under Rule 1(3). The affidavit of examination-in-chief under Order XVIII Rule 4 must be filed for each witness the party proposes to examine, and a witness whose affidavit is not filed is not to be examined merely because he was listed. The Rule 1A route dispenses with process but not with the obligation to list and to file the affidavit.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Order XVI Rule 1A permits a party to bring a witness without a summons, but it does not dispense with the requirement of listing the witness under Rule 1(1) and filing his affidavit of examination-in-chief under Order XVIII Rule 4."
:::

#### *Gopal Krishnaji Ketkar v. Mohamed Haji Latif*, AIR 1968 SC 1413 — 3-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A party in possession of the best evidence on a point withheld it, and the consequences of the withholding were considered.

**Ratio decidendi:** Where a party has in his possession evidence which would throw light on the controversy and withholds it, the court is entitled to draw an inference **against** him, and it is not open to that party to contend that the burden of proof lay on the other side. The duty to produce the best available evidence rests on the party who has it, irrespective of the formal incidence of the burden.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A party who withholds documents or witnesses within his exclusive knowledge or control cannot shelter behind the incidence of the burden of proof; the court may draw an adverse inference under section 114(g) of the Evidence Act notwithstanding that the burden lay elsewhere."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
The Evidence Act, 1872 has been replaced by the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023: the adverse-inference provision, section 114(g), is now section 119(g), and the judge's power to put questions, section 165, is now section 168. The fine cap of ₹500 in Order XVI Rule 12 has been amended upwards by several States — check your State amendment. Rule 19's distance limits are also subject to State variation, and some High Courts have made rules permitting video-conference evidence which effectively displaces the Rule 19 problem; check the current video-conferencing rules of your High Court, most of which were framed or revised after 2020.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

::: danger Six ways the witness stage goes wrong
1. **The list is filed late or not at all.** Fifteen days from the settlement of issues, and five days after that to take out the summonses. Miss it and every witness needs a Rule 1(3) application.
2. **Diet money not deposited.** The summons simply does not issue, and the party discovers this on the date fixed for evidence.
3. **Summoning the manager when you needed the document.** Rule 6 lets a custodian comply by sending the document. Summoning him to give evidence wastes a date and antagonises the institution.
4. **Confusing production with proof.** The document arrives under a Rule 6 summons and nobody tenders or exhibits it. It is not evidence.
5. **The party does not enter the witness box.** *Vidyadhar v. Manikrao* is cited against him in every judgment where it happens. If the client cannot be a witness, plead why.
6. **Asking for a warrant against a witness on an unverified private service return.** Rule 10(1)(a) makes examination of the server mandatory in that situation, and an application that skips it will be dismissed.
:::

::: warning Strategic timing
- **File the witness list the same week the issues are settled**, with the Rule 1(2) purpose statement and the Rule 2 deposit. It costs an afternoon and removes the commonest cause of a wasted evidence date.
- **List generously and examine selectively.** A name on the list can be dropped; a name omitted needs a Rule 1(3) application with reasons recorded.
- **Prefer Rule 1A for your own witnesses.** Bringing them removes service risk entirely and lets you control the sequence.
- **Use Rule 6, not Rule 1, for institutional records**, and cite the Bankers' Books Evidence Act where the record is a bank's.
- **Keep Rule 7 in reserve.** If the opponent's officer is in the courtroom, the moment is now, not on the next date.
- **Where the witness is beyond the Rule 19 limits, apply for a commission early** — under Order XXVI Rule 4 the commission takes months, and applying after evidence has begun guarantees an adjournment you will be blamed for.
:::

> It is respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to:
>
> **(a)** issue a summons under Order XVI Rule 6 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 to the Branch Manager, \_\_\_\_ Bank, \_\_\_\_ Branch, requiring him to cause to be produced before this Hon'ble Court a certified copy of the statement of account No. \_\_\_\_ for the period from \_\_\_\_ to \_\_\_\_, together with the certificate prescribed by section 2(8) and section 4 of the Bankers' Books Evidence Act, 1891;
>
> **(b)** record that the said summons is issued for production only and that the said Branch Manager shall be deemed to have complied with the summons upon causing the said documents to be produced, without the necessity of personal attendance;
>
> **(c)** permit the applicant to deposit the expenses assessed by this Hon'ble Court under Order XVI Rule 2 within such time as may be fixed;
>
> **(d)** pass such further or other order as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit and proper.

**Model prayer — application under Order XVI Rule 10 where a witness has evaded service:**

> It is respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to:
>
> **(a)** examine on oath, under Order XVI Rule 10(1) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, the process server / agent who effected service of the summons dated \_\_\_\_ upon the witness Shri \_\_\_\_, touching the service and non-service thereof;
>
> **(b)** hold, upon such examination, that the evidence of the said witness is material to the decision of this suit and that he has without lawful excuse failed to attend, alternatively has intentionally avoided service;
>
> **(c)** issue a proclamation under Order XVI Rule 10(2) requiring the said witness to attend and give evidence on a date to be fixed, and direct that a copy thereof be affixed on a conspicuous part of the house in which he ordinarily resides;
>
> **(d)** in the event of continued non-attendance, issue a bailable warrant under Order XVI Rule 10(3) for the arrest of the said witness;
>
> **(e)** pass such further or other order as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit and proper.

## Order XVII — adjournments

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **Rule 1. Court may grant time and adjourn hearing.** — (1) The Court may, if sufficient cause is shown, at any stage of the suit grant time to the parties or to any of them, and may from time to time adjourn the hearing of the suit for reasons to be recorded in writing:
>
> *Provided that no such adjournment shall be granted more than three times to a party during hearing of the suit.*
>
> (2) **Costs of adjournment.** — In every such case the Court shall fix a day for the further hearing of the suit, and shall make such orders as to costs occasioned by the adjournment or such higher costs as the Court deems fit:
>
> *Provided that,*
>
> *(a) when the hearing of the suit has commenced, it shall be continued from day to day until all the witnesses in attendance have been examined, unless the Court finds that, for the exceptional reasons to be recorded by it, the adjournment of the hearing beyond the following day is necessary,*
>
> *(b) no adjournment shall be granted at the request of a party, except where the circumstances are beyond the control of that party,*
>
> *(c) the fact that the pleader of a party is engaged in another Court, shall not be a ground for adjournment,*
>
> *(d) where the illness of a pleader or his inability to conduct the case for any reason, other than his being engaged in another Court, is put forward as a ground for adjournment, the Court shall not grant the adjournment unless it is satisfied that the party applying for adjournment could not have engaged another pleader in time,*
>
> *(e) where a witness is present in Court but a party or his pleader is not present or the party or his pleader, though present in Court, is not ready to examine or cross-examine the witness, the Court may, if it thinks fit, record the statement of the witness and pass such orders as it thinks fit dispensing with the examination-in-chief or cross-examination of the witness, as the case may be, by the party or his pleader not present or not ready as aforesaid.*

> **Rule 2. Procedure if parties fail to appear on day fixed.** — Where, on any day to which the hearing of the suit is adjourned, the parties or any of them fail to appear, the Court may proceed to dispose of the suit in one of the modes directed in that behalf by Order IX or make such other order as it thinks fit.
>
> *Explanation.* — Where the evidence or a substantial portion of the evidence of any party has already been recorded and such party fails to appear on any day to which the hearing of the suit is adjourned, the Court may, in its discretion, proceed with the case as if such party were present.

> **Rule 3. Court may proceed notwithstanding either party fails to produce evidence.** — Where any party to a suit to whom time has been granted fails to produce his evidence, or to cause the attendance of his witnesses, or to perform any other act necessary to the further progress of the suit, for which time has been allowed, the Court may, notwithstanding such default, —
>
> *(a)* if the parties are present, proceed to decide the suit forthwith, or
>
> *(b)* if the parties are, or any of them is, absent, proceed under rule 2.

#### Statutory mechanics

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| R. 1(1) — "**if sufficient cause is shown** … for **reasons to be recorded in writing**" | Condition precedent plus a duty to reason | An adjournment without recorded reasons is an appealable irregularity |
| R. 1(1), proviso — "**no** such adjournment shall be granted **more than three times** to a party" | Numerical cap | The 2002 amendment's central discipline |
| R. 1(2) — "the Court **shall** fix a day … and **shall** make such orders as to costs" | Mandatory | Costs on adjournment are not discretionary as to their imposition |
| R. 1(2)(a) — "**it shall be continued from day to day** until all the witnesses in attendance have been examined" | Mandatory day-to-day rule | Departure requires "exceptional reasons to be recorded" |
| R. 1(2)(b) — "**no** adjournment … except where the circumstances are **beyond the control** of that party" | Prohibitory with a narrow exception | The default position after 2002 is refusal |
| R. 1(2)(c) — counsel "**engaged in another Court** shall not be a ground" | Absolute exclusion | The single most-cited and least-observed clause in the Code |
| R. 1(2)(d) — illness of a pleader: court "**shall not** grant … unless … could not have engaged another pleader in time" | Condition precedent | Illness alone is insufficient; inability to substitute must be shown |
| R. 1(2)(e) — witness present, party or pleader absent or unready | Enabling, drastic | The court may record the statement and **dispense with** chief or cross-examination |
| R. 2 — "may proceed to dispose of the suit in one of the modes directed … by **Order IX**" | Cross-reference | Absence on an adjourned date attracts the Order IX consequences and remedies |
| R. 2, Explanation — where substantial evidence is already recorded, the court "may … proceed **as if such party were present**" | Deeming discretion | Converts what would be an *ex parte* decree into a decree on the merits |
| R. 3 — "to whom **time has been granted** fails to produce his evidence" | Condition precedent | Rule 3 does not apply unless time was actually granted for that purpose |
| R. 3(a) — "**if the parties are present**, proceed to **decide the suit forthwith**" | Merits disposal | Appeal under section 96 only; no Order IX remedy |
| R. 3(b) — "if the parties are, or any of them is, **absent**, proceed under rule 2" | Routes to Order IX | Preserves the setting-aside remedies where a party is absent |

::: warning The three-adjournment cap is real, and the "engaged in another court" clause is not a suggestion
The proviso to Rule 1(1) caps adjournments at **three per party for the whole hearing of the suit**, and clause (c) of the proviso to Rule 1(2) states in terms that a pleader's engagement in another court **shall not be a ground** for adjournment. The Supreme Court has repeatedly deprecated the routine grant of adjournments in disregard of these provisions. In practice the cap is honoured unevenly, but a party who has exhausted three adjournments and is met with a Rule 3 order has no answer, and the appellate court will not rescue him.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**(a) Rule 1 is the grant; Rules 2 and 3 are the consequences of wasting it.** Rule 1 lets the court adjourn on sufficient cause, subject to the cap, the day-to-day rule and the costs obligation. Rule 2 deals with a party who is **absent** on the adjourned date. Rule 3 deals with a party who was **given time for a specific purpose** and has not used it. The two are not alternatives to be picked at will; they are triggered by different facts, and the difference decides what remedy the losing party has.

**(b) The distinction that decides the remedy.** If a party is **absent** on an adjourned date, Rule 2 applies, Order IX applies through it, and the resulting decree is an *ex parte* decree or a dismissal for default — remediable under Order IX Rule 13 or Rule 9. If a party is **present but unready** after time has been granted, Rule 3(a) applies, the court decides the suit forthwith **on the merits**, and the only remedy is a first appeal under section 96. A defendant who is present through counsel and whose evidence is closed under Rule 3 cannot apply under Order IX Rule 13, because there is nothing *ex parte* about the decree.

**(c) The Explanation to Rule 2 blurs the line deliberately.** Where a party's evidence, or a substantial part of it, has already been recorded and he then stops appearing, the court may proceed "as if such party were present". The consequence is that the decree is a decree on the merits rather than an *ex parte* decree, and the Order IX Rule 13 route closes. The Explanation exists to stop a party who has led half his evidence from walking away and then obtaining a fresh trial by setting aside a supposed *ex parte* decree.

**(d) "Sufficient cause" narrowed sharply in 2002.** Clause (b) of the proviso to Rule 1(2) permits an adjournment at a party's request only "where the circumstances are **beyond the control** of that party". That is a materially higher standard than sufficient cause simpliciter. Illness of a party, a death in the family, a genuinely unforeseen unavailability of a witness — these qualify. Counsel's diary, an unfiled document, an unpaid expert, and a client who is travelling do not.

**(e) The pleader's engagement elsewhere.** Clause (c) is unambiguous and is the clause most often ignored in practice. Clause (d) then closes the obvious workaround: where illness or inability of the pleader is urged, the court must not adjourn unless satisfied that the party **could not have engaged another pleader in time**. The burden is on the party, and it is a burden about the party's conduct, not the advocate's health.

**(f) Clause (e) is the sanction for wasting a witness's day.** If the witness is in court and the party or pleader is absent or unready to examine or cross-examine, the court may record the statement and **dispense with** the chief-examination or the cross-examination. A cross-examination dispensed with under clause (e) is gone: the evidence stands unchallenged. This is the provision that most directly punishes casual unpreparedness, and it is used.

**(g) Rule 3 requires that time was actually granted.** The condition precedent is that the party "to whom time has been granted" failed to produce his evidence or to do the act "for which time has been allowed". If no time was granted for that purpose, Rule 3 is not available. This is the standard and often successful answer to a Rule 3 application: the order sheet shows the date was fixed for the opposite party's evidence, or for arguments, and no time was granted to the defaulting party at all.

**(h) Rule 3 is not a dismissal power either.** The court "decides the suit forthwith" — on the material actually on record. If the plaintiff has led no evidence, the suit is dismissed on the merits. If the defendant's evidence is closed, the suit is decided on the plaintiff's evidence, which the court must still weigh; a plaintiff whose own evidence does not make out his case does not win because the defendant defaulted.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

::: tip Illustration 1 — evidence closed under Rule 3, and the wrong remedy chosen
A suit for recovery. The defendant is granted three successive adjournments to lead evidence. On the fourth date his counsel appears, states that the witness is unavailable, and seeks one more adjournment. The court declines, closes the defendant's evidence under Rule 3(a), hears arguments and decrees the suit.

**Sword (plaintiff).** He resists the adjournment by producing the order sheet: three adjournments granted to the defendant for his evidence, exhausting the Rule 1(1) proviso cap. He points out that the reason offered — the witness's unavailability, unexplained — is not a circumstance beyond the defendant's control under clause (b), and that counsel's difficulty is expressly excluded by clause (c). He asks for closure under Rule 3(a), the parties being present.

**Shield (defendant).** His counsel makes the fatal choice: he files an application under **Order IX Rule 13** to set aside what he calls an *ex parte* decree. It is dismissed, because the decree is not *ex parte* — the defendant was present through counsel and the suit was decided on the merits under Rule 3(a). By the time he files a first appeal under section 96, limitation has run and he must also seek condonation. The correct course on the day was to press the adjournment on affidavit, offering heavy costs and an undertaking to produce the witness on the next date without fail, and if refused, to file the appeal at once.
:::

::: tip Illustration 2 — the witness who came and was not cross-examined
A partition suit. The plaintiff's witness, an eighty-year-old attesting witness to a will, is brought from another district and is present in court. The defendant's counsel is engaged before another court and sends a junior to seek an adjournment of the cross-examination.

**Sword (plaintiff).** He invokes clause (e) of the proviso to Rule 1(2): the witness is present, the pleader is not ready to cross-examine, and the court may record the statement and dispense with cross-examination. He reinforces it with clause (c) — engagement in another court is not a ground — and with the age and distance of the witness, which make a second attendance uncertain.

**Shield (defendant).** The junior must do three things at once: put on record that he holds instructions and can conduct the cross-examination himself if the court so directs, rather than let the cross be dispensed with; offer costs; and if an adjournment is truly unavoidable, offer an undertaking that the witness's expenses for the second attendance will be borne by the defendant. What he must not do is simply ask for a date. A cross-examination dispensed with under clause (e) leaves the will's attestation proved and unchallenged.
:::

::: tip Illustration 3 — the Explanation to Rule 2 and the vanishing plaintiff
A specific performance suit. The plaintiff has examined himself and two witnesses; his evidence is nearly complete. He then stops appearing for four consecutive dates.

**Sword (defendant).** He invokes the Explanation to Rule 2: a substantial portion of the plaintiff's evidence has been recorded, so the court may proceed **as if the plaintiff were present**. He asks that the plaintiff's evidence be closed, that he be permitted to lead his own evidence, and that the suit be decided on the merits. The advantage to him is decisive: a dismissal on the merits is res judicata and cannot be undone by an Order IX Rule 9 restoration application.

**Shield (plaintiff).** When he returns, he must resist the characterisation. He argues that the Explanation confers a **discretion** ("may, in its discretion") and that it should not be exercised where his absence is explained, where his evidence was not in fact substantially complete, and where the defendant will suffer no prejudice from a restoration on costs. If the court has already proceeded under the Explanation, his remedy is a first appeal, not restoration — and he should file both, in the alternative, to protect himself.
:::

::: info Section 35B is the forgotten enforcement mechanism
Section 35B of the Code provides that where a party obtains an adjournment and is ordered to pay costs, the payment of those costs is a **condition precedent to the further prosecution of the suit** (if the defaulter is the plaintiff) **or the defence** (if the defaulter is the defendant). Non-payment on the next date means the party "shall not be permitted to prosecute the suit or the defence, as the case may be". This is a far sharper tool than Order XVII Rule 3 and requires no finding about the sufficiency of any cause — only that the costs were ordered and were not paid. Ask for costs under section 35B expressly, and record the consequence in the order.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

#### *Salem Advocate Bar Association, Tamil Nadu v. Union of India (II)*, (2005) 6 SCC 344 — 3-Judge Bench

*Facts:* Among the amendments challenged was the proviso to Order XVII Rule 1(1) capping adjournments at three per party.

**Ratio decidendi:** The proviso limiting adjournments to three is **valid**, but it cannot be read so rigidly as to deny justice in a wholly exceptional case; in an extraordinary situation where a fourth adjournment is unavoidable — such as a genuine and unforeseeable calamity — the court retains power to grant it, on recording the exceptional reasons and imposing appropriate costs. The provision is nonetheless to be **strictly observed** as the norm, and routine adjournments in disregard of it are impermissible. Costs under Rule 1(2) must be real and compensatory, not nominal.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The three-adjournment cap in the proviso to Order XVII Rule 1(1) is the norm and is to be strictly observed; a further adjournment may be granted only in an extraordinary situation, for exceptional reasons recorded, and on compensatory costs."
:::

#### *Shiv Cotex v. Tirgun Auto Plast (P) Ltd.*, (2011) 9 SCC 259 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A plaintiff was given repeated opportunities to lead evidence, failed to do so, and the suit was dismissed after the evidence was closed under Order XVII Rule 3.

**Ratio decidendi:** Where a party has been afforded sufficient opportunity to lead evidence and has failed to avail of it, the court is **not obliged to give further opportunities** and may close the evidence and decide the suit. "Sufficient cause" is not to be stretched to accommodate a litigant who is indolent. A court is not powerless in the face of a party who protracts a trial, and the failure of the plaintiff to lead evidence despite opportunities justifies dismissal of the suit on the merits.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A party who has had adequate opportunity to lead evidence and has not availed of it cannot complain when his evidence is closed; the court is not bound to grant further opportunity, and a suit dismissed in those circumstances is dismissed on the merits."
:::

#### *Ishwarlal Mali Rathod v. Gopal*, (2021) SCC OnLine SC 1322 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A trial court granted repeated adjournments over several years in disregard of the Rule 1 proviso, and the practice came before the Supreme Court.

**Ratio decidendi:** The grant of repeated adjournments in disregard of the proviso to Order XVII Rule 1 and of clauses (b) to (e) of the proviso to Rule 1(2) defeats the object of the 1999 and 2002 amendments and is to be **deprecated**. Trial courts must record reasons, must observe the cap, and must impose real costs. The practice of granting adjournments as a matter of course is a principal cause of the delay in civil litigation and cannot be countenanced.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The Supreme Court has deprecated the routine grant of adjournments; a party opposing an adjournment should place the order sheet before the court, count the adjournments already granted to the applicant, and invoke the proviso to Order XVII Rule 1(1) in terms."
:::

#### *Prakash Chander Manchanda v. Janki Manchanda*, (1986) 4 SCC 699 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A decree was passed after the defendant, having been given time, failed to appear, and the question was whether the decree was one under Order XVII Rule 2 (attracting Order IX) or under Rule 3 (a merits decree).

**Ratio decidendi:** Where a party is **absent** on the adjourned date, the court can proceed **only under Rule 2**, and the resulting decree carries the Order IX consequences and remedies. Rule 3 in its unamended form applies where the party is **present** and has failed to perform the act for which time was granted. The court cannot invoke Rule 3 against an absent party so as to convert the decree into one on the merits and deprive him of the Order IX Rule 13 remedy.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Where the defaulting party is absent, the court must proceed under Order XVII Rule 2 and the decree attracts Order IX; only where the party is present may the court decide the suit forthwith under Rule 3(a), with the consequence that the decree is on the merits and appealable only."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
*Prakash Chander Manchanda* was decided on Rule 3 before its restructuring into clauses (a) and (b); the present Rule 3(b) expressly routes an absent party back to Rule 2, which codifies the same result. Cite the present text, and use the case for the principle. Note also that the *Salem Advocate (II)* relaxation of the three-adjournment cap is narrow and is frequently overstated — it permits a fourth adjournment in an extraordinary situation, not a general discretion to disregard the proviso.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

::: danger Six adjournment mistakes with lasting consequences
1. **Seeking a date because counsel is elsewhere.** Clause (c) excludes it in terms. Send a junior with instructions instead — a junior who can conduct the cross saves the cross.
2. **Letting a present witness go un-cross-examined.** Clause (e) permits the court to dispense with the cross-examination. That evidence then stands unchallenged for the rest of the case.
3. **Applying under Order IX Rule 13 against a Rule 3(a) decree.** It will be dismissed, and by then the section 96 limitation will have run. Identify which rule the court acted under **before** choosing a remedy.
4. **Ignoring section 35B.** Costs ordered on an adjournment and not paid bar further prosecution of the suit or the defence. Both sides forget this — the payer at his peril, and the opponent to his loss.
5. **Applying orally.** After 2002, an adjournment sought at a party's request needs circumstances beyond that party's control. That is a factual case and it needs an affidavit.
6. **Not counting.** Neither side usually knows how many adjournments the other has had. The party who arrives with the order sheet tabulated wins the application.
:::

::: warning Strategic timing
- **Keep a running count of adjournments** — yours and the opponent's — from the first date of hearing. It is the single most effective thing you can bring to an adjournment argument.
- **When you must seek time, do it on affidavit, offer costs, and offer an undertaking.** A specific undertaking to produce the witness on the next date without fail converts a refusal into a conditional grant.
- **When resisting, ask for the Rule 3(a) order in terms, and ask that the order record that the parties were present.** That recital is what forecloses the Order IX Rule 13 application later.
- **Always ask for costs under section 35B specifically**, and ask the court to record that payment is a condition precedent to further prosecution.
- **If your evidence is closed, file the first appeal within limitation and, if there is any doubt about the character of the decree, file the setting-aside application in parallel.** Choosing wrong is fatal; choosing both is merely expensive.
- **Bring an out-of-station witness only when you are certain the date will hold** — and if you have brought one, tell the court so at the outset, because clause (e) then works for you.
:::

## Order XVIII — hearing of the suit and examination of witnesses

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **Rule 1. Right to begin.** — The plaintiff has the right to begin unless the defendant admits the facts alleged by the plaintiff and contends that either in point of law or on some additional facts alleged by the defendant the plaintiff is not entitled to any part of the relief which he seeks, in which case the defendant has the right to begin.

> **Rule 2. Statement and production of evidence.** — (1) On the day fixed for the hearing of the suit or on any other day to which the hearing is adjourned, the party having the right to begin shall state his case and produce his evidence in support of the issues which he is bound to prove.
>
> (2) The other party shall then state his case and produce his evidence (if any) and may then address the Court generally on the whole case.
>
> (3) The party beginning may then reply generally on the whole case.

> **Rule 3. Evidence where several issues.** — Where there are several issues, the burden of proving some of which lies on the other party, the party beginning may, at his option, either produce his evidence on those issues or reserve it by way of answer to the evidence produced by the other party; and, in the latter case, the party beginning may produce evidence on those issues after the other party has produced all his evidence, and the other party may then reply specially on the evidence so produced by the party beginning; but the party beginning will then be entitled to reply generally on the whole case.

> **Rule 3A. Party to appear before other witnesses.** — Where a party himself wishes to appear as a witness, he shall so appear before any other witness on his behalf has been examined, unless the Court, for reasons to be recorded, permits him to appear as his own witness at a later stage.

> **Rule 4. Recording of evidence.** — (1) In every case, the examination-in-chief of a witness shall be on **affidavit** and copies thereof shall be supplied to the opposite party by the party who calls him for evidence:
>
> *Provided that where documents are filed and the parties rely upon the documents, the proof and admissibility of such documents which are filed along with affidavit shall be subject to the orders of the Court.*
>
> (2) The evidence (cross-examination and re-examination) of the witness in attendance, whose evidence (examination-in-chief) by affidavit has been furnished to the Court, shall be taken either by the Court or by the Commissioner appointed by it:
>
> *Provided that the Court may, while appointing a commission under this sub-rule, consider taking into account such relevant factors as it thinks fit.*
>
> (3) The Court or the Commissioner, as the case may be, shall record evidence either in writing or mechanically in the presence of the Judge or of the Commissioner, as the case may be, and where such evidence is recorded by the Commissioner he shall return such evidence together with his report in writing signed by him to the Court appointing him and the evidence taken under it shall form part of the record of the suit.
>
> (4) The Commissioner may record such remarks as it thinks material respecting the demeanour of any witness while under examination.
>
> (5) The report of the Commissioner shall be submitted to the Court appointing the commission within sixty days from the date of issue of the commission unless the Court for reasons to be recorded in writing extends the time.

> **Rule 5. How evidence shall be taken in appealable cases.** — In cases in which an appeal is allowed, the evidence of each witness shall be —
>
> *(a)* taken down in the language of the Court, —
>
> *(i)* in writing by, or in the presence and under the personal direction and superintendence of, the Judge, or
>
> *(ii)* from the dictation of the Judge directly on a typewriter; or
>
> *(b)* if the Judge, for reasons to be recorded, so directs, recorded mechanically in the language of the Court in his presence.

> **Rule 8. Memorandum when evidence not taken down by Judge.** — Where the evidence is not taken down in writing by the Judge, or from his dictation in the open Court, or recorded mechanically in his presence, he shall be bound, as the examination of each witness proceeds, to make a memorandum of the substance of what each witness deposes, and such memorandum shall be written and signed by the Judge and shall form part of the record.

> **Rule 12. Remarks on demeanour of witnesses.** — The Court may record such remarks as it thinks material respecting the demeanour of any witness while under examination.

> **Rule 17. Court may recall and examine witness.** — The Court may at any stage of a suit recall any witness who has been examined and may (subject to the law of evidence for the time being in force) put such questions to him as the Court thinks fit.

> **Rule 18. Power of Court to inspect.** — The Court may at any stage of a suit inspect any property or thing concerning which any question may arise, and where the Court inspects any property or thing it shall, as soon as may be practicable, make a memorandum of any relevant facts observed at such inspection and such memorandum shall form a part of the record of the suit.

#### Statutory mechanics

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| R. 1 — "The plaintiff **has the right to begin unless** the defendant **admits the facts**" | Rule with a single exception | The exception requires an admission of the facts **plus** a plea in avoidance |
| R. 2(1) — "**shall** state his case and produce his evidence in support of the issues which he is **bound to prove**" | Mandatory, tied to the burden | The framing of issues under O. XIV determines what must be led |
| R. 3 — "may, at his **option**, either produce his evidence on those issues or **reserve** it" | Election | Reserving preserves rebuttal but concedes the first word on those issues |
| R. 3A — "he **shall** so appear **before any other witness**" | Mandatory sequencing | Prevents the party tailoring his own evidence to his witnesses' |
| R. 3A — "unless the Court, **for reasons to be recorded**, permits" | Discretionary escape | Permission must be sought **before**, not explained afterwards |
| R. 4(1) — "the examination-in-chief of a witness **shall be on affidavit**" | Mandatory since 2002 | Chief-examination is no longer taken orally |
| R. 4(1), proviso — proof and admissibility of documents "**subject to the orders of the Court**" | Saving | Filing a document with the affidavit does not prove or exhibit it |
| R. 4(2) — cross and re-examination "**shall be taken either by the Court or by the Commissioner**" | Mandatory forum | Cross-examination remains oral and before a judicial or quasi-judicial officer |
| R. 4(5) — commissioner's report "**within sixty days**" unless extended for recorded reasons | Fixed period | Prevents commissions becoming open-ended |
| R. 5 — in appealable cases evidence "**shall** be taken down in the language of the Court" | Mandatory | The appellate record depends on it |
| R. 8 — the Judge "**shall be bound** … to make a memorandum of the substance" | Mandatory fallback | Where evidence is not recorded verbatim by or before the Judge |
| R. 12 — "The Court **may** record such remarks … respecting the **demeanour**" | Discretionary | The single thing an appellate court cannot replicate |
| R. 17 — "**at any stage** … recall any witness … and may put such questions **as the Court thinks fit**" | Court's own power | Not a party's right to fill a gap |
| R. 18 — local inspection; memorandum "**shall form a part of the record**" | Mandatory recording | Without the memorandum the inspection is unusable in appeal |

::: warning Order XVIII Rule 4 changed the trial, and Rule 12 explains what was lost
Before 2002 the examination-in-chief was oral, taken in open court, and the judge who would decide the case watched the witness give his primary account. Since 2002 the chief-examination arrives as an affidavit drafted by counsel, and the judge sees the witness for the first time under cross-examination. Rule 12's power to record remarks on demeanour — and the parallel power given to a commissioner by Rule 4(4) — is the residue of the older method. Where credibility is genuinely in issue, ask the court to record a demeanour note; an appellate court is bound to give weight to the trial judge's assessment of demeanour and can never form its own.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**(a) Who begins, and why it matters.** The plaintiff begins, because he ordinarily bears the burden on the issues that must be proved for him to succeed. The exception in Rule 1 is narrow and cumulative: the defendant must **admit the facts alleged by the plaintiff** and contend that, in law or on additional facts he alleges, the plaintiff is nevertheless not entitled to relief. That is the classic plea in confession and avoidance — admitted tenancy but a plea of discharge; admitted execution but a plea of adverse possession. The party who begins also **replies generally on the whole case**, which is a real forensic advantage: the last word.

**(b) Rule 3 and the tactics of reserving evidence.** Where some issues carry the defendant's burden, the plaintiff may either lead his evidence on them now or reserve it as an answer to whatever the defendant leads. Reserving lets you meet the defendant's case rather than guess at it, but it means you do not put your version on those issues first, and courts sometimes read a reservation as a concession that you have nothing affirmative to say. Reserve where the defendant's plea is a positive case you cannot anticipate — forgery, a family arrangement, an oral agreement — and lead where you have documents that answer it in advance.

**(c) Rule 3A is a trap for the unwary and a weapon against the careless.** A party who intends to be a witness **must** be examined before any other witness on his side, unless the court permits otherwise for reasons recorded. The purpose is to stop a party from hearing his own witnesses and then shaping his account. The permission must be sought **in advance**; a party who examines two witnesses and then seeks to enter the box himself is asking the court to record reasons after the mischief the rule guards against has already occurred, and courts frequently refuse.

**(d) The affidavit of examination-in-chief is a pleading of evidence, and it binds.** Because the chief-examination is settled in chambers, it must be drafted with the issues in front of you. Three disciplines follow. It must cover **every** issue on which that witness's evidence is needed — nothing can be added orally later. It must confine itself to facts within the witness's personal knowledge, since Rule 4 does not license hearsay or argument. And it must formally identify and tender each document the witness proves, because the proviso to Rule 4(1) makes plain that filing a document with the affidavit neither proves it nor exhibits it.

**(e) Cross-examination is still oral, and still before a judicial officer.** Rule 4(2) requires cross and re-examination to be taken by the court or by a commissioner it appoints. The commissioner route is common in busy courts and in commercial matters; it is faster and can sit outside court hours, but it costs money and, crucially, it means the **judge does not see the witness**. Where credibility is the whole case, resist a commission and ask that the cross be taken by the court, or at least insist on demeanour remarks under Rule 4(4).

**(f) Objections during evidence.** Two kinds recur. Objections to **questions** — leading questions in chief, questions not arising from the pleadings, questions going only to credit that are barred — must be taken as the question is put and recorded. Objections to **documents** follow the Order XIII scheme discussed above: inadmissibility survives to appeal, mode of proof does not. Where admissibility is contested, ask for the *Bipin Panchal* course: mark subject to objection, decide in the judgment.

**(g) Recall under Rule 17 is the court's power, not a party's remedy.** The rule empowers the **court** to recall a witness and put such questions as it thinks fit. It is not a route for a party to repair an omission in his own cross-examination. A party seeking recall must ordinarily invoke the court's inherent power under section 151, and must show that the omission was not the result of negligence and that no prejudice will result. Applications to recall are viewed sceptically, because the obvious risk is a witness coached in the interval.

**(h) Local inspection under Rule 18.** In boundary disputes, easement suits, nuisance actions and encroachment cases, the court may inspect the property itself. What makes the inspection usable is the **memorandum**: Rule 18 requires the court to record the relevant facts observed, and that memorandum forms part of the record. An inspection without a memorandum is invisible to the appellate court and cannot support a finding. Distinguish this from a **commission for local investigation** under Order XXVI Rule 9, where a commissioner — usually with a surveyor — inspects, measures and reports.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

::: tip Illustration 1 — the defendant claims the right to begin
An eviction suit. The written statement admits the tenancy, admits the rate of rent, admits the notice and its service, and pleads only that the landlord's requirement is not bona fide because he owns two other vacant shops in the same building.

**Sword (defendant).** He claims the right to begin under Rule 1: he has admitted the facts alleged by the plaintiff and contends that on additional facts alleged by him — the two vacant shops — the plaintiff is not entitled to relief. If the claim succeeds he leads first, and more importantly he gets the general reply on the whole case, the last word before judgment.

**Shield (plaintiff).** He resists by pointing to what remains unadmitted. If the plaint pleads the specific requirement — that the landlord's son needs the shop for a named business — and that averment is denied, then the facts alleged by the plaintiff are **not** admitted, the condition in Rule 1 fails, and the plaintiff begins. The argument turns entirely on a paragraph-by-paragraph reading of the written statement, which is why an evasive denial that admits too much is dangerous, and a total admission coupled with a pure plea in avoidance is a genuine tactical option.
:::

::: tip Illustration 2 — the Rule 3A objection that closes a case
A suit for specific performance. The plaintiff files affidavits of chief-examination for the scribe of the agreement and for an attesting witness, and examines both. Only afterwards does he file his own affidavit and seek to enter the witness box.

**Sword (defendant).** He objects under Rule 3A: the plaintiff was bound to appear before any other witness on his behalf was examined, and no permission was sought or granted in advance. He submits that the sequence is not a formality but a safeguard, that the plaintiff has now heard his own witnesses cross-examined and knows exactly where the difficulties lie, and that permitting him to depose now defeats the object of the rule. In the alternative, if permission is granted, he asks that the reasons be recorded and that the circumstance be available as a comment on the weight of the plaintiff's evidence.

**Shield (plaintiff).** He applies at once for permission under the proviso, offering an explanation on affidavit — that he was hospitalised on the dates the other witnesses were examined, annexing the discharge summary. Where the explanation is genuine and documented, permission is usually granted; where it is offered from the bar without material, it is often refused, and a plaintiff in a specific performance suit who cannot depose to his own readiness and willingness will lose.
:::

::: tip Illustration 3 — the affidavit that forgot an issue
A money suit with an issue on limitation, the plaintiff relying on an acknowledgment of liability in a letter of 2 March 2021. The plaintiff's affidavit of chief-examination proves the invoices and the non-payment in detail but says nothing about the letter of 2 March.

**Sword (defendant).** He does not object to the affidavit. He cross-examines on the invoices, does not touch the letter, and at final arguments submits that there is no evidence proving the acknowledgment — the letter was filed but never proved or exhibited through any witness — and that the suit is therefore barred by limitation. Under the proviso to Rule 4(1), filing a document with the affidavit does not prove it.

**Shield (plaintiff).** The moment he realises the gap he must act, and the choice of vehicle matters. He applies to file a **supplementary affidavit of examination-in-chief** confined to the letter of 2 March, offering the defendant a fresh opportunity to cross-examine on it and offering costs. That is far more likely to succeed than an application to recall the witness under Rule 17, which is the court's power, or a reopening application under section 151 filed after arguments have begun. The lesson is upstream: draft the affidavit against the list of issues, issue by issue.
:::

::: info The three ways Order XVIII Rule 4 can go wrong for you
**One — the affidavit omits an issue.** The affidavit of examination-in-chief *is* the chief examination. Anything not deposed to in it is simply not in evidence, and you cannot repair it in re-examination, which is confined to matters arising out of cross. Draft the affidavit issue by issue with the framed issues open in front of you.
**Two — the affidavit deposes to matters not pleaded.** Evidence beyond the pleadings is to be ignored, however cogent. The affidavit must live inside the four corners of the plaint or written statement, and if it does not, the answer is an amendment under Order VI Rule 17 first — which after the 2002 proviso is very hard to obtain once trial has commenced.
**Three — the affidavit is not accompanied by the documents.** Order XIII Rule 1 requires the originals to be produced at or before the settlement of issues, and Order VII Rule 14(3) / Order VIII Rule 1A(3) require leave for documents not filed with the pleading. An affidavit that refers to a document not on record is an affidavit that refers to nothing.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence — Order XVIII

**Salem Advocate Bar Association, Tamil Nadu v. Union of India (II), (2005) 6 SCC 344 — 3-Judge Bench**

*Facts:* A comprehensive challenge to the 1999 and 2002 amendments, including the newly inserted Order XVIII Rule 4 permitting examination-in-chief on affidavit.

**Ratio decidendi:** Order XVIII Rule 4 is valid. Examination-in-chief may be given on affidavit, but cross-examination and re-examination must be taken **either by the court itself or by a commissioner appointed by it** — the court cannot delegate the recording to a person who is not answerable to it, and it retains the duty to record objections and to note demeanour. Where a commissioner records the evidence, the court must still apply its own mind to the record before deciding.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Under Order XVIII Rule 4 as construed in *Salem Advocate (II)*, my client's chief examination stands on affidavit, but this Court alone — or a commissioner appointed by it — may record the cross-examination; the opposite party cannot insist on any other mode."
:::

**Ameer Trading Corporation Ltd. v. Shapoorji Data Processing Ltd., (2004) 1 SCC 702 — 2-Judge Bench**

*Facts:* Objection that permitting examination-in-chief by affidavit denied the opposite party the safeguards of oral chief examination.

**Ratio decidendi:** The examination-in-chief of a witness may be tendered on affidavit; there is no vested right to have the chief examination given orally. The substantive right is the right to **cross-examine**, which remains wholly intact, and the affidavit acquires the character of evidence only when the deponent presents himself for cross-examination.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"*Ameer Trading* settles that an affidavit of chief examination is competent; the protection lies not in the mode of chief examination but in the right to cross-examine, which my client has exercised in full."
:::

**Vadiraj Naggappa Vernekar (dead) through LRs v. Sharadchandra Prabhakar Gogate, (2009) 4 SCC 410 — 2-Judge Bench**

*Facts:* A party applied under Order XVIII Rule 17 to recall a witness after the evidence had closed, in substance to bring on record material it had omitted.

**Ratio decidendi:** Order XVIII Rule 17 confers a power on the **court**, to be exercised where the court needs a clarification on a point that has arisen in the evidence. It is not a right in a party, and it cannot be used to fill a lacuna in the party's own case or to lead fresh evidence after the evidence has closed. Rule 17A, which had permitted production of evidence not previously known, stands omitted since 1999.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"An application under Order XVIII Rule 17 is maintainable only to enable this Court to clarify a point; *Vadiraj Naggappa Vernekar* forbids its use to supply a deficiency in the applicant's own evidence."
:::

**Bagai Construction through its Proprietor Lalit Bagai v. Gupta Building Material Store, (2013) 14 SCC 1 — 2-Judge Bench**

*Facts:* After both sides had closed evidence and arguments were substantially advanced, the plaintiff sought to reopen its evidence and lead further material.

**Ratio decidendi:** A court may, in a proper case, permit evidence to be reopened, but not where the application is made after arguments have commenced and the object is to fill up gaps exposed during argument. Sufficient reasons must exist, they must be recorded, and the discretion is not to be exercised so as to defeat the 2002 amendments aimed at expedition.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The application to reopen evidence has been filed after arguments began and, on *Bagai Construction*, is a device to fill a gap that argument exposed; it must be dismissed."
:::

**Mange Ram v. Brij Mohan, (1983) 4 SCC 36 — 2-Judge Bench**

*Facts:* A party's witnesses were not produced; the question was the consequence of a failure to take steps to secure their attendance.

**Ratio decidendi:** A party who has been given an opportunity to produce evidence and who fails to take the steps the Code prescribes cannot complain that the trial proceeded; but the court must record what opportunity was in fact given, and closure of evidence is a judicial act requiring application of mind, not an automatic consequence of one default.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"*Mange Ram* requires this Court, before closing my client's evidence, to record what opportunity was actually afforded; no such opportunity appears in the order sheet of the preceding three dates."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Order XVIII Rule 17A (production of evidence not previously known) was **omitted by Act 46 of 1999 with effect from 1 July 2002** — decisions that rely on it are of historical interest only, and the correct present vehicle is section 151 read with Rule 17. Section 165 of the Evidence Act 1872 (the Judge's power to put questions) is now **section 168 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023**, and section 114(g) is now **section 119(g)**. In suits of a Specified Value under the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, the substituted Order XVIII Rule 2(3A)–(3E) and the substituted Order XI regime apply, with written arguments and time-bound oral submissions; check the Schedule before assuming the unamended Rule applies.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit — Order XVIII

::: danger Six ways the evidence stage is lost
1. **The affidavit of chief examination does not track the issues.** Every framed issue on which your client bears the burden must be deposed to in terms. A missing issue is a finding against you, and no amount of documentary material will cure it.
2. **The affidavit travels beyond the pleadings.** Evidence outside the pleadings must be disregarded. Amend first under Order VI Rule 17 — remembering that after trial has commenced the proviso requires you to show that despite due diligence the matter could not have been raised earlier — or draft within what you have pleaded.
3. **The party is examined after his own witnesses without leave.** Order XVIII Rule 3A is peremptory in form. If your client must be examined later — because he is abroad, ill, or awaiting a document — apply for leave with reasons **before** the first witness enters the box, and get the leave recorded.
4. **Rebuttal evidence is led without having reserved the right.** Order XVIII Rule 3 requires the reservation to be made when you begin. If you lead evidence generally on an issue where the burden lay on the other side, you have exhausted your innings on it.
5. **Documents are tendered but never exhibited.** A document referred to in an affidavit is not in evidence until it is tendered through a witness and marked. Keep an exhibit register and reconcile it against your list of documents before closing evidence.
6. **Objections are not recorded.** Following *Bipin Shantilal Panchal*, the practice is to mark subject to objection and decide admissibility at judgment — but the objection must appear on the deposition. An objection taken orally and not recorded is an objection abandoned, and it cannot be revived in appeal.
:::

::: warning Strategic timing
**Before the first date of evidence:** file the affidavit of chief examination, the list of witnesses under Order XVI Rule 1 (within fifteen days of settlement of issues), the process fee and witness expenses under Order XVI Rule 2, and any Order XVIII Rule 3A leave application.
**At the commencement of trial:** if the defendant admits the plaintiff's facts, claim the right to begin under the proviso to Rule 1 — the tactical value is that your client is heard last in reply under Rule 2(2).
**When you begin:** if any issue carries a burden on the other side, reserve the right to lead evidence in rebuttal on it under Rule 3, expressly and on the record.
**During cross-examination:** put your case to every witness on every point on which you will later ask for a finding. A point not put is a point conceded.
**Before evidence is closed:** reconcile the affidavits against the issues, and the exhibits against the list of documents. Anything you discover afterwards must come through section 151 (Rule 17A being omitted) or Order XLI Rule 27, both of which are difficult.
**After arguments have begun:** on *Bagai Construction*, an application to reopen is presumptively an attempt to fill a gap. Do not leave it this late.
:::

## Order XIX — Affidavits

Order XIX is three rules long and is almost always misunderstood. It does not make affidavits evidence. It gives the court a power, in a defined situation, to order that a **particular point** be proved by affidavit rather than by oral testimony — subject to a proviso that protects the other side's right to cross-examine. Everything else about affidavits in a civil suit is governed by Rule 3, which draws the single most important distinction in interlocutory practice: an affidavit in support of a final adjudication must be confined to facts within the deponent's personal knowledge, while an affidavit on an **interlocutory application** may state matters of belief, provided the grounds of that belief are disclosed.

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics — Order XIX

> **Rule 1. Power to order any point to be proved by affidavit.** — Any Court may at any time for sufficient reason order that any particular fact or facts may be proved by affidavit, or that the affidavit of any witness may be read at the hearing, on such conditions as the Court thinks reasonable:
>
> Provided that where it appears to the Court that either party *bona fide* desires the production of a witness for cross-examination, and that such witness can be produced, an order shall not be made authorising the evidence of such witness to be given by affidavit.

> **Rule 2. Power to order attendance of deponent for cross-examination.** — (1) Upon any application evidence may be given by affidavit, but the Court may, at the instance of either party, order the attendance for cross-examination of the deponent.
>
> (2) Such attendance shall be in Court, unless the deponent is exempted from personal appearance in Court, or the Court otherwise directs.

> **Rule 3. Matters to which affidavits shall be confined.** — (1) Affidavits shall be confined to such facts as the deponent is able of his own knowledge to prove, except on interlocutory applications, on which statements of his belief may be admitted: provided that the grounds thereof are stated.
>
> (2) The costs of every affidavit which shall unnecessarily set forth matters of hearsay or argumentative matter, or copies of or extracts from documents, shall (unless the Court otherwise directs) be paid by the party filing the same.

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "Any Court **may** at any time for sufficient reason order" (R. 1) | Discretionary, but conditioned | The power exists at every stage, but "sufficient reason" must be recorded; a routine order under R. 1 is liable to be set aside |
| "that any **particular fact or facts** may be proved by affidavit" (R. 1) | Limiting | The order must identify the fact. A blanket direction that the whole case be proved on affidavit is outside Rule 1 — that result is reached, if at all, through Order XVIII Rule 4 |
| "or that the affidavit of any witness may be **read at the hearing**" (R. 1) | Enabling | Converts an affidavit into material the court may act on — absent such an order (or Order XVIII R. 4), an affidavit is not evidence |
| "on such **conditions** as the Court thinks reasonable" (R. 1) | Enabling | The court may, for instance, permit the affidavit but direct the deponent to be available for cross on a fixed date |
| "Provided that … *bona fide* desires the production of a witness for cross-examination … an order **shall not** be made" (R. 1 proviso) | **Mandatory prohibition** | This is the heart of Rule 1. Two conditions — the desire must be *bona fide*, and the witness must be capable of being produced. If both are satisfied, the discretion is extinguished |
| "and that such witness **can be produced**" (R. 1 proviso) | Condition precedent to the prohibition | Where the witness is dead, abroad, or genuinely unavailable, the proviso does not bite and the affidavit may be ordered to be read |
| "Upon any **application** evidence may be given by affidavit" (R. 2(1)) | Permissive, general | This is the ordinary interlocutory practice — injunction, attachment, restoration, stay applications are all decided on affidavit |
| "the Court **may**, at the instance of either party, order the attendance for cross-examination" (R. 2(1)) | Discretionary | The right to cross-examine on an interlocutory affidavit is not automatic; the applicant must show why the disputed fact cannot be resolved on the papers |
| "Such attendance **shall** be in Court" (R. 2(2)) | Mandatory, subject to exceptions | Cross-examination on an affidavit is taken in open court unless the deponent is exempted or the court directs otherwise |
| "Affidavits **shall** be confined to such facts as the deponent is able of his **own knowledge** to prove" (R. 3(1)) | **Mandatory** | The default rule. An affidavit on personal knowledge is the only competent form where the affidavit is to serve as proof |
| "**except on interlocutory applications**, on which statements of his **belief** may be admitted" (R. 3(1)) | Exception | The practical working rule of every injunction and stay application |
| "**provided that the grounds thereof are stated**" (R. 3(1) proviso) | **Condition precedent to the exception** | A belief without disclosed grounds is worthless. This is the single most common defect in interlocutory affidavits and the easiest attack available to the respondent |
| "The **costs** of every affidavit which shall unnecessarily set forth matters of hearsay or argumentative matter … shall … be paid by the party filing" (R. 3(2)) | Mandatory default, displaceable | A costs sanction for prolix, argumentative affidavits. Rarely invoked, always available |

::: warning An affidavit is not evidence unless the Code makes it so
This is the proposition that decides more appeals than any other under Order XIX. Section 3 of the Evidence Act 1872 (now section 2(1)(e) of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023) defines "evidence" as statements made **before the court** by witnesses, and documents produced for inspection. An affidavit is neither. It becomes material the court may act on in exactly three situations:
1. the court has ordered under **Order XIX Rule 1** that a particular fact be proved by affidavit, or that a particular affidavit be read at the hearing;
2. the affidavit is filed on an **interlocutory application**, where Rule 2(1) permits evidence to be given by affidavit; or
3. it is an affidavit of **examination-in-chief under Order XVIII Rule 4**, in which case it acquires the character of evidence when the deponent tenders himself for cross-examination.

Outside those three situations, an affidavit filed in a suit — however detailed, however sworn — proves nothing at the trial. Practitioners lose cases by assuming the reply affidavit filed at the injunction stage is on the record at trial. It is not.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification — Order XIX

**(a) Rule 1 is a case-management power, not a mode of proof.** The court is not choosing between affidavit and oral evidence as equivalents. It is deciding that a discrete fact — the date a notice was despatched, the identity of a signatory, the chain of custody of a register — is not seriously in dispute and does not merit a witness being brought from another State. The order must therefore identify the fact and record the reason. Where the entire case is to go on affidavit, the vehicle is Order XVIII Rule 4, not Order XIX Rule 1.

**(b) The proviso is the safeguard, and it has two limbs.** The court is prohibited from ordering evidence by affidavit where (i) a party *bona fide* desires production of the witness for cross-examination, **and** (ii) the witness can be produced. Both must be satisfied. A party who says "I want to cross-examine" without identifying anything he proposes to challenge is not acting *bona fide*, and the court may say so. Conversely, where the witness is genuinely capable of attending and the opposite party has a real challenge to put, the court has no discretion left — "shall not be made" means what it says.

**(c) Rule 2 is the engine of all interlocutory practice.** Every temporary injunction, every attachment before judgment, every application to set aside an *ex parte* decree, every stay application is decided on affidavits. The court is not conducting a trial; it is forming a *prima facie* view. Rule 2 makes that constitutionally comfortable by preserving the power to call the deponent for cross-examination where the court cannot safely proceed on the papers. That power is used sparingly — but where the interlocutory order will effectively decide the suit (a mandatory injunction, a receiver over a running business), an application under Rule 2(1) is a serious weapon.

**(d) Personal knowledge versus belief — and why the grounds matter.** Rule 3(1) sets the standard by reference to what the deponent "is able of his own knowledge to prove". At the interlocutory stage this is relaxed to belief. But belief is only admissible if the **grounds** are stated. "I believe the defendant is about to alienate the property" is inadmissible. "I believe the defendant is about to alienate the property, my belief being founded on the newspaper advertisement dated 12 March 2026 annexed as Annexure A-3 offering the suit land for sale, and on the statement made to me by the tenant Shri Ram Lal on 14 March 2026 that he had been shown the premises by a prospective purchaser" is admissible, and is the difference between an injunction and a dismissal.

**(e) The verification clause is where affidavits die.** The body of the affidavit and the verification must correspond. Paragraphs deposed to as true to personal knowledge must be facts the deponent actually witnessed; paragraphs on information must identify the source; paragraphs on legal advice must say so. A verification which recites that "paragraphs 1 to 24 are true to my knowledge" over an affidavit in which paragraph 17 recounts what a third party said is a false verification, and courts have refused relief on that ground alone.

**(f) Affidavits by a constituted attorney or an officer.** A power-of-attorney holder may depose to acts done by him under the power; he may not depose to the personal knowledge, state of mind or intention of the principal, because those are facts he cannot of his own knowledge prove. In a suit for specific performance, where readiness and willingness is a state of mind of the plaintiff, an affidavit by an attorney holder who was not privy to the transaction is worth nothing. The same logic applies to a company officer deposing to matters that occurred before he joined.

**(g) Argumentative and hearsay affidavits attract a costs order.** Rule 3(2) is a rarely used but entirely live provision. An affidavit that reproduces the plaint, annexes forty documents already on the record, and argues the law across nine pages is exactly what the sub-rule contemplates. When resisting such an affidavit, ask for the Rule 3(2) costs order in terms; even where it is not granted, the request focuses the court on the affidavit's defects.

**(h) Order XIX and Order XVIII Rule 4 are not the same thing.** Rule 1 of Order XIX predates the 2002 reform and survives it. It applies to any court, at any time, to particular facts, on sufficient reason recorded. Order XVIII Rule 4 applies at trial, to the examination-in-chief of every witness, as a matter of course, without any need for an order. Where you want a *specific* fact taken as proved on affidavit without calling a witness at all — a formal proof, a certificate, a routine business record — Order XIX Rule 1 is the correct application, and it is the one nobody makes.

**(i) A false affidavit is a criminal offence, and the court can act.** An affidavit is sworn. A false statement in it attracts sections 191 and 193 of the Indian Penal Code, now **sections 227 and 229 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023**, and the procedural route is a complaint by the court under section 340 of the CrPC, now **section 379 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023**. Courts require the additional element that a prosecution be **expedient in the interests of justice** — a wrong statement is not enough; deliberate falsehood on a material point going to the root of the proceeding is.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations — Order XIX

::: tip Illustration 1 — Formal proof of a bank's dispatch record
A suit on a guarantee turns partly on whether a demand notice was posted on 3 April 2024. The bank's despatch register is maintained at a regional office 700 kilometres from the court, and the clerk who made the entry has since been transferred twice.

**Sword (Plaintiff bank):** Apply under Order XIX Rule 1 for an order that the fact of despatch be proved by the affidavit of the present custodian of the register, exhibiting a certified extract, and simultaneously invoke the Bankers' Books Evidence Act, 1891, under which a certified copy of an entry is admissible without producing the book. The application must identify the fact — despatch on 3 April 2024 — and give the reason: distance, the routine nature of the entry, and the absence of any pleaded denial.

**Shield (Defendant guarantor):** Invoke the proviso. State on affidavit precisely what you propose to put in cross — that the register shows an interpolation, that the serial numbering is out of sequence, that the postal receipt bears a different date — and confirm that the custodian is willing and able to attend. If the challenge is specific and the witness is producible, the court "shall not" make the Rule 1 order. A general assertion that you "wish to cross-examine" will not do; *bona fides* must appear from the material.
:::

::: tip Illustration 2 — The injunction affidavit that states belief without grounds
A plaintiff in a suit for specific performance of an agreement to sell obtains an *ex parte* temporary injunction on an affidavit stating that "the defendant is attempting to create third-party rights in the suit property and will do so unless restrained".

**Sword (Plaintiff):** Understand the exposure and cure it before the returnable date. File a supplementary affidavit disclosing the grounds — the specific advertisement, the specific enquiry received from a broker, the specific visit by a surveyor, each with a date and a document. Under Rule 3(1) the belief is admissible only if the grounds are stated, and the *ex parte* order was obtained on an affidavit that stated none.

**Shield (Defendant):** Attack on two fronts at the first hearing. First, under Order XIX Rule 3(1), the operative paragraph is a bare statement of belief with no disclosed grounds and is therefore inadmissible even at the interlocutory stage — the injunction rests on nothing. Second, under Order XXXIX Rule 4 read with the proviso, an *ex parte* injunction obtained on a knowingly false or misleading affidavit **shall** be vacated, and the suppression of the fact that the plaintiff himself had earlier offered the property for sale is exactly such a misstatement. Ask, in terms, for the Rule 3(2) costs order.
:::

::: tip Illustration 3 — Cross-examining a deponent on a receivership application
In a partnership dissolution suit, one partner applies for the appointment of a receiver over a running hotel, alleging on affidavit that the managing partner has diverted ₹1.4 crore of receipts to a personal account. The managing partner denies it on affidavit. The application, if allowed, will effectively decide the commercial fate of the business years before the suit is tried.

**Sword (Applicant partner):** Apply under Order XIX Rule 2(1) for the attendance of the managing partner for cross-examination on his reply affidavit. The ground is that the affidavits are irreconcilable on a single, narrow, documentarily testable fact — the destination of identified receipts — and that the consequences of the application are effectively final. Confine the application to that fact; a request to cross-examine at large will be refused.

**Shield (Managing partner):** Resist the Rule 2 application on the footing that an interlocutory application is not a trial, that the court can form a *prima facie* view on the bank statements already on record, and that cross-examination now would prejudge the very issue framed for trial. Offer, in the alternative, to file a further affidavit annexing the complete bank statements — which meets the court's concern without putting your client in the box a year before he is due there.
:::

::: info The affidavit is stamped, not court-fee-bearing
An affidavit attracts **stamp duty** under Article 4 of Schedule I to the Indian Stamp Act, 1899, as adapted by the State schedule — a small fixed sum — together with the oath or notarial fee payable to the officer administering it. It does **not** attract *ad valorem* court fee, because it is not a plaint, memorandum of appeal, or application for a substantive relief valued in money. The interlocutory application it supports carries the fixed fee prescribed by the State Court Fees Act or the High Court Rules. An unstamped affidavit is curable — the court will direct the deficiency to be made good — but an affidavit sworn before an officer not authorised under section 139 CPC read with the Oaths Act, 1969, is a nullity and must be re-sworn.

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence — Order XIX

**Sudha Devi v. M.P. Narayanan, (1988) 3 SCC 366 — 2-Judge Bench**

*Facts:* A party sought to rely on affidavits filed in the proceedings as proof of the facts stated in them, no order under Order XIX having been made and the deponents not having been examined.

**Ratio decidendi:** Affidavits are **not** "evidence" within the meaning of section 3 of the Evidence Act, and cannot be relied on as proof of the facts they state unless the court, for sufficient reason, passes an order under Order XIX Rule 1 or Rule 2 that they be so read. A court that decides a contested question of fact on the strength of untested affidavits acts without evidence.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The finding under challenge rests wholly on the reply affidavit filed at the injunction stage; on *Sudha Devi*, that affidavit is not evidence, no order under Order XIX having been passed, and the finding is therefore a finding without evidence."
:::

**Ayaaubkhan Noorkhan Pathan v. State of Maharashtra, (2013) 4 SCC 465 — 2-Judge Bench**

*Facts:* An adverse order was founded on statements of persons whom the affected party had never been permitted to cross-examine.

**Ratio decidendi:** The right to cross-examine is an essential ingredient of a fair hearing wherever a statement is used against a party to found an adverse conclusion. A statement recorded behind the back of a party, and not tested in cross-examination, cannot be treated as material on which a finding may rest, and the value of an affidavit untested by cross-examination is correspondingly slight.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"My client *bona fide* seeks the production of this deponent, and on *Ayaaubkhan Noorkhan Pathan* the untested affidavit cannot found a finding against him; the proviso to Order XIX Rule 1 accordingly bars the order sought."
:::

**Barium Chemicals Ltd. v. Company Law Board, AIR 1967 SC 295 — 5-Judge Bench**

*Facts:* An administrative order was defended on an affidavit which asserted the existence of the requisite state of satisfaction without disclosing the material on which it rested.

**Ratio decidendi:** An affidavit which asserts a belief or an opinion without disclosing the grounds and the material on which it is founded is of no assistance to the court. Where the existence of circumstances is a condition precedent, the affidavit must set out those circumstances; a bare assertion in the language of the provision is not a substitute for the disclosure the law requires.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The affidavit asserts an apprehension of alienation in the very language of Order XXXIX Rule 1 without disclosing a single ground; on the *Barium Chemicals* principle, reflected in the proviso to Order XIX Rule 3(1), it discloses no material on which this Court can act."
:::

**Smt. Savitramma v. H.G. Narayana Rao, 1988 SCC OnLine Kar 118 / and the settled line on attorney-holder affidavits, affirmed in Janki Vashdeo Bhojwani v. Indusind Bank Ltd., (2005) 2 SCC 217 — 2-Judge Bench**

*Facts:* A suit in which readiness and willingness — a state of mind personal to the plaintiff — was sought to be proved through the deposition and affidavit of a power-of-attorney holder who had no personal knowledge of the transaction.

**Ratio decidendi:** A power-of-attorney holder may depose about acts **done by him** in exercise of the power. He cannot depose to facts of which only the principal can have personal knowledge, nor to the principal's state of mind. Order XIX Rule 3(1) confines an affidavit to what the deponent can of his own knowledge prove, and an attorney-holder's affidavit on the principal's readiness and willingness fails that test.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"On *Janki Vashdeo Bhojwani*, the attorney-holder cannot depose to the plaintiff's readiness and willingness; the affidavit offends Order XIX Rule 3(1) and the issue must be decided against the plaintiff for want of competent evidence."
:::

**A.K.K. Nambiar v. Union of India, (1969) 3 SCC 864 — 3-Judge Bench**

*Facts:* Affidavits filed by both sides were neither properly verified nor did they disclose the source of the statements made.

**Ratio decidendi:** Affidavits which are not verified in accordance with the Rules, and which do not disclose whether the statements are made on personal knowledge or on information — and if on information, its source — are of **no value**, and the court is entitled to disregard them altogether. The verification is not a formality; it is what makes the affidavit usable.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The verification recites that the whole affidavit is true to personal knowledge, though paragraphs 9 and 14 plainly recount what third parties are said to have stated; on *A.K.K. Nambiar* this affidavit is of no value and must be disregarded."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Section 3 of the Evidence Act, 1872 (the definition of "evidence" on which *Sudha Devi* turns) is now **section 2(1)(e) of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023**; the substance is unchanged, but cite both. Sections 191 and 193 IPC are now **sections 227 and 229 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023**, and section 340 CrPC is now **section 379 BNSS 2023**. Affidavits in commercial suits are additionally governed by the **Statement of Truth** prescribed by the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 (substituted Order VI Rule 15A and the Appendix), which has its own form and its own consequences for a false statement — do not file a conventional verification in a Specified Value suit. Several High Courts prescribe their own affidavit forms and identification requirements under section 139 CPC; check the Original Side or Civil Rules of Practice of the forum before swearing.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit — Order XIX

::: danger Six ways an affidavit fails
1. **Belief without grounds.** Rule 3(1) admits belief on interlocutory applications *only* if the grounds are stated. Every operative paragraph that begins "I believe" or "I apprehend" must be followed by the material — a document, a dated conversation with an identified person, an inspection — on which the belief rests.
2. **A verification that does not match the body.** Split the verification: paragraphs on personal knowledge, paragraphs on information from a named source, paragraphs on legal advice, paragraphs on the record. A blanket "all true to my knowledge" over an affidavit containing hearsay is a false verification and, on *A.K.K. Nambiar*, deprives the affidavit of value.
3. **An attorney-holder deposing to the principal's state of mind.** Readiness and willingness, knowledge, intention, *bona fides* — none of these can be proved by an attorney who was not there. Get the principal's own affidavit, or accept that the issue is lost.
4. **Treating the interlocutory affidavit as trial evidence.** Nothing filed at the injunction stage is on the record at trial unless it is re-tendered as an affidavit of examination-in-chief under Order XVIII Rule 4, or an order is obtained under Order XIX Rule 1. Practitioners routinely argue final relief on interlocutory affidavits and are met with *Sudha Devi*.
5. **Suppression.** An *ex parte* order obtained on an affidavit that conceals a material fact attracts the proviso to Order XXXIX Rule 4 — the order **shall** be vacated — and exposes the deponent to a complaint under section 379 BNSS. The test is materiality, not centrality; if the fact would have affected the court's discretion, it had to be disclosed.
6. **Prolixity.** Rule 3(2) makes the party filing an argumentative or hearsay-laden affidavit pay its costs. An affidavit is not a written argument. Facts, dates, documents, grounds of belief — and nothing else.
:::

::: warning Strategic timing
**Before drafting any interlocutory affidavit:** list the facts you must establish for the relief sought, then ask of each one whether the deponent knows it personally. Where he does not, either find a deponent who does or plead the belief with its grounds. This exercise takes twenty minutes and saves the application.
**When filing an application under Order XIX Rule 1:** file it early, before the witness list under Order XVI Rule 1 is due, so that if the application fails you can still summon the witness as of right.
**When resisting a Rule 1 application:** file the counter-affidavit *specifying the cross-examination*. The proviso requires your desire to be *bona fide*, and *bona fides* is demonstrated by particulars, not by assertion.
**When seeking cross-examination under Rule 2(1):** move promptly and confine the request to one or two decisive facts. An application to cross-examine at large invites the answer that you are seeking a trial within an application.
**Before the final hearing:** audit the record. Every fact you intend to rely on in argument must be traceable either to an affidavit of examination-in-chief under Order XVIII Rule 4, to oral deposition, to an exhibited document, or to an admission. Anything traceable only to an interlocutory affidavit is not there.
:::

## Order XX — Judgment and Decree

Order XX is where the trial becomes an enforceable instrument. It contains four ideas that decide appeals and destroy executions: judgment must be pronounced within a fixed outer time; judgment must contain the points for determination, the decision on each, and the **reasons**; there must be a finding on **each separate issue**; and the decree must **agree with the judgment** and state with precision the relief granted. Almost every execution that fails, fails because of a decree drawn carelessly under Rule 6 — a decree for possession that does not describe the property by boundaries, a money decree silent on interest after the date of the decree, a partition decree that declares shares but does not direct partition by metes and bounds.

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics — Order XX

> **Rule 1. Judgment when pronounced.** — (1) The Court, after the case has been heard, shall pronounce judgment in an open Court, either at once, or as soon thereafter as may be practicable and when the judgment is to be pronounced on some future day, the Court shall fix a day for that purpose of which due notice shall be given to the parties or their pleaders:
>
> Provided that where the judgment is not pronounced at once, every endeavour shall be made by the Court to pronounce the judgment within thirty days from the date on which the hearing of the case was concluded but, where it is not practicable so to do on the ground of the exceptional and extraordinary circumstances of the case, the Court shall fix a future day for the pronouncement of the judgment, and such day shall not ordinarily be a day beyond sixty days from the date on which the hearing of the case was concluded, and due notice of the day so fixed shall be given to the parties or their pleaders.
>
> (2) Where a written judgment is to be pronounced, it shall be sufficient if the findings of the Court on each issue and the final order passed in the case are read out and it shall not be necessary for the Court to read out the whole judgment.
>
> (3) The judgment may be pronounced by dictation in open Court to a shorthand writer if the Judge is specially empowered by the High Court in this behalf.

> **Rule 2. Power to pronounce judgment written by Judge's predecessor.** — A Judge shall pronounce a judgment written and signed but not pronounced by his predecessor.

> **Rule 3. Judgment to be signed.** — The judgment shall be dated and signed by the Judge in open Court at the time of pronouncing it and, when once signed, shall not afterwards be altered or added to, save as provided by section 152 or on review.

> **Rule 4. Judgments of Small Cause Courts.** — (1) Judgments of a Court of Small Causes need not contain more than the points for determination and the decision thereon.
>
> (2) Judgments of other Courts. — Judgments of other Courts shall contain a concise statement of the case, the points for determination, the decision thereon, and the reasons for such decision.

> **Rule 5. Court to state its decision on each issue.** — In suits in which issues have been framed, the Court shall state its finding or decision, with the reasons therefor, upon each separate issue, unless the finding upon any one or more of the issues is sufficient for the decision of the suit.

> **Rule 5A. Court to inform parties as to where an appeal lies in cases where parties are not represented by pleaders.** — Except where both the parties are represented by pleaders, the Court shall, when it pronounces its judgment in a case subject to appeal, inform the parties present in Court as to the Court to which an appeal lies and the period of limitation for the filing of such appeal and place on record the information given to the parties.

> **Rule 6. Contents of decree.** — (1) The decree shall agree with the judgment; it shall contain the number of the suit, the names and descriptions of the parties, their registered addresses, and particulars of the claim, and shall specify clearly the relief granted or other determination of the suit.
>
> (2) The decree shall also state the amount of costs incurred in the suit, and by whom or out of what property and in what proportions such costs are to be paid.
>
> (3) The Court may direct that the costs payable to one party by the other shall be set off against any sum which is admitted or found to be due from the former to the latter.

> **Rule 6A. Preparation of decree.** — (1) Every endeavour shall be made to ensure that the decree is drawn up as expeditiously as possible and, in any case, within fifteen days from the date on which the judgment is pronounced.
>
> (2) An appeal may be preferred against the decree without filing a copy of the decree and in such a case the copy made available to the party by the Court shall for the purposes of Rule 1 of Order XLI be treated as the decree. But as soon as the decree is drawn, the judgment shall cease to have the effect of a decree for the purposes of execution or for any other purpose.

> **Rule 7. Date of decree.** — The decree shall bear date the day on which the judgment was pronounced, and, when the Judge has satisfied himself that the decree has been drawn up in accordance with the judgment, he shall sign the decree.

> **Rule 12. Decree for possession and mesne profits.** — (1) Where a suit is for the recovery of possession of immovable property and for rent or mesne profits, the Court may pass a decree —
> (a) for the possession of the property;
> (b) for the rent or mesne profits which have accrued on the property during a period prior to the institution of the suit or directing an inquiry as to such rent or mesne profits;
> (c) directing an inquiry as to rent or mesne profits from the institution of the suit until —
> (i) the delivery of possession to the decree-holder,
> (ii) the relinquishment of possession by the judgment-debtor with notice to the decree-holder through the Court, or
> (iii) the expiration of three years from the date of the decree, whichever event first occurs.
>
> (2) Where an inquiry is directed under clause (b) or clause (c), a final decree in respect of the rent or mesne profits shall be passed in accordance with the result of such inquiry.

> **Rule 18. Decree in suit for partition of property or separate possession of a share therein.** — Where the Court passes a decree for the partition of property or for the separate possession of a share therein, then, —
> (1) if and in so far as the decree relates to an estate assessed to the payment of revenue to the Government, the decree shall declare the rights of the several parties interested in the property, but shall direct such partition or separation to be made by the Collector, or any gazetted subordinate of the Collector deputed by him in this behalf, in accordance with such declaration and with the provisions of section 54;
> (2) if and in so far as such decree relates to any other immovable property or to movable property, the Court may, if the partition or separation cannot be conveniently made without further inquiry, pass a preliminary decree declaring the rights of the several parties interested in the property and giving such further directions as may be required.

> **Rule 20. Certified copies of judgment and decree to be furnished.** — Certified copies of the judgment and decree shall be furnished to the parties on application to the Court, and at their expense.

::: warning The decree, not the judgment, is what gets executed
Section 2(2) defines a decree as the **formal expression** of an adjudication. Order XX Rule 6 requires it to agree with the judgment and to state the relief clearly. In execution, section 47 confines the executing court to questions relating to execution, discharge or satisfaction — it **cannot go behind the decree**, cannot construe the judgment against the decree, and cannot supply a relief the decree omits. The consequences follow inexorably:
- A decree for possession that describes the property as "the suit property as described in the plaint" without reproducing the schedule and boundaries will be met with an objection that the bailiff cannot identify what to deliver.
- A money decree silent on **interest from the date of the decree to realisation** loses that interest; section 34(1) permits the court to award it, but the executing court cannot add it.
- A decree that declares a half share in a partition suit without directing partition by metes and bounds is a declaratory decree, and a separate final-decree proceeding is required.
- A decree that omits costs, or omits to say by whom they are payable, leaves the costs unexecutable under Rule 6(2).

The remedy for a genuine slip is section 152 — available at any time, with no limitation. The remedy for an omission that reflects the court's actual (if erroneous) decision is appeal, not section 152. Confusing the two is the most common error at this stage.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification — Order XX

**(a) Pronouncement in open court is what makes a judgment exist.** Until it is pronounced, a written and signed document is a draft with no legal consequence. This is why Rule 2 exists: a successor Judge may pronounce what his predecessor wrote **and signed**, because the intellectual act was complete and only the formal act remained. If the predecessor wrote but did not sign, nothing exists to pronounce, and the successor must rehear the case. The distinction is unforgiving and is worth checking whenever a judgment is delivered by a Judge who did not hear the arguments.

**(b) The thirty and sixty day limits are not decorative.** The 1976 Amendment inserted the proviso to Rule 1 after decades of judgments delivered months, sometimes years, after arguments closed. The Supreme Court in *Anil Rai* laid down a graded administrative response — after two months a party may move the Chief Justice for an early judgment; after six months, either party may apply for withdrawal of the case and reassignment. No decree is void because it was delivered late, but the delay is itself a ground of challenge where it can be shown to have caused the court to lose the thread of the evidence.

**(c) Reasons are the judgment.** Rule 4(2) requires four things — a concise statement of the case, the points for determination, the decision on each, and the reasons. This is not a formula for elegance; it is the mechanism by which the appellate court can test the decision and the losing party can understand why it lost. The requirement holds even where the defendant has filed no written statement and judgment is passed under Order VIII Rule 10: *Balraj Taneja* holds that the court must still apply its mind to the plaint, satisfy itself that the facts pleaded disclose a cause of action and entitle the plaintiff to the relief, and give reasons. A judgment that says only "suit decreed as prayed" is no judgment.

**(d) A finding on each issue — and why trial courts should never rely on the exception.** Rule 5 permits a court to stop where one finding disposes of the suit. In practice this is a trap. If the trial court dismisses a suit purely on limitation and records no finding on title, and the appellate court reverses on limitation, it must either remand under Order XLI Rule 23A — losing years — or itself decide title on a record no trial court has evaluated. Appellate courts repeatedly deprecate the practice. The correct discipline is to record findings on every framed issue and then state which finding is decisive.

**(e) *Functus officio*, and the two exceptions.** Once the judgment is signed and pronounced, the court's power over it is exhausted. Rule 3 preserves two exceptions. **Section 152** allows correction of clerical or arithmetical mistakes and errors arising from any accidental slip or omission — the test is whether the correction gives effect to what the court *intended and actually decided*, or alters that decision. Adding an omitted survey number that appears throughout the judgment is a slip; adding interest the judgment never mentioned is not. **Review under section 114 read with Order XLVII Rule 1** is available on discovery of new and important matter, on an error apparent on the face of the record, or for any other sufficient reason — and carries a thirty-day limitation under Article 124.

**(f) Preliminary and final decrees.** Several suits are decided in two stages: partition (Rule 18), accounts (Rule 16), administration (Rule 13), dissolution of partnership (Rule 15), and mortgage suits (Order XXXIV). The preliminary decree adjudicates rights — the shares, the liability to account, the amount due; the final decree works out the consequences — the metes and bounds, the sum found on accounting, the sale. Both are decrees within section 2(2), and both are separately appealable. A crucial point of practice: in a partition suit **more than one preliminary decree may be passed**, because shares can change by death, and *Phoolchand v. Gopal Lal* settles that the court retains the power to amend the preliminary decree to give effect to such changes before the final decree.

**(g) Mesne profits, and the three-year cap.** Rule 12 distinguishes three periods. Profits **before** the suit must be claimed and proved as part of the suit, and are valued for court fee. Profits **from institution to delivery of possession** may be the subject of an inquiry directed by the decree, worked out in a final decree — and critically, that inquiry is capped at **three years from the date of the decree** or delivery or relinquishment, whichever comes first. Profits **beyond** that period require a fresh suit. A decree that grants possession but forgets to direct the Rule 12 inquiry deprives the plaintiff of everything after the date of the plaint, and Order II Rule 2 may then bar a separate suit for it.

**(h) Interest — three periods, three rules.** Section 34 governs. Interest **before the suit** is a matter of contract or of substantive law and must be pleaded and proved. Interest ***pendente lite*** — from the institution to the date of the decree — is in the court's discretion. Interest **after the decree** to realisation is again discretionary, and where the decree is silent, section 34(2) deems the interest to have been refused so that a fresh suit for it is barred. The rate for post-decree interest is capped at six per cent per annum unless the liability arises out of a **commercial transaction**, in which case the contractual rate may be allowed. Every money decree must therefore say, in terms, what interest runs from the date of the decree.

**(i) Rule 6A(2) is the practitioner's safety valve.** Limitation for a first appeal is thirty days to the District Court (Article 116) or ninety days to the High Court (Article 116/117), running from the date of the decree — which by Rule 7 is the date of the judgment — with the time for obtaining copies excluded under section 12(2). Registries routinely take weeks to draw a decree. Rule 6A(2) resolves this: an appeal may be filed **without** a copy of the decree, and the copy of the judgment supplied by the court is treated as the decree for the purpose of Order XLI Rule 1. Once the decree is drawn, it takes over. Never let limitation expire waiting for a decree.

**(j) Rule 5A and the unrepresented party.** Where either party is without a pleader, the court must state in open court which court the appeal lies to and what the limitation period is, and must record that it has done so. This is a small provision with a real consequence: where the court has failed to comply and an unrepresented litigant files late, the failure is a strong circumstance in support of condonation under section 5 of the Limitation Act.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations — Order XX

::: tip Illustration 1 — The possession decree the bailiff cannot execute
A decree for possession is passed in respect of "the suit shop bearing Municipal No. 14/2, Gandhi Bazar", the plaint schedule having given boundaries. The decree reproduces only the municipal number. On execution, the judgment-debtor objects that the premises he occupies bear number 14/2-A and that 14/2 is a different shop occupied by a third party. The bailiff returns the warrant unexecuted.

**Sword (Decree-holder):** Do not fight this in the execution court, which under section 47 cannot go behind the decree. Move the **trial court** under section 152 to correct the decree by incorporating the schedule with boundaries as set out in the plaint and as found in paragraph 19 of the judgment. Section 152 has no limitation and the correction here plainly gives effect to what the court decided rather than altering it — the judgment itself identifies the property by boundaries. Once corrected, the decree relates back to its date under Rule 7 and Article 136 is unaffected.

**Shield (Judgment-debtor):** Resist on the footing that the identity of the property was a **contested issue** on which evidence was led, that the court's omission was a deliberate limitation of the relief, and that what is sought is not a slip but an enlargement of the decree — which lies only in appeal. Where the judgment itself is ambiguous on identity, this argument has real force; where the judgment sets out the boundaries, it does not.
:::

::: tip Illustration 2 — Partition decree without a Collector's direction
A suit for partition of ancestral property succeeds. Part of the property is agricultural land assessed to Government revenue; the rest is a house in town. The trial court passes a single preliminary decree declaring a one-third share and appoints a commissioner to divide **all** the property by metes and bounds.

**Sword (Plaintiff seeking a workable decree):** Point out at the earliest opportunity — ideally before the decree is drawn — that Order XX Rule 18(1) read with section 54 is **mandatory**: as regards the revenue-paying estate, the civil court may declare the shares but the partition must be directed to be made by the **Collector**. A commissioner's division of that land is a nullity and will not be recognised by the revenue authorities. Ask for a decree that bifurcates: declaration plus a Rule 18(1) direction to the Collector for the agricultural land, and a preliminary decree under Rule 18(2) with a commissioner for the house.

**Shield (Contesting co-sharer):** The same defect is available to you at the final-decree stage or in appeal, and it is a jurisdictional objection which cannot be waived, because it goes to the competence of the civil court to effect the division rather than to a mere irregularity in procedure. If a co-sharer has died since the preliminary decree, also invoke *Phoolchand* to have the preliminary decree amended to reflect the devolved shares before any final decree is passed.
:::

::: tip Illustration 3 — Mesne profits forgotten, and interest deemed refused
A landlord succeeds in a suit for possession and for arrears of rent of ₹4,20,000. The judgment awards possession and the arrears. The decree is drawn accordingly. It says nothing about mesne profits from the date of the suit, and nothing about interest after the date of the decree. Possession is delivered two and a half years later after a contested execution.

**Sword (Landlord):** The position is largely lost, and the lesson is prospective. Mesne profits for the two and a half years of wrongful occupation after institution could have been recovered only through an inquiry directed under Order XX Rule 12(1)(c), capped at three years; no inquiry having been directed, there is nothing for the executing court to work out, and a separate suit is exposed to Order II Rule 2 because the claim was available at institution. On interest, section 34(2) deems post-decree interest to have been **refused** where the decree is silent, so the executing court cannot allow it. The only serious avenue is section 152, and it will succeed only if the judgment itself dealt with mesne profits or interest and the decree failed to carry that into effect.

**Shield (Tenant in execution):** Object under section 47 to any attempt to recover mesne profits or post-decree interest. The executing court is bound by the decree as drawn; it cannot grant a relief the decree does not contain, and section 34(2) supplies an express deeming provision on interest. If the decree-holder moves under section 152, resist on the ground that this is not a slip but an attempt to obtain in execution what was never adjudicated.

**The drafting lesson:** every prayer for possession must be accompanied by a prayer for mesne profits with an express request for an inquiry under Order XX Rule 12, and every money prayer must ask in terms for interest *pendente lite* and future interest until realisation at a specified rate.
:::

::: info Court fee at the decree stage
Three fee events arise around Order XX and are routinely missed.
**One — mesne profits.** Under section 11 of the Court Fees Act, 1870, where a decree directs an inquiry into mesne profits and the amount found due exceeds the amount at which the plaintiff valued the relief in the plaint, the decree **shall not be executed** until the deficit fee is paid. The final decree is therefore conditional on the fee.
**Two — accounts.** Under section 7(iv)(f), the plaintiff values the relief himself in the plaint; on the final decree the fee is recomputed on the amount found due, and the same conditionality applies.
**Three — partition.** Where the plaintiff is in joint possession, a fixed fee under Article 17(vi) of Schedule II suffices at institution; where he has been excluded, *ad valorem* fee on the value of his share is payable. Several States (notably Karnataka, Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu) have their own partition-fee provisions — check the State Act, not the Central one.
Section 149 permits the deficit to be made good at any stage, and payment then relates back, curing the institution.

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence — Order XX

**Anil Rai v. State of Bihar, (2001) 7 SCC 318 — 2-Judge Bench**

*Facts:* A High Court judgment was delivered nearly two years after the conclusion of arguments.

**Ratio decidendi:** Unreasonable delay between the conclusion of arguments and the delivery of judgment defeats the purpose of adjudication and erodes confidence in the system. The Court laid down graded directions: judgments should ordinarily be delivered within the time indicated by Order XX Rule 1; where two months elapse, a party may move the Chief Justice for an early judgment; where three months elapse, the Chief Justice may withdraw the case and reassign it; and where six months elapse, either party may apply for withdrawal of the case from the Judge and its reassignment, and such an application shall ordinarily be allowed.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Arguments in this matter concluded on —, more than six months ago; on the directions in *Anil Rai*, this application for withdrawal and reassignment ought ordinarily to be allowed."
:::

**Balraj Taneja v. Sunil Madan, (1999) 8 SCC 396 — 2-Judge Bench**

*Facts:* A suit was decreed under Order VIII Rule 10 on the defendant's failure to file a written statement, the judgment doing little more than recording that failure and decreeing the suit.

**Ratio decidendi:** Even where a judgment is passed for failure to file a written statement, the court is not a mere rubber stamp. A "judgment" as defined in section 2(9) means the statement given by the Judge of the **grounds** of a decree, and Order XX Rules 4(2) and 5 require a concise statement of the case, the points for determination, the decision and the reasons. The court must apply its mind to whether the plaint discloses a cause of action and whether the facts pleaded entitle the plaintiff to the relief claimed, and where the plaint raises questions requiring proof, it must call for evidence.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The impugned judgment records only the absence of a written statement and decrees the suit; on *Balraj Taneja* it is not a judgment within section 2(9) read with Order XX Rule 4(2), the court having applied no mind to whether the plaint disclosed a cause of action."
:::

**Swaran Lata Ghosh v. H.K. Banerjee, (1969) 1 SCC 709 — 3-Judge Bench**

*Facts:* A trial court decided a suit without framing issues in the manner required and without recording findings supported by reasons on the questions arising.

**Ratio decidendi:** A judgment must set out the points for determination, the decision on each, and the reasons; a judgment which records conclusions without disclosing the process of reasoning, or which fails to deal with the issues arising on the pleadings, is not a judgment in the eye of law. The appellate court cannot perform its function on such a record, and the ordinary consequence is a remand.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The judgment records conclusions on issues 2 and 4 without a word of reasoning; on *Swaran Lata Ghosh* it fails the requirement of Order XX Rules 4(2) and 5 and the matter must go back for fresh findings."
:::

**Phoolchand v. Gopal Lal, (1967) 3 SCR 153 / AIR 1967 SC 1470 — 3-Judge Bench**

*Facts:* After a preliminary decree in a partition suit, a party died, altering the shares; the question was whether the court could pass a second preliminary decree.

**Ratio decidendi:** The Code does **not** prohibit more than one preliminary decree in a partition suit. Where events after the preliminary decree — typically a death and consequent devolution — require the shares declared to be altered, the court has power to amend the preliminary decree or to pass a further preliminary decree so that the final decree divides the property in accordance with the rights as they stand. This is confined to partition suits, where the rights of parties may change before the division is worked out.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"On *Phoolchand*, this Hon'ble Court retains the power to pass a further preliminary decree giving effect to the devolution on the death of Defendant No. 2; a final decree on the original shares would divide the property contrary to the rights as they now stand."
:::

**Shakuntala Devi Jain v. Kuntal Kumari, AIR 1969 SC 575 — 3-Judge Bench; and Central Bank of India v. Ravindra, (2002) 1 SCC 367 — 5-Judge Bench (on interest)**

*Facts (Ravindra):* A Constitution Bench considered the scope of section 34 and the award of interest by a decree, including the treatment of interest after the date of the decree.

**Ratio decidendi:** Section 34 divides interest into three periods — before the suit, *pendente lite*, and after the decree. Pre-suit interest is a matter of substantive law and must be pleaded and proved. Interest *pendente lite* and future interest are in the court's discretion and must be **expressly awarded**; by section 34(2), where the decree is silent as to interest from the date of the decree, such interest is **deemed to have been refused** and a separate suit is barred. The decree must therefore specify the rate and the period.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The decree being silent as to interest from its date, section 34(2) deems such interest to have been refused, and the executing court has no jurisdiction to allow it."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Order XX Rule 6A(2) was inserted by the 1976 Amendment and its second limb — that the judgment ceases to have the effect of a decree once the decree is drawn — matters in appeal practice; do not rely on the judgment copy after the decree is available. The *Anil Rai* directions are **administrative directions to the High Courts**, not a rule of invalidity: a delayed judgment is not void, and an appeal that argues only delay will fail. Section 34's six per cent cap on post-decree interest does not apply where the liability arises out of a **commercial transaction**, a term defined in the Explanation II to section 34(1), and separate statutory regimes displace section 34 altogether — notably the **Interest Act, 1978**, the **MSMED Act, 2006** (compound interest at three times the RBI bank rate), and section 31(7) of the **Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996** for awards. Court fee provisions cited here are from the Central Court Fees Act, 1870; most States have their own Acts with different sections and different partition-fee rules — always check the State Act.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit — Order XX

::: danger Eight decree-drafting failures that destroy execution
1. **The property is not described in the decree.** The schedule with boundaries, survey numbers, municipal numbers and area must be reproduced **in the decree itself**. "As described in the plaint" is not a description; the bailiff executes the decree, not the plaint.
2. **No direction for an inquiry into mesne profits.** Order XX Rule 12(1)(c) must be invoked in the prayer and carried into the decree. Without it, everything after the date of the plaint is lost, and Order II Rule 2 may bar a fresh suit.
3. **Silence on post-decree interest.** Section 34(2) deems it refused. Every money decree must state the rate and that interest runs "until realisation".
4. **Costs unquantified or unallocated.** Rule 6(2) requires the amount, the payer, and the proportions. A decree that says "costs to follow the event" without figures cannot be executed for costs.
5. **A partition decree that declares shares but directs nothing.** Declaration alone yields a declaratory decree. For revenue-paying estates the decree **must** direct partition by the Collector under Rule 18(1) and section 54; for other property it must provide for a final decree, ordinarily with a commission under Order XXVI Rule 13.
6. **A decree that omits a relief the judgment granted.** This is the classic section 152 case — and it must be moved before the trial court, promptly, and with the passage of the judgment quoted. It cannot be raised for the first time in execution, where section 47 shuts the door.
7. **Instalments granted in the judgment but not in the decree.** Rule 11 permits payment by instalments; if the concession appears only in the reasoning it is unenforceable, and the judgment-debtor's default cannot be regulated.
8. **A decree against a legal representative that does not limit the liability.** Where a decree is passed against an LR, section 50 and Order XXII confine the liability to the estate that has come to his hands. The decree must say so, or execution will be levied against personal assets and a fresh round of section 47 objections will follow.
:::

::: warning Strategic timing
**Before arguments close:** re-read the prayer clause. Anything you want in the decree must have been prayed for. Mesne profits, an inquiry under Rule 12, interest *pendente lite* and future interest, a preliminary decree, a commission — all of them originate in the prayer.
**When judgment is reserved:** diarise thirty days and sixty days. At two months, a written request to the Chief Justice under *Anil Rai* is appropriate and is not disrespectful.
**On the day of pronouncement:** if your client is unrepresented, ensure the Rule 5A information is given and recorded. Apply for certified copies of the judgment and decree **the same day** and keep the receipt — section 12(2) of the Limitation Act excludes the copying time, and the receipt is your proof.
**Within fifteen days:** the decree should be drawn under Rule 6A(1). Submit your own draft to the office. Do not leave the drafting to a clerk working from a judgment he has not read.
**Before the decree is signed:** check it, line by line, against the judgment — property description, relief, interest, costs, instalments, directions for a final decree. Rule 7 requires the Judge to satisfy himself that the decree accords with the judgment; a variance pointed out now takes a minute, and after signature takes a section 152 application.
**If limitation is running and the decree is not ready:** file the appeal on the judgment copy under Rule 6A(2). Never let time expire waiting for the registry.
**Within thirty days of the decree:** review under Article 124, if there is an error apparent. Section 152 has no limitation, but review does.
:::

**Model prayer — correction of the decree under section 152**

> It is therefore most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to:
> **(a)** Correct the decree dated — passed in Original Suit No. — of — by incorporating therein the description of the suit property with its boundaries as set out in Schedule A to the plaint and as found by this Hon'ble Court in paragraph 19 of the judgment, the omission thereof being an accidental slip within the meaning of section 152 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908;
> **(b)** Direct that the corrected decree shall bear the date of the original decree in accordance with Order XX Rule 7; and
> **(c)** Pass such further orders as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit.

**Model prayer clause — a possession suit, drawn so that the decree works**

> It is therefore most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to:
> **(a)** Pass a decree for possession of the suit property more fully described in Schedule A hereto, by directing the Defendant to deliver vacant and peaceful possession thereof to the Plaintiff;
> **(b)** Pass a decree for ₹— towards arrears of rent for the period — to —;
> **(c)** Direct an inquiry under Order XX Rule 12(1)(c) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 into the mesne profits payable by the Defendant from the date of institution of this suit until delivery of possession to the Plaintiff, or the relinquishment of possession by the Defendant with notice to the Plaintiff through this Hon'ble Court, or the expiration of three years from the date of the decree, whichever event first occurs, and pass a final decree in accordance with the result of such inquiry;
> **(d)** Award interest on the sum decreed under prayer (b) at the rate of — per cent per annum from the date of institution of the suit until the date of the decree, and thereafter at the rate of — per cent per annum until realisation;
> **(e)** Award costs of and incidental to this suit, quantified and payable by the Defendant to the Plaintiff; and
> **(f)** Pass such further or other orders as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit in the facts and circumstances of the case.

**Model prayer clause — a partition suit including a revenue-paying estate**

> It is therefore most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to:
> **(a)** Pass a preliminary decree declaring that the Plaintiff is entitled to a one-third undivided share in the suit properties described in Schedules A and B hereto;
> **(b)** In so far as the properties in Schedule A are assessed to the payment of revenue to the Government, direct, in accordance with Order XX Rule 18(1) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 read with section 54 thereof, that the partition and separation of the Plaintiff's share be made by the Collector or a gazetted subordinate deputed by him, in accordance with the declaration aforesaid;
> **(c)** In so far as the properties in Schedule B are concerned, appoint a Commissioner under Order XXVI Rule 13 to effect a partition by metes and bounds in accordance with the shares declared, and upon the Commissioner's report pass a final decree allotting the Plaintiff's share and directing separate possession thereof;
> **(d)** Direct an inquiry into mesne profits payable to the Plaintiff in respect of her share from the date of exclusion, and pass a final decree accordingly;
> **(e)** Award costs; and
> **(f)** Pass such further orders as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit.

## Chapter consolidation — Orders XVI to XX together

These five Orders are one continuous mechanism. Read them as a sequence of gates, each of which can be closed against you by inattention rather than by the merits.

| Stage | What must happen | Governing rule | The failure that costs the case |
|---|---|---|---|
| Issues settled | List of witnesses filed within **15 days** | O. XVI R. 1(1) | Right to summon lost; leave required |
| Summons applied for | Expenses deposited before issue | O. XVI R. 2 | Summons not issued; date wasted |
| Witness does not attend | Proclamation, attachment, fine | O. XVI RR. 10–12 | Party blamed for a witness's default it never sought to compel |
| Trial opens | Right to begin determined | O. XVIII R. 1 | Right of reply under R. 2(2) surrendered |
| Before any witness | Rebuttal reserved; R. 3A leave taken | O. XVIII RR. 3, 3A | No second innings; adverse inference against the party |
| Evidence led | Affidavit of chief examination, issue by issue | O. XVIII R. 4(1) | Issue decided against you for want of evidence |
| Cross-examination | Before the court or its commissioner | O. XVIII R. 4(2); *Salem Advocate (II)* | Recording by an unauthorised person; record vitiated |
| Interlocutory relief along the way | Affidavit on belief with **grounds stated** | O. XIX R. 3(1) proviso | Injunction refused; *ex parte* order vacated |
| Adjournment sought | Sufficient cause; **not more than three** | O. XVII R. 1 | Costs, or closure of evidence under R. 3 |
| Evidence closed | Written arguments with an index | O. XVIII R. 2(3A) | Findings drawn without your best material |
| Judgment | Reasons, and a finding on **each issue** | O. XX RR. 4(2), 5 | Remand; years lost |
| Decree | Agrees with the judgment; relief specified | O. XX R. 6(1) | Inexecutable decree; s. 47 objections |
| Post-judgment | Copies applied for the same day; appeal on the judgment if the decree is not ready | O. XX RR. 20, 6A(2); s. 12(2) Limitation Act | Appeal out of time while the registry types |

::: tip The ten sentences worth memorising
1. **An affidavit is not evidence** unless an order is made under Order XIX Rule 1 or Rule 2, or it is an affidavit of chief examination under Order XVIII Rule 4 followed by cross-examination — *Sudha Devi*.
2. **Belief without grounds is worthless.** Order XIX Rule 3(1) admits belief on interlocutory applications only if the grounds are stated in the affidavit itself.
3. **Chief examination on affidavit is competent; cross-examination must be before the court or its commissioner** — *Salem Advocate (II)*, *Ameer Trading*.
4. **Order XVIII Rule 17 is the court's power to clarify, not a party's route to fill a lacuna** — *Vadiraj Naggappa Vernekar*; Rule 17A stands omitted since 2002.
5. **Three adjournments per party is a statutory cap**, and the court must record sufficient cause and impose costs — Order XVII Rule 1 as amended in 2002.
6. **Order XVII Rule 2 and Rule 3 give different remedies**: a Rule 2 order attracts Order IX and is restorable; a Rule 3 decision on the merits is only appealable.
7. **A judgment must contain reasons and a finding on each framed issue**, even where it is passed under Order VIII Rule 10 — *Balraj Taneja*, *Swaran Lata Ghosh*.
8. **The decree, not the judgment, is executed**, and section 47 forbids the executing court from going behind it — so every relief must be carried into the decree under Order XX Rule 6.
9. **Silence in a decree as to post-decree interest is a refusal** — section 34(2); and mesne profits after institution are lost unless an inquiry is directed under Order XX Rule 12(1)(c), which is capped at three years.
10. **A revenue-paying estate is partitioned by the Collector, not by the civil court** — Order XX Rule 18(1) read with section 54; and more than one preliminary decree is permissible in a partition suit — *Phoolchand*.
:::

::: warning Before you file
- Diarise the **fifteen-day** window for the witness list from the date issues are settled, and deposit expenses with the summons application in the same visit.
- Draft the affidavit of examination-in-chief with the framed issues physically in front of you, and tick each issue off as you cover it. Then check every fact in it against the pleadings.
- Reserve rebuttal evidence and take Rule 3A leave **before** the first witness is called, and see that the order sheet records it.
- Never rely at trial on an affidavit filed at the interlocutory stage. Re-tender it under Order XVIII Rule 4 or obtain an order under Order XIX Rule 1.
- Split every verification by source — knowledge, information (with the source named), belief (with grounds), and legal advice. A blanket verification destroys the affidavit's value.
- When judgment is reserved, diarise thirty and sixty days, and treat *Anil Rai* as a live administrative remedy rather than a rhetorical flourish.
- On the day of pronouncement, apply for certified copies and **keep the receipt** — it is the proof of the section 12(2) exclusion.
- Submit your own draft decree. Check it against the judgment for the schedule and boundaries, the relief, interest until realisation, mesne profits, costs with figures, instalments, and any direction for a final decree.
- If limitation is running and the decree is not drawn, file the appeal on the judgment copy under Order XX Rule 6A(2).
- Section 152 has no limitation but cures only slips; review under Article 124 has **thirty days** and cures errors apparent. Diagnose which one you have before you draft.
:::
