# Orders I & II — Parties to Suits, Frame of Suit and Order II Rule 2

Order I answers *who must be, and who may be, on the record*. Order II answers *how much of the dispute must be brought in one go*. Together they govern the architecture of every plaint, and between them they contain the most unforgiving trap in the entire Code — **Order II Rule 2**.

The governing philosophy is the avoidance of multiplicity. The Code wants one suit, between all the necessary parties, covering the whole of one cause of action, decided once. Order I secures the "all the parties" half; Order II secures the "whole of the cause of action" half.

| Provision | One-line function |
|---|---|
| **O. I R. 1** | Who may be joined as **plaintiffs** — same act or transaction, common question of law or fact. |
| **O. I R. 3** | Who may be joined as **defendants** — the same test, mirrored. |
| **O. I R. 3A** | Power to order **separate trials** where joinder may embarrass or delay. |
| **O. I R. 9** | **No suit shall be defeated** by misjoinder or non-joinder — **except** for non-joinder of a **necessary party**. |
| **O. I R. 10(1)** | Substitution or addition of a **plaintiff** where the suit is filed in the wrong name or by mistake. |
| **O. I R. 10(2)** | The court's power to **strike out or add** any party, at any stage, on application or *suo motu*. |
| **O. I R. 10(5)** | Limitation against a **newly added defendant** runs from the date of his impleadment. |
| **O. I R. 13** | Objections as to **misjoinder or non-joinder must be taken at the earliest possible opportunity** or are deemed waived. |
| **O. II R. 1** | Frame of suit — every suit shall be framed so as to afford ground for a **final decision** and prevent further litigation. |
| **O. II R. 2** | **The whole claim** must be included; relinquishment or omission of any portion **bars** a later suit for it. |
| **O. II R. 3** | Joinder of **causes of action** against the same defendant. |
| **O. II R. 4** | Restrictions on joining other claims with a suit for **recovery of immovable property**. |
| **O. II R. 6** | Power to order **separate trials** where joinder of causes of action embarrasses the trial. |
| **O. II R. 7** | Objections to **misjoinder of causes of action** must be taken at the earliest opportunity. |

::: info The architecture in one breath
Order I Rule 9 says a defect of parties will not kill your suit — **unless** the missing party is a *necessary* party, in which case it will. Order II Rule 2 says a defect of claims will kill your *second* suit — always. The asymmetry is deliberate: parties can be added; claims, once omitted, cannot be revived.
:::

## Order I — Parties to Suits

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **Rule 1. Who may be joined as plaintiffs.** — All persons may be joined in one suit as plaintiffs where —
> **(a)** any right to relief in respect of, or arising out of, the same act or transaction or series of acts or transactions is alleged to exist in such persons, whether jointly, severally or in the alternative; and
> **(b)** if such persons brought separate suits, any common question of law or fact would arise.

> **Rule 3. Who may be joined as defendants.** — All persons may be joined in one suit as defendants where —
> **(a)** any right to relief in respect of, or arising out of, the same act or transaction or series of acts or transactions is alleged to exist against such persons, whether jointly, severally or in the alternative; and
> **(b)** if separate suits were brought against such persons, any common question of law or fact would arise.

> **Rule 3A. Power to order separate trials where joinder of plaintiffs or defendants may embarrass or delay trial.** — Where it appears to the Court that any joinder of plaintiffs or defendants may embarrass or delay the trial of the suit, the Court may put the plaintiffs to their election or order separate trials or make such other order as may be expedient in the interests of justice.

> **Rule 9. Misjoinder and non-joinder.** — No suit shall be defeated by reason of the misjoinder or non-joinder of parties, and the Court may in every suit deal with the matter in controversy so far as regards the rights and interests of the parties actually before it:
> **Provided that** nothing in this rule shall apply to non-joinder of a necessary party.

> **Rule 10. Suit in name of wrong plaintiff.** — **(1)** Where a suit has been instituted in the name of the wrong person as plaintiff, or where it is doubtful whether it has been instituted in the name of the right plaintiff, the Court may at any stage of the suit, if satisfied that the suit has been instituted through a bona fide mistake, and that it is necessary for the determination of the real matter in dispute so to do, order any other person to be substituted or added as plaintiff upon such terms as the Court thinks just.
> **(2) Court may strike out or add parties.** — The Court may at any stage of the proceedings, either upon or without the application of either party, and on such terms as may appear to the Court to be just, order that the name of any party improperly joined, whether as plaintiff or defendant, be struck out, and that the name of any person who ought to have been joined, whether as plaintiff or defendant, or whose presence before the Court may be necessary in order to enable the Court effectually and completely to adjudicate upon and settle all the questions involved in the suit, be added.
> **(3)** No person shall be added as a plaintiff suing without a next friend or as the next friend of a plaintiff under any disability without his consent.
> **(4)** Where a defendant is added, the plaint shall, unless the Court otherwise directs, be amended in such manner as may be necessary, and amended copies of the summons and of the plaint shall be served on the new defendant and, if the Court thinks fit, on the original defendant.
> **(5)** Subject to the provisions of the Indian Limitation Act, 1877, section 22, the proceedings as against any person added as defendant shall be deemed to have begun only on the service of the summons.

> **Rule 13. Objections as to non-joinder or misjoinder.** — All objections on the ground of non-joinder or misjoinder of parties shall be taken at the earliest possible opportunity and, in all cases where issues are settled, at or before such settlement, unless the ground of objection has subsequently arisen, and any such objection not so taken shall be deemed to have been waived.

**Statutory mechanics.**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "All persons **may** be joined" (RR. 1, 3) | **Permissive** | Joinder under these rules is a facility, not a duty. The plaintiff elects. |
| "**and**" between clauses (a) and (b) of RR. 1 and 3 | **Conjunctive** | Both limbs must be satisfied: same act/transaction **and** a common question. One alone is insufficient. |
| "whether **jointly, severally or in the alternative**" | Enabling breadth | Permits inconsistent and alternative claims against different defendants in one suit — the foundation of the "sue everyone in the chain" pleading. |
| "**No** suit **shall** be defeated by reason of misjoinder or non-joinder" (R. 9) | **Prohibitory, curative** | A defect of parties is *never* by itself fatal. |
| "**Provided that** nothing in this rule shall apply to non-joinder of a **necessary party**" | **Carve-out — the exception that matters** | Non-joinder of a *necessary* party **is** fatal. This proviso is the whole battleground of Order I. |
| "The Court **may** at any stage … order that the name of any party … be struck out … [or] be added" (R. 10(2)) | **Discretionary, wide** | Exercisable *suo motu*, without application, at any stage — including in appeal. |
| "**ought to have been joined** … **or** whose presence … **may be necessary** in order to enable the Court **effectually and completely** to adjudicate" | **Two distinct limbs** | Limb one = *necessary* party. Limb two = *proper* party. R. 10(2) covers both. |
| "the proceedings as against any person added as defendant shall be deemed to have begun only on the **service of the summons**" (R. 10(5)) | **Limitation rule** | The added defendant gets the benefit of limitation computed to the date of his impleadment, not the date of the original plaint. |
| "**shall** be taken at the earliest possible opportunity … **deemed to have been waived**" (R. 13) | **Mandatory + deeming** | Mirrors Section 21. A belated misjoinder/non-joinder objection is statutorily waived. |

::: warning The apparent contradiction between Rule 9 and Rule 13 — resolved
Rule 13 says *all* objections as to non-joinder are waived if not taken before settlement of issues. Rule 9's proviso says non-joinder of a **necessary** party defeats the suit. How do they sit together? The accepted reconciliation: Rule 13 governs objections as to **proper** parties and formal defects, which are waived if not taken in time. The absence of a **necessary** party goes to the court's ability to grant effective relief at all, and the court will notice it — indeed must notice it — whenever it arises, because it cannot pass an effective decree without that party. In practice, however, **never rely on this**: take the objection before issues are settled.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — The three categories of party.** Everything in Order I turns on this taxonomy, and it must be at your fingertips.

| Category | Test | Effect of omission | Can the court add him? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Necessary party** | (i) There must be a **right to some relief against him** in respect of the matter involved; **and** (ii) **no effective decree can be passed in his absence** | Suit **liable to be dismissed** — proviso to R. 9 | Yes, under R. 10(2), even *suo motu* |
| **Proper party** | His presence is not indispensable, but is **necessary for a complete and final decision** on the questions involved | Suit proceeds; decree binds only those on record | Yes, under R. 10(2) |
| **Neither** (a stranger) | No interest in the subject matter and no relief claimed against him | Irrelevant | No — he is liable to be struck out as improperly joined |

The **two-limb test for a necessary party is conjunctive**. A person against whom relief is claimed but without whom an effective decree can still be passed is a *proper* party, not a necessary one. This distinction decides whether the suit survives.

**Sub-module B — Worked examples of necessary parties.**

| Suit | Necessary party | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Partition of joint family property | **All coparceners / co-sharers** | A partition decree that binds only some co-owners is unworkable; shares cannot be allotted piecemeal |
| Suit by a tenant against a stranger interfering with possession | The **landlord** is ordinarily *not* necessary | The tenant's possessory right is independently enforceable |
| Suit to set aside a sale deed | **All parties to the deed**, vendor and vendee | The deed cannot be avoided as against a person not before the court |
| Suit for specific performance against a vendor who has resold | The **subsequent purchaser** | Relief against the property is ineffective without binding him |
| Suit challenging an appointment to an office | The **appointee** | No effective order can be made unseating a person not heard |
| Suit for eviction against a sub-tenant | The **tenant** | The sub-tenancy derives from the tenancy |
| Suit for possession against a trespasser | Co-owners **not** necessary — one co-owner may sue for the benefit of all | Established exception |

**Sub-module C — Order I Rule 10(2) and the "dominus litis" principle.** The plaintiff is *dominus litis* — master of the suit. He chooses whom to sue and cannot ordinarily be compelled to fight a person against whom he claims no relief. This principle has real force: a stranger cannot force his way into a suit merely because he has an interest in the outcome.

But the principle yields in two situations:
1. Where the person sought to be added is a **necessary** party — the plaintiff's choice cannot deprive the court of the ability to pass an effective decree.
2. Where his presence is required for the court to "**effectually and completely** adjudicate upon and settle all the questions involved" — the second limb of Rule 10(2), which is wider than the necessary-party test and is the basis on which most *proper* parties are added.

**Sub-module D — Rule 10(5): the limitation consequence of impleadment.** When a defendant is added, the suit is deemed to have commenced against **him** on the date of his impleadment (in practice, on service of summons), not on the date the plaint was originally filed. The consequence is severe and frequently overlooked:

> P files a money suit against D1 on 1 January 2024, one month before limitation expires. In March 2025 P discovers D2 is jointly liable and applies to implead him. D2 is impleaded in June 2025. **The claim against D2 is time-barred**, because as against D2 the suit began in June 2025, long after limitation expired.

The saving is **Section 21 of the Limitation Act, 1963** (the modern successor to the reference in Rule 10(5) to the 1877 Act), whose proviso permits the court, where the omission to include the new party was due to a **mistake made in good faith**, to direct that the suit shall be deemed to have been instituted against him on any earlier date.

::: danger Fatal error — impleading a defendant after limitation has run
This kills more claims than any other single mistake in party practice. Before applying to implead any defendant, check the limitation position **as against that defendant on today's date**. If the period has expired, the application must expressly invoke the proviso to Section 21 of the Limitation Act, 1963, plead the mistake, plead the good faith, and pray for a direction that the suit be deemed instituted against him on the original date. An impleadment application that omits this prayer hands the new defendant a complete defence.
:::

**Sub-module E — Rule 10(1) versus Rule 10(2).** These are different powers and are routinely confused:

- **Rule 10(1)** applies where the suit was instituted in the name of the **wrong plaintiff** or it is doubtful whether the right plaintiff is suing. It requires a **bona fide mistake** and permits substitution or addition of a plaintiff. Its typical use: a suit filed in a trade name instead of the proprietor's name, or by a firm instead of its partners.
- **Rule 10(2)** is the general power to strike out or add **any** party, plaintiff or defendant. It requires no mistake and may be exercised *suo motu*.

**Sub-module F — Misjoinder and multifariousness.** Three distinct defects, often conflated:

| Defect | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Misjoinder of parties** | Joining a person who ought not to have been joined |
| **Misjoinder of causes of action** | Joining claims that may not lawfully be combined (an Order II problem) |
| **Multifariousness** | Misjoinder of **both** parties and causes of action together — several plaintiffs with distinct causes of action against several defendants |

None of these is by itself fatal. Rule 9 saves misjoinder of parties; Order II Rule 6 and Order I Rule 3A permit the court to order separate trials; and both Order I Rule 13 and Order II Rule 7 deem the objections waived if not taken before settlement of issues.

**Sub-module G — Representative suits under Rule 8.** Where numerous persons share the same interest, one or more may sue or be sued on behalf of all, **with the permission of the court** and after notice at the plaintiff's expense. Two conditions are jurisdictional: **same interest** among the represented persons, and **leave of the court** obtained before the suit proceeds. A decree in a properly constituted representative suit binds all the represented persons — which is why Explanation VI to Section 11 makes it a species of res judicata.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — the partition suit missing a coparcener.** P sues his brothers D1 and D2 for partition of ancestral property, omitting their sister S, who is a coparcener by virtue of the 2005 amendment to the Hindu Succession Act.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** If the omission is discovered early, P applies at once under Order I Rule 10(2) to implead S, and — because a coparcener's right accrues by birth and the claim is for a share, not a time-barred money claim — limitation is ordinarily not an obstacle. P frames the application as adding a *necessary* party so that the court may pass an effective preliminary decree under Order XX Rule 18.
- **Shield (defendants):** D1 and D2 raise the non-joinder of a necessary party as preliminary objection No. 1 in the written statement, invoking the proviso to Order I Rule 9, and contend that no partition decree allotting shares by metes and bounds can be passed in S's absence without exposing the parties to a second partition suit at her instance. They ask for dismissal, not impleadment — because dismissal costs the plaintiff his court fee and his years.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A decree of partition passed in the absence of a coparcener would not conclude the shares and would compel a second partition — this Hon'ble Court cannot effectually and completely adjudicate upon the questions involved without her, and she is therefore a necessary party within the proviso to Order I Rule 9."
:::

**Illustration 2 — specific performance and the subsequent purchaser.** P holds an agreement to sell from D1. D1 sells the property to D2 during the currency of the agreement. P sues D1 alone for specific performance.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P must implead D2 as a necessary party. Without D2 on the record, a decree directing D1 to convey is worthless — D1 no longer holds the title, and D2's purchase is unaffected by a decree to which he was not a party. P should also consider the doctrine of *lis pendens* under Section 52 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, and, where the sale to D2 preceded the suit, plead that D2 is not a bona fide purchaser for value without notice within Section 19(b) of the Specific Relief Act, 1963.
- **Shield (subsequent purchaser):** D2, once impleaded, pleads that he is a transferee for value who has paid his money in good faith and without notice of the original contract, and is therefore protected by Section 19(b) of the Specific Relief Act. He also examines whether he was impleaded after the three-year period under Article 54 of the Limitation Act had run against him, and if so pleads Rule 10(5).

**Illustration 3 — the plaintiff resisting impleadment (dominus litis).** P sues D for eviction from a shop. X applies to be impleaded, claiming that he is in occupation under an arrangement with D and that his rights will be affected.

- **Sword (applicant X):** X pleads that he is in actual possession, that a decree for eviction will be executed against him, and that his presence is necessary for the court to effectually and completely adjudicate on the question of who is entitled to possession — the second limb of Rule 10(2).
- **Shield (plaintiff P):** P invokes *dominus litis*: he claims no relief against X, does not admit any right in X, and is entitled to choose his opponent. He argues that X is at best a proper party and that his presence would enlarge the scope of a simple eviction suit into a title contest. P adds that X's remedy, if he is dispossessed in execution, lies under **Order XXI Rule 97 to 101**, which provides a complete adjudicatory mechanism for obstructionists claiming an independent right.

::: info The Order XXI Rules 97–101 answer
This is the standard and highly effective response to a third-party impleadment application in a possession suit. Because Rules 97 to 101 of Order XXI give any person resisting execution a full adjudication of his right, with the determination having the force of a decree under Rule 103, the applicant suffers no prejudice by being kept out of the suit. Deploy it whenever a stranger seeks to complicate a straightforward possession claim.
:::

**Illustration 4 — misjoinder of parties that is not fatal.** P sues D1, the manufacturer, D2, the distributor, and D3, the retailer, in one suit for damages for a defective machine, claiming against them "jointly, severally or in the alternative".

- **Sword (plaintiff):** Order I Rule 3 expressly permits this. The right to relief arises out of the same transaction — the supply chain of a single machine — and common questions of fact (was the machine defective, and when did the defect arise) would arise if separate suits were brought. The words "or in the alternative" allow P to plead inconsistent cases against different defendants without electing at the pleading stage.
- **Shield (defendants):** D1 pleads misjoinder, contending that his contract was with D2 alone and that he has no privity with P, so no common question arises between him and D3. His realistic remedy is not dismissal — Rule 9 forecloses that — but an application under **Order I Rule 3A** for separate trials, or under **Order II Rule 6**, on the ground that the joinder will embarrass and delay the trial.

**Illustration 5 — the belated non-joinder objection.** D contests a suit on the merits, issues are settled, evidence is led, and at final arguments D submits that a co-owner was not joined.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** Order I Rule 13 is a complete answer if the missing person is only a *proper* party — the objection is deemed waived. P also points out that under Rule 9 the court may deal with the matter so far as regards the rights of the parties actually before it.
- **Shield (defendant):** D's only route is to establish that the omitted person is a **necessary** party, so that the proviso to Rule 9 displaces Rule 13. He must show that no effective decree can be passed at all — not merely that the absent person has an interest.

**Limitation table for party-related applications.**

| Application | Article, Limitation Act 1963 | Period | Runs from |
|---|---|---|---|
| Impleadment of a new defendant — effect on the claim | **S. 21** (with proviso) | Claim tested as on the date of impleadment | Date of impleadment / service |
| To bring the legal representatives of a deceased **plaintiff** on record | **Art. 120** | 90 days | Date of death |
| To bring the legal representatives of a deceased **defendant** on record | **Art. 120** | 90 days | Date of death |
| To set aside an **abatement** | **Art. 121** | 60 days | Date of abatement |
| To restore a suit dismissed for default | **Art. 122** | 30 days | Date of dismissal |
| Condonation of delay in the above | **S. 5** | — | Sufficient cause to be shown |

::: danger Fatal error — the abatement cascade
When a party dies, three clocks start at once: **90 days** to bring the legal representatives on record (Art. 120); on expiry, the suit **abates** as against that party; then **60 days** to apply to set aside the abatement (Art. 121); and thereafter only Section 5 condonation, which requires sufficient cause for **each** period separately. Where the deceased was a necessary party and the abatement is not set aside, the whole suit may abate. Diarise the date of death the moment it is reported.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Udit Narain Singh Malpaharia v. Additional Member, Board of Revenue, Bihar**, AIR 1963 SC 786 — 3 Judges.
*Facts:* A writ petition challenging the grant of a settlement, in which the beneficiary of the grant was not impleaded.
**Ratio decidendi:** The classic formulation of the distinction. A **necessary party** is one without whom no order can be made effectively; a **proper party** is one in whose absence an effective order can be made, but whose presence is necessary for a complete and final decision on the question involved in the proceeding. Where a necessary party is not before the court, the proceeding is liable to be dismissed.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"On Udit Narain Singh, the test is whether an effective order can be made in the absence of the person concerned; if it cannot, he is a necessary party and his non-joinder is fatal."
:::

**Kasturi v. Iyyamperumal**, (2005) 6 SCC 733 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* Strangers to an agreement to sell, claiming independent title and possession, sought to be impleaded in a suit for specific performance.
**Ratio decidendi:** In a suit for specific performance of a contract for sale, the parties to the contract, or their legal representatives, and a transferee *pendente lite* are the only necessary parties. A person claiming **independent title and possession adverse to the vendor** is neither a necessary nor a proper party, because the scope of a specific performance suit is confined to whether there was a valid agreement and whether the plaintiff is ready and willing to perform; the court is not to convert it into a title suit.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Kasturi forecloses the impleadment of strangers claiming adverse title in a specific performance suit — the enquiry is confined to the contract and the plaintiff's readiness and willingness, and a title contest cannot be imported into it."
:::

**Mumbai International Airport Pvt. Ltd. v. Regency Convention Centre & Hotels Pvt. Ltd.**, (2010) 7 SCC 417 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* An application by a third party to be impleaded in a suit, resisted by the plaintiff on the ground that he was *dominus litis*.
**Ratio decidendi:** The Court set out the governing propositions: the general rule is that the plaintiff is *dominus litis* and cannot be compelled to implead a person against whom he does not seek relief; the court may nevertheless add a person under Order I Rule 10(2) if he is a **necessary** party, or if his presence is required for a complete and effective adjudication; but the second limb does not entitle the court to add a person whose presence would convert the suit into a different suit or enlarge its scope beyond what the plaintiff has chosen to litigate.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Mumbai International Airport confirms that the plaintiff's dominion over his own suit yields only to necessity — not to the mere convenience or interest of a stranger, and never so as to enlarge the suit into a different controversy."
:::

**Razia Begum v. Sahebzadi Anwar Begum**, AIR 1958 SC 886 — 3 Judges.
*Facts:* Application for impleadment in a suit for a declaration of marital status.
**Ratio decidendi:** In suits relating to **status**, as distinguished from suits relating to property, the court has a wider discretion to add parties, because a declaration of status operates *in rem* and affects persons beyond the immediate litigants. The test of "legal interest" is applied more liberally in status suits than in property suits.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Razia Begum recognises a wider power of impleadment in suits concerning status, precisely because the declaration sought will affect persons not before the court."
:::

**Ramesh Hirachand Kundanmal v. Municipal Corporation of Greater Bombay**, (1992) 2 SCC 524 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* Whether a person with a commercial interest in the outcome could be impleaded under the second limb of Order I Rule 10(2).
**Ratio decidendi:** The words "all the questions involved in the suit" in Rule 10(2) mean the questions **in the suit as framed by the plaintiff**, not any question that might conceivably arise between the applicant and a party. The person to be added must be directly and legally interested in the answer to the controversy, and a mere commercial or indirect interest is insufficient.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The questions 'involved in the suit' are the questions the plaintiff has raised; a stranger with a commercial interest in the outcome is not thereby a proper party."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Citations and bench strengths above should be confirmed in the official reports (AIR / SCC). The Kasturi line of authority in particular has been the subject of considerable subsequent elaboration, including on the position of transferees *pendente lite* and on the boundary between a "specific performance suit" and a title dispute — check the current position before relying on it.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**The pre-filing party audit — run this on every plaint.**

1. **Against whom do I claim relief?** Every such person must be a defendant.
2. **Can the decree I seek be executed without any person not on my list?** If not, that person is a **necessary** party. Add him.
3. **Is any defendant a party to a document I seek to avoid, cancel or enforce?** All parties to the document must be before the court.
4. **Has the property changed hands?** Implead the transferee, and consider Section 52 of the Transfer of Property Act.
5. **Is any defendant a minor, a person of unsound mind, a firm, a company, a trust, a deity, or the Government?** Each has its own regime — Orders XXX, XXXI, XXXII, XXXIII and XXVII, and Sections 79 and 80.
6. **Is limitation running against any person I might later need to add?** If so, add him **now**, not later. Rule 10(5) is unforgiving.
7. **Is the correct description used?** A firm sued in its trade name; a company by its correct registered name; the Government by the Union of India or the State, not by a department; a public officer in his official designation.

**Drafting traps causing rejection or dismissal.**

| Trap | Consequence | Cure |
|---|---|---|
| Omitting a coparcener in a partition suit | Non-joinder of a necessary party; dismissal | Implead all co-sharers at institution |
| Suing the vendor alone after a resale | Ineffective decree | Implead the subsequent purchaser |
| Impleading after limitation has expired against the new defendant | Complete defence to that defendant | Invoke S. 21 proviso, Limitation Act, in the application itself |
| Suing "M/s ABC Enterprises" without disclosing it is a proprietorship | Suit against a non-existent legal person | Sue the proprietor, "carrying on business in the name and style of…" |
| Suing a department ("Public Works Department") instead of the State | Defective party; liable to be struck out | Sue the State through the Secretary, and comply with S. 80 |
| Failing to obtain leave under O. I R. 8 in a representative suit | Decree does not bind the represented class | Apply for leave and effect notice at the outset |
| Raising non-joinder for the first time in appeal | Deemed waived under R. 13 unless the party is necessary | Plead it as preliminary objection No. 1 |
| Not applying under O. XXII on a party's death | Abatement | Diarise 90 days from death |

**Strategic timing.**

| Move | When |
|---|---|
| Plaintiff's own impleadment application | At institution; or immediately upon discovery, with a limitation prayer |
| Defendant's non-joinder objection | Written statement, preliminary objection No. 1, before settlement of issues (O. I R. 13) |
| Application for separate trials | As soon as the embarrassment is apparent (O. I R. 3A / O. II R. 6) |
| Third party's impleadment application | Before issues are settled; delay is a strong discretionary factor |
| Application to strike out an improperly joined party | Before settlement of issues |
| Application under O. XXII | Within 90 days of death |

> **(c)** direct, in exercise of the powers under the proviso to Section 21 of the Limitation Act, 1963, that the suit as against the proposed Defendant shall be deemed to have been instituted on [original date of institution], the omission to implead him at the institution of the suit having been due to a mistake made in good faith, namely [state the mistake];
> **(d)** direct issue of amended summons together with a copy of the amended plaint upon the said Defendant; and
> **(e)** pass such further or other order as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit and proper.

> **PRELIMINARY OBJECTION NO. 1 — NON-JOINDER OF NECESSARY PARTIES**
>

## Order II — Frame of Suit

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **Rule 1. Frame of suit.** — Every suit shall as far as practicable be framed so as to afford ground for final decision upon the subjects in dispute and to prevent further litigation concerning them.

> **Rule 2. Suit to include the whole claim.** — **(1)** Every suit shall include the whole of the claim which the plaintiff is entitled to make in respect of the cause of action; but a plaintiff may relinquish any portion of his claim in order to bring the suit within the jurisdiction of any Court.
> **(2) Relinquishment of part of claim.** — Where a plaintiff omits to sue in respect of, or intentionally relinquishes, any portion of his claim, he shall not afterwards sue in respect of the portion so omitted or relinquished.
> **(3) Omission to sue for one of several reliefs.** — A person entitled to more than one relief in respect of the same cause of action may sue for all or any of such reliefs; but if he omits, except with the leave of the Court, to sue for all such reliefs, he shall not afterwards sue for any relief so omitted.
>
> ***Explanation.*** — For the purposes of this rule an obligation and a collateral security for its performance and successive claims arising under the same obligation shall be deemed respectively to constitute but one cause of action.
>
> ***Illustration***
> A lets a house to B at a yearly rent of Rs. 1,200. The rent for the whole of the years 1905, 1906 and 1907 is due and unpaid. A sues B in 1908 only for the rent due for 1906. A shall not afterwards sue B for the rent due for 1905 or 1907.

> **Rule 3. Joinder of causes of action.** — **(1)** Save as otherwise provided, a plaintiff may unite in the same suit several causes of action against the same defendant, or the same defendants jointly; and any plaintiffs having causes of action in which they are jointly interested against the same defendant or the same defendants jointly may unite such causes of action in the same suit.
> **(2)** Where causes of action are united, the jurisdiction of the Court as regards the suit shall depend on the amount or value of the aggregate subject-matters at the date of instituting the suit.

> **Rule 4. Only certain claims to be joined with suits for the recovery of immovable property.** — No cause of action shall, unless with the leave of the Court, be joined with a suit for the recovery of immovable property, except —
> **(a)** claims for mesne profits or arrears of rent in respect of the property claimed or any part thereof;
> **(b)** claims for damages for breach of any contract under which the property or any part thereof is held; and
> **(c)** claims in which the relief sought is based on the same cause of action:
> Provided that nothing in this rule shall be deemed to prevent any party in a suit for foreclosure or redemption from asking to be put into possession of the mortgaged property.

> **Rule 6. Power of Court to order separate trials.** — Where it appears to the Court that any causes of action joined in one suit cannot be conveniently tried or disposed of together, the Court may order separate trials or make such other order as may be expedient in the interests of justice.

> **Rule 7. Objections as to misjoinder.** — All objections on the ground of misjoinder of causes of action shall be taken at the earliest possible opportunity and, in all cases where issues are settled, at or before such settlement, unless the ground of objection has subsequently arisen, and any such objection not so taken shall be deemed to have been waived.

**Statutory mechanics of Rule 2.**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "Every suit **shall** include the **whole of the claim**" (R. 2(1)) | **Mandatory** | The obligation to plead the entire claim arising from one cause of action. |
| "but a plaintiff **may relinquish** any portion … to bring the suit within the **jurisdiction** of any Court" | **Express permission, single purpose** | The only sanctioned reason for relinquishment is to fit a pecuniary ceiling — and the price is that the relinquished portion is gone forever. |
| "Where a plaintiff **omits to sue** in respect of, **or intentionally relinquishes**, any portion of his claim" (R. 2(2)) | Two triggers, one deliberate and one not | **Note: mere omission suffices.** Intention is required only for "relinquishes"; "omits" is unqualified. Inadvertence is no defence. |
| "he **shall not afterwards sue** in respect of the portion so omitted or relinquished" | **Absolute prohibitory bar** | No leave, no exception, no discretion. |
| "if he omits, **except with the leave of the Court**, to sue for **all such reliefs**" (R. 2(3)) | **Bar with a statutory escape hatch** | Unlike R. 2(2), sub-rule (3) permits leave — but the leave must be obtained **in the first suit**, not applied for later. |
| **Explanation** | **Deeming provision, three limbs** | (i) an obligation, (ii) collateral security for it, and (iii) successive claims under it, are **one** cause of action. |

**The critical structural distinction — Rule 2(2) versus Rule 2(3).**

| | **Rule 2(2)** | **Rule 2(3)** |
|---|---|---|
| What is omitted | A **portion of the claim** | One of several **reliefs** |
| Leave available? | **No** | **Yes** — "except with the leave of the Court" |
| When must leave be sought | N/A | **In the first suit**, before it is decided |
| Effect of omission | Later suit for that portion barred absolutely | Later suit for that relief barred **unless leave was obtained** |

This is why the standard practice of pleading "the plaintiff craves leave of this Hon'ble Court to sue hereafter for such further reliefs as may become due" is worth including — but note that it protects only under sub-rule (3), and only if the court actually grants leave. It does nothing about an omitted *portion of the claim* under sub-rule (2).

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — The policy.** A defendant should be vexed once, and once only, in respect of one cause of action. If a plaintiff could sue for a slice of his claim, see how the court reacted, and then sue for the rest, litigation would be endless and the defendant would be harassed by instalments. Order II Rule 2 makes the plaintiff put his entire case on the table at once.

**Sub-module B — "Cause of action" is the master concept.** Everything in Rule 2 depends on identifying the cause of action, because the bar operates only where the second suit is founded on the **same** cause of action as the first. The cause of action is the **bundle of material facts** the plaintiff must plead and prove to entitle him to the decree. It is not the relief, not the evidence, and not the legal label.

The practical test in a Rule 2 contest is therefore: *would the plaintiff, in the second suit, have to prove any material fact that he did not have to prove in the first?* If the answer is no, the causes of action are the same and the bar applies.

**Sub-module C — The four ingredients the defendant must prove.** A defendant pleading Order II Rule 2 carries a heavy burden and must establish, cumulatively:

1. That the **second suit** is founded on the **same cause of action** as the first;
2. That in respect of that cause of action, the plaintiff was **entitled to more than one relief**, or the claim was divisible;
3. That the plaintiff **omitted** to sue for the relief or portion now claimed, **without the leave of the court**; and
4. — and this is the ingredient that defeats most Rule 2 pleas — that the **plaint in the first suit is on the record**, proved as evidence, so that the court can compare the two causes of action.

**Sub-module D — Why the first plaint must be produced.** The court cannot decide whether the causes of action are identical by looking at the second plaint alone. The pleadings in the former suit are the only material from which the earlier cause of action can be ascertained, and they must be **proved and exhibited**. A Rule 2 objection pleaded without producing the earlier plaint fails for want of proof — regardless of how obviously meritorious it might be.

**Sub-module E — The Explanation, unpacked.**

| Limb | Meaning | Illustration |
|---|---|---|
| "an **obligation**" | The principal liability | A promissory note for ₹10 lakh |
| "and a **collateral security** for its performance" | Any security given for that obligation | A mortgage or a guarantee securing the note |
| "**shall be deemed** … but one cause of action" | Deeming | A suit on the note, omitting to enforce the mortgage, bars a later mortgage suit |
| "**successive claims arising under the same obligation**" | Instalments and recurring dues | Three years' arrears of rent under one lease — sue for all, or lose the rest |

The Explanation is the trap in the loan-recovery and tenancy contexts. It converts what looks like three separate claims into one.

**Sub-module F — Order II Rule 2 versus Explanation IV to Section 11.** These two bars are cousins and are constantly confused. The comparison is worth memorising:

| | **Order II Rule 2** | **Section 11, Explanation IV (constructive res judicata)** |
|---|---|---|
| What is barred | A claim or **relief** the plaintiff could have sued for and did not | A **ground of attack or defence** that might and ought to have been raised and was not |
| Whom it binds | The **plaintiff** only | **Both** parties |
| Requires a decision on merits in the first suit? | **No** — the bar operates even if the first suit was dismissed for default or withdrawn | **Yes** — the matter must have been heard and finally decided |
| Turns on | Identity of the **cause of action** | Identity of the **matter in issue** |
| Escape hatch | **Leave** of the court in the first suit (R. 2(3) only) | None |
| Proof required | The **plaint** in the first suit | The **judgment and decree** in the first suit |

**Sub-module G — What Order II Rule 2 does *not* bar.**

- A claim founded on a **different cause of action**, even between the same parties over the same property. A suit for possession and a later suit for a subsequent trespass are different causes of action.
- A claim which had **not accrued** when the first suit was filed. The bar attaches to what the plaintiff was "entitled to make" at the date of the earlier suit; a cause of action arising afterwards is untouched.
- A **defendant's** claims — Rule 2 binds the plaintiff. A defendant who omits a counter-claim is not barred by Rule 2, though he may face Explanation IV to Section 11 or Order VIII Rule 6A considerations.
- Reliefs in respect of **distinct properties or distinct transactions**, even between the same parties.
- A suit where the earlier proceeding was **not a suit** — for example, a writ petition or an arbitration.

**Sub-module H — Rule 4 and the possession suit.** Rule 4 confines what may be tacked onto a suit for recovery of immovable property, unless leave is obtained. Permitted without leave: mesne profits or arrears of rent in respect of the same property; damages for breach of the contract under which the property is held; and any claim resting on the **same** cause of action. Everything else — an unrelated money claim, damages for a separate tort — requires leave. Note the interaction: Rule 2 compels you to include the whole claim, while Rule 4 restricts what you may include. **Where a claim falls outside Rule 4 but arises from the same cause of action, you must apply for leave under Rule 4 — and that same application will preserve you under Rule 2(3).**

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — the classic: possession now, mesne profits later.** P sues D, a trespasser, for possession of a shop and obtains a decree. Two years later P files a second suit claiming mesne profits for the period of D's unlawful occupation up to the date of the first decree.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P will argue that possession and mesne profits are distinct reliefs resting on distinct facts, and that in any event he was entitled to await the outcome of the possession suit.
- **Shield (defendant):** D pleads Order II Rule 2(3). The cause of action for both is identical — D's wrongful occupation of P's property. P was entitled to both reliefs when he filed the first suit; he sued for one and omitted the other without leave. D produces the certified copy of the first plaint, proves it, points to the absence of any leave recorded in the first suit, and asks for rejection under **Order VII Rule 11(d)**.

::: danger Fatal error — suing for possession without mesne profits
This is the single most common Order II Rule 2 casualty. Mesne profits accruing **up to the date of the suit** must be claimed in the possession suit itself. Mesne profits accruing **after** the suit are separately provided for by **Order XX Rule 12**, which permits the decree to direct an enquiry into future mesne profits — but only if you ask. **Always claim past mesne profits in the prayer, and always seek an Order XX Rule 12 direction for future mesne profits.** Omit either and that money is gone.
:::

**Illustration 2 — the Explanation in action: note and mortgage.** D borrows ₹50 lakh from P on a promissory note, secured by a mortgage of D's flat. P sues on the note alone and obtains a money decree. Execution yields nothing. P then files a mortgage suit under Order XXXIV.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P argues that the note and the mortgage are distinct instruments creating distinct rights — a personal covenant and a security interest — and that Order XXXIV Rule 14 contemplates separate treatment.
- **Shield (defendant):** D relies squarely on the **Explanation**: "an obligation and a collateral security for its performance … shall be deemed … but one cause of action". The loan is the obligation; the mortgage is the collateral security; they are one cause of action; P sued on part of it and is barred. D also points to **Order XXXIV Rule 14**, which independently bars a mortgagee who has obtained a money decree from bringing the mortgaged property to sale otherwise than by instituting a suit for sale.

::: warning Drafting Trap — the secured lender's election
A secured creditor who sues on the personal covenant alone risks losing the security; one who sues only on the security may find the personal remedy compromised. The safe course is a **single composite suit** — for sale of the mortgaged property under Order XXXIV Rule 4, and, in the same suit, for a personal decree for any balance under Order XXXIV Rule 6. Never split them.
:::

**Illustration 3 — successive rent instalments.** The statutory illustration to Rule 2. A landlord with three years' arrears sues for one year's rent only.

- **Sword (landlord):** The only defence is that each year's rent is a separate cause of action accruing on a separate date.
- **Shield (tenant):** The Explanation expressly deems "successive claims arising under the same obligation" to be one cause of action. The lease is the obligation; the yearly rent is a successive claim under it; the arrears are one cause of action. The suit for the omitted years is barred.
- **The practical answer for the landlord:** sue for **all** arrears due at the date of the suit, and add a prayer for rent and damages for use and occupation **accruing during the pendency of the suit and until delivery of possession**. That forward-looking prayer is what saves the post-institution period.

**Illustration 4 — where Rule 2 does *not* apply: a fresh trespass.** P sues D for an injunction restraining D from encroaching on P's boundary, and succeeds. Eighteen months later D makes a **new** encroachment at a different point.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** The second suit rests on a wholly new set of material facts — a new act of encroachment, on a new date, at a new location. The cause of action is different, and Rule 2 has nothing to say about it.
- **Shield (defendant):** D would have to show that the second encroachment was already in existence and known to P when the first suit was filed, so that P could and should have sued for it then. Absent that, the plea fails.

**Illustration 5 — the plea that fails for want of the first plaint.** D pleads Order II Rule 2 in his written statement, referring to an earlier suit between the parties, but produces only the earlier **judgment**, not the earlier **plaint**.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P submits that the plea is unsustainable. To decide whether the causes of action are the same, the court must see what was pleaded in the earlier suit; the judgment records what was decided, not what was claimed. The burden is on the defendant, and he has not discharged it.
- **Shield (defendant):** D's only remedy is to apply to produce the certified copy of the earlier plaint — which, if the trial has advanced, means an application under **Order XLI Rule 27** in appeal, subject to its strict conditions, or an application to lead additional evidence at trial with an explanation for the delay.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A plea under Order II Rule 2 cannot be adjudicated without the plaint in the former suit being brought on record and proved; the defendant bears that burden, and in its absence the plea must fail however plausible it may appear."
:::

**Illustration 6 — Rule 4 leave in a possession suit.** P sues D for recovery of a godown and, in the same suit, claims ₹8 lakh due under an unrelated supply contract between them.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P must apply for **leave under Rule 4**, since the money claim is neither mesne profits, nor arrears of rent, nor damages for breach of the contract under which the property is held, nor founded on the same cause of action.
- **Shield (defendant):** D objects to misjoinder of causes of action under Rule 4, and applies under **Rule 6** for separate trials on the ground that the two claims involve entirely different evidence and will embarrass the trial. Note that D must raise this **before settlement of issues** or it is deemed waived under **Rule 7**.

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Gurbux Singh v. Bhooralal**, AIR 1964 SC 1810 — 3 Judges.
*Facts:* A plea of Order II Rule 2 was raised, but the plaint in the earlier suit was not produced in evidence.
**Ratio decidendi:** The foundational decision on proof. In order to establish a plea under Order II Rule 2(3), the defendant must prove: (i) that the second suit is in respect of the same cause of action as the first; (ii) that in respect of that cause of action the plaintiff was entitled to more than one relief; and (iii) that being entitled to more than one relief, the plaintiff omitted, without leave, to sue for the relief for which the second suit is filed. **The plea cannot be established except by producing in evidence the plaint in the previous suit**, since it is only from that pleading that the cause of action in the earlier suit can be ascertained. A plea of this kind, being technical and capable of shutting out a legitimate claim, must be strictly established.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Gurbux Singh requires the defendant to place the earlier plaint on record and prove it; in its absence the bar under Order II Rule 2 cannot be adjudicated and the plea must be rejected."
:::

**Mohammad Khalil Khan v. Mahbub Ali Mian**, AIR 1949 PC 78 — Privy Council.
*Facts:* Successive suits arising from the same transaction, with different reliefs claimed in each.
**Ratio decidendi:** The tests are: (i) is the claim in the new suit in fact founded on a cause of action distinct from that in the previous suit; (ii) were the reliefs claimed in the new suit in fact relievable in the previous suit; and (iii) was the omission the result of a deliberate relinquishment or of an accident. The cause of action is the **media upon which the plaintiff asks the court to arrive at a conclusion in his favour**, and where the evidence needed to support the two claims is substantially the same, the causes of action are the same.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The test of identity of causes of action is whether the same evidence would sustain both claims — if it would, the second suit is barred."
:::

**Virgo Industries (Eng.) Pvt. Ltd. v. Venturetech Solutions Pvt. Ltd.**, (2013) 1 SCC 625 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* A first suit for permanent injunction restraining alienation, founded on agreements to sell, followed by a second suit for specific performance of the same agreements.
**Ratio decidendi:** The object of Order II Rule 2 is to avoid the splitting of claims and multiplicity of proceedings. Where the plaintiff was entitled, at the time of the first suit, to sue for specific performance and instead sued only for an injunction founded on the same agreements, the cause of action being the same, the subsequent suit for specific performance is barred. The Court emphasised that the bar operates on the plaintiff's entitlement at the date of the first suit.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Virgo Industries holds that a plaintiff who sues for an injunction founded on an agreement, when he was already entitled to specific performance of it, cannot afterwards sue for specific performance — the causes of action being identical."
:::

**Rathnavathi v. Kavita Ganashamdas**, (2015) 5 SCC 223 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* Whether a suit for declaration and possession, and a suit for specific performance, arose from the same cause of action.
**Ratio decidendi:** The bar of Order II Rule 2 applies only where the cause of action in both suits is the same. Where the two suits are founded on different facts — one on the agreement of sale and the other on a distinct transaction or a distinct wrong — the bar is not attracted, and the court must examine the pleadings in both suits to determine the question.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Order II Rule 2 requires identity, not similarity, of causes of action; a difference in the material facts pleaded takes the case outside the bar."
:::

**Alka Gupta v. Narender Kumar Gupta**, (2010) 10 SCC 141 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* A suit dismissed at the threshold on grounds including Order II Rule 2, without the issue having been framed or the parties heard on it.
**Ratio decidendi:** A suit cannot be dismissed on the ground of Order II Rule 2, or of res judicata, unless the plea is raised, an issue is framed, and the parties are given an opportunity to lead evidence. Neither bar can be applied on assumptions or without the necessary material on record.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A bar under Order II Rule 2 is a mixed question of fact and law; it cannot be decided without a pleading, an issue and the requisite material, and a summary dismissal on that ground is unsustainable."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Bench strengths and citations above should be verified in the official reports. Gurbux Singh remains the leading authority on the evidentiary requirement and is reliably cited; the bench strength stated should nonetheless be confirmed. Mohammad Khalil Khan is a Privy Council decision, followed but not binding, and its formulation has been restated in later Supreme Court decisions.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**The Order II Rule 2 pre-filing audit — run this before every plaint is signed.**

1. **Write out the cause of action in one sentence.** Identify the bundle of material facts.
2. **List every relief that this cause of action could sustain — today.** Not the reliefs you want; every relief you are *entitled* to.
3. **For each relief you are omitting, ask why.** If the answer is "we'll see how this goes" or "we may sue for that later", you are about to lose it.
4. **If the omission is deliberate** — because of court fee, or because the evidence is not yet ready — then **apply for leave under Order II Rule 2(3) in the plaint itself**, and ensure that the leave is **recorded in an order**. An unrecorded prayer for leave is worthless.
5. **Check the Explanation.** Is there a collateral security? Are there successive claims — instalments, rent, interest? They are one cause of action.
6. **Check Rule 4** if the suit is for recovery of immovable property, and apply for leave for anything outside clauses (a) to (c).
7. **Check Rule 3(2).** If you are joining causes of action, the aggregate value governs jurisdiction; the suit may now belong in a higher court.

**The defendant's Order II Rule 2 checklist.**

1. **Obtain a certified copy of the plaint in the earlier suit** before you plead the bar. Without it the plea is stillborn (*Gurbux Singh*).
2. **Plead the bar specifically** in the written statement, setting out the earlier suit number, the parties, the cause of action pleaded there, and the relief now claimed which was then available and omitted.
3. **Ask for an issue to be framed.** A bar under Rule 2 cannot be applied without a pleading, an issue, and material (*Alka Gupta*).
4. **Consider Order VII Rule 11(d)** — but only where the bar is apparent from the plaint itself read with the earlier plaint on record. Where facts must be investigated, Rule 11(d) is the wrong vehicle and the plea belongs in the written statement.
5. **Plead Explanation IV to Section 11 in the alternative.** The two bars cover overlapping but different ground, and one may succeed where the other fails.

**Drafting traps.**

| Trap | Consequence | Cure |
|---|---|---|
| Possession claimed without past mesne profits | Mesne profits barred forever | Claim past mesne profits **and** pray for an O. XX R. 12 enquiry into future mesne profits |
| Suit on a promissory note where a mortgage secures it | Mortgage remedy barred by the Explanation and by O. XXXIV R. 14 | One composite suit under O. XXXIV RR. 4 and 6 |
| Suing for one year's rent out of three | Remaining arrears barred | Claim all arrears due at institution, plus future rent and damages for use and occupation |
| Relinquishing part of the claim to fit a pecuniary ceiling | The relinquished portion is gone permanently (R. 2(1)) | File in the higher court instead; the fee difference is almost always less than the abandoned claim |
| Injunction suit on an agreement, specific performance saved for later | Specific performance barred (*Virgo Industries*) | Claim specific performance in the first suit, with injunction as ancillary relief |
| Praying for leave under R. 2(3) but not obtaining an order | No leave in law | Have the leave **recorded in the order sheet**; verify it before the suit is decreed |
| Joining an unrelated money claim to a possession suit without leave | Misjoinder under R. 4 | Apply for leave under R. 4 in the plaint |
| Pleading R. 2 without the earlier plaint | Plea fails (*Gurbux Singh*) | Obtain and exhibit the certified copy |

**Strategic timing.**

| Move | When |
|---|---|
| Prayer for leave under O. II R. 2(3) | **In the plaint**, at institution |
| Amendment to add an omitted relief | Under O. VI R. 17, **before** the first suit is decided — after decree there is no remedy |
| Defendant's R. 2 objection | Written statement; and by application under O. VII R. 11(d) where the bar appears on the record |
| Objection to misjoinder of causes of action | Earliest opportunity, **at or before settlement of issues** (R. 7) |
| Application for separate trials | As soon as the embarrassment is apparent (R. 6) |

**Model prayer clause — possession suit, Order II Rule 2 compliant.**

> It is therefore most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to pass a decree:
> **(a)** for possession, directing the Defendant to deliver vacant, peaceful and physical possession of the suit property more particularly described in Schedule A hereto to the Plaintiff;
> **(b)** for a sum of ₹[___] towards **mesne profits / damages for use and occupation** in respect of the suit property for the period from [date] to the date of institution of this suit, together with interest thereon at [rate]% per annum;
> **(c)** directing an enquiry under **Order XX Rule 12 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908** into the mesne profits in respect of the suit property from the date of institution of this suit until delivery of possession to the Plaintiff, and for a decree for the amount so found due;
> **(d)** for a permanent injunction restraining the Defendant, his servants, agents and any person claiming through him, from alienating, encumbering, parting with possession of, or creating any third-party interest in, the suit property;
> **(e)** for costs of and incidental to this suit; and
> **(f)** for such further or other relief as the nature and circumstances of the case may require and this Hon'ble Court may deem fit.

**Model leave prayer — Order II Rule 2(3).**

> **(g)** the Plaintiff, being entitled to further reliefs in respect of the same cause of action, namely [specify the relief precisely — e.g., "damages for the breach of the Agreement dated [date], the quantum whereof cannot presently be ascertained pending the report of the surveyor"], and being presently unable to claim the same for the reasons stated in paragraph [___] above, most respectfully prays that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to grant **leave under Order II Rule 2(3) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908** to institute a separate suit in respect of the said relief, and to record such leave.

::: warning Verify that leave was actually granted
Drafting the prayer is only half of it. Before the suit is decided, check the order sheet and confirm that an order granting leave under Order II Rule 2(3) has been **recorded**. Counsel routinely include the prayer, never press it, and discover years later that no leave was ever granted. The prayer is not the leave.
:::

## Chapter consolidation — the two bars of the plaint

| | **Order I** (parties) | **Order II** (claims) |
|---|---|---|
| **Governing question** | Is everyone here who needs to be? | Is everything here that needs to be? |
| **Default position** | Defects are **forgiven** — R. 9 | Defects are **fatal** — R. 2(2) |
| **The exception** | Non-joinder of a **necessary** party is fatal | Leave under R. 2(3) saves an omitted **relief** |
| **Cure available during the suit** | Impleadment under R. 10(2), at any stage | Amendment under O. VI R. 17, before decree |
| **Cure available after decree** | — | **None** |
| **Waiver of objection** | R. 13 — before settlement of issues | R. 7 (misjoinder of causes) — before settlement of issues |
| **Burden of proof on the objector** | Show that an effective decree cannot be passed | Produce and prove the **plaint in the former suit** |
| **Limitation consequence** | R. 10(5) — the clock runs to the date of impleadment | Art. 51 — mesne profits die in 3 years while possession survives 12 |

::: tip The three sentences worth memorising from this chapter
**One.** A suit is never defeated by misjoinder or non-joinder — unless the person missing is a *necessary* party, meaning one without whom no effective decree can be passed.
**Two.** When you implead a defendant, the suit begins against him on that date; check limitation as against him **before** you apply, and invoke the proviso to Section 21 of the Limitation Act in the application itself.
**Three.** Claim every relief your cause of action can bear, or obtain recorded leave under Order II Rule 2(3) — and never file a possession suit without claiming past mesne profits and praying for an Order XX Rule 12 enquiry into future mesne profits.
:::
