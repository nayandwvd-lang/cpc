# Orders XII, XIII, XIV & XV — Admissions, Documents, Issues and Disposal at First Hearing

These four Orders cover the passage from pleadings to trial. Order XII is about admissions — how to extract them, how to prove them, and how to convert them into a decree without waiting for a trial. Order XIII governs the documentary record: what must be produced, when, what happens to a document that arrives late, and the court's power to impound. Order XIV is the intellectual centre of the whole Code — the framing of issues, which fixes what will be tried, who must prove it, and therefore what evidence is admissible at all. Order XV is the short but underrated power to dispose of a suit at the first hearing where there is nothing left to try.

::: info The architecture in one breath
The Code assumes that most suits contain far less genuine dispute than the pleadings suggest. These four Orders are the machinery for finding that out early. Order XII forces the parties to say what they admit and gives the court power to decree on the admission at once. Order XIII closes the documentary record so that neither side can produce a surprise document at trial. Order XIV then reduces whatever survives to a finite list of issues, each with an assigned burden — and no evidence is admissible on a matter not covered by an issue. Order XV is the release valve: where the exercise reveals nothing to try, the court decides the suit there and then.
:::

## Order XII — admissions and judgment on admissions

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **Rule 1. Notice of admission of case.** — Any party to a suit may give notice, by his pleading, or otherwise in writing, that he admits the truth of the whole or any part of the case of any other party.

> **Rule 2. Notice to admit documents.** — Either party may call upon the other party to admit, within seven days from the date of service of the notice, any document, saving all just exceptions; and in case of refusal or neglect to admit, after such notice, the costs of proving any such document shall be paid by the party so neglecting or refusing, whatever the result of the suit may be, unless the Court otherwise directs; and no costs of proving any document shall be allowed unless such notice is given, except where the omission to give the notice is in the opinion of the Court a saving of expense.

> **Rule 2A. Document to be deemed to be admitted if not denied after service of notice to admit documents.** — (1) Every document which a party is called upon to admit, if not denied specifically or by necessary implication, or stated to be not admitted in the pleading of that party or in his reply to the notice to admit documents, shall be deemed to be admitted except as against a person under a disability:
>
> *Provided that the Court may, in its discretion and for reasons to be recorded, require any document so admitted to be proved otherwise than by such admission.*
>
> (2) Where a party unreasonably neglects or refuses to admit a document after the service on him of the notice to admit documents, the Court may direct him to pay costs to the other party by way of compensation.

> **Rule 3A. Power of Court to record admission.** — Notwithstanding anything contained in these rules, the Court may, at any stage of the proceeding, of its own motion, call upon any party to admit any document and shall, in such a case, record whether the party admits or refuses or neglects to admit such document.

> **Rule 4. Notice to admit facts.** — Any party may, by notice in writing, at any time not later than nine days before the day fixed for the hearing, call on any other party to admit, for the purposes of the suit only, any specific fact or facts mentioned in such notice. And in case of refusal or neglect to admit the same within six days after service of such notice, or within such further time as may be allowed by the Court, the costs of proving such fact or facts shall be paid by the party so neglecting or refusing, whatever the result of the suit may be, unless the Court otherwise directs:
>
> *Provided that any admission made in pursuance of such notice is to be deemed to be made only for the purposes of the particular suit, and not as an admission to be used against the party on any other occasion or in favour of any person other than the party giving the notice.*

> **Rule 5. Form of notice.** — A notice to admit facts shall be in Form No. 10 in Appendix C, and admissions of facts shall be in Form No. 11 in Appendix C.

> **Rule 6. Judgment on admissions.** — (1) Where admissions of fact have been made either in the pleading or otherwise, whether orally or in writing, the Court may at any stage of the suit, either on the application of any party or of its own motion and without waiting for the determination of any other question between the parties, make such order or give such judgment as it may think fit, having regard to such admissions.
>
> (2) Whenever a judgment is pronounced under sub-rule (1), a decree shall be drawn up in accordance with the judgment and the decree shall bear the date on which the judgment was pronounced.

> **Rule 7. Affidavit of signature.** — An affidavit of the pleader or his clerk, of the due signature of any admissions made in pursuance of any notice to admit documents or facts, shall be sufficient evidence of such admissions.

> **Rule 8. Notice to produce documents.** — Notice to produce documents shall be in Form No. 12 in Appendix C, with such variations as circumstances may require. An affidavit of the pleader, or his clerk, of the service of any notice to produce, and of the time when it was served, with a copy of the notice to produce, shall in all cases be sufficient evidence of the service of the notice, and of the time when it was served.

> **Rule 9. Costs.** — If a notice to admit or produce specifies documents which are not necessary, the costs occasioned thereby shall be borne by the party giving such notice.

#### Statutory mechanics

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| R. 2 — "within **seven days** from the date of service" | Fixed period for documents | Silence past seven days engages R. 2A |
| R. 2 — costs "**shall** be paid … **whatever the result of the suit may be**" | Mandatory costs sanction | The only costs order in the Code that is indifferent to who wins |
| R. 2 — "**no** costs of proving any document shall be allowed unless such notice is given" | Prohibitory | A party who never served a notice to admit cannot recover proof costs |
| R. 2A(1) — "**shall be deemed to be admitted**" | Deeming provision | Non-denial equals admission, subject to the proviso and to disability |
| R. 2A(1), proviso — "the Court **may** … require any document so admitted to be proved" | Discretionary escape, reasons required | Prevents the deeming from producing injustice |
| R. 2A(1) — "**except as against a person under a disability**" | Carve-out | Minors and persons of unsound mind cannot be fixed by silence |
| R. 3A — "**Notwithstanding** anything contained in these rules" | Non-obstante | The court's *suo motu* power operates free of the notice machinery |
| R. 3A — "**shall** … record whether the party admits or refuses" | Mandatory recording | The mirror of Order X Rule 3 |
| R. 4 — "not later than **nine days** before the day fixed for the hearing" | Condition precedent | A late notice to admit facts carries no costs consequence |
| R. 4 — "within **six days** after service" | Fixed period for facts | Extendable by the Court |
| R. 4, proviso — admission "**only for the purposes of the particular suit**" | Limiting proviso | A Rule 4 admission cannot be used in other litigation or by strangers |
| R. 6(1) — "either in the pleading **or otherwise, whether orally or in writing**" | Deliberately wide | Admissions in correspondence, affidavits, or across the bar all qualify |
| R. 6(1) — "the Court **may** at **any stage** … **or of its own motion**" | Discretionary, unrestricted as to time | No application is strictly necessary and no stage is too early or too late |
| R. 6(1) — "**without waiting for the determination of any other question**" | Enabling | Authorises a partial decree while the rest of the suit continues |
| R. 6(1) — "such order or such judgment as it **may think fit**" | Discretion | The court is never bound to decree on an admission |
| R. 6(2) — "a decree **shall** be drawn up" | Mandatory | A Rule 6 judgment produces a decree, and therefore an appeal under section 96 |

::: warning "May", not "shall" — and it decides most Rule 6 applications
Rule 6 confers a discretion. Even a clear, unequivocal admission does not entitle a party to a decree as of right. The court will refuse where the admission is conditional, where it is coupled with a plea that would defeat the claim (limitation, a set-off, a counter-claim, want of title), or where a decree on part would embarrass the trial of the rest. The winning application is therefore not the one that shows an admission; it is the one that shows an admission which, standing alone and taking the defence at its highest, leaves no answer to that part of the claim.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**(a) Three kinds of admission, with different consequences.** The Order deals with (i) admissions in the **pleadings**, which need no notice and are binding without proof; (ii) admissions extracted by **notice** under Rules 2 and 4, which carry a costs sanction and, for documents, a deeming provision; and (iii) admissions made **elsewhere** — in correspondence, in an affidavit, in a reply notice, across the bar — which Rule 6 expressly brings within its reach. Only the second category is limited to the purposes of the particular suit.

**(b) The costs sanction is the point of Rules 2 and 4.** Ordinarily costs follow the event. Rules 2 and 4 create a rare exception: a party who unreasonably refuses to admit a document or a fact pays the cost of proving it **whatever the result of the suit may be**. Serving a notice to admit therefore costs you nothing and either narrows the trial or builds a costs claim you keep even if you lose.

**(c) Rule 2A converts silence into admission — for documents only.** There is no corresponding deeming provision for facts. If you are served with a notice to admit documents and you do nothing, the documents are deemed admitted as to execution and contents, subject to "all just exceptions" — meaning admissibility, relevance and stamping objections survive. If you are served with a notice to admit facts and you do nothing, the facts are not admitted; you merely become liable for the cost of proving them.

**(d) "Saving all just exceptions" is doing real work.** Admitting a document under Rule 2 admits that it is what it purports to be. It does not admit that it is relevant, that it is admissible, that it is sufficiently stamped, or that the statements in it are true. Those objections are preserved by the phrase and should be recorded expressly in the reply.

**(e) Rule 6 is the fastest route to a decree in the Code.** It requires no trial, no evidence and no issues. The court reads the admission and decrees. It can be invoked at any stage — before the written statement is filed, after issues, even during arguments — and the court can act on its own motion. In practice it is used in three recurring situations: a tenant who admits the tenancy and the landlord's title but disputes the rate of rent; a defendant who admits receipt of goods but disputes quality on part; and a partition defendant who admits the plaintiff's share but disputes the mode of division.

**(f) The admission must be clear, unequivocal and unconditional.** An admission hedged with a plea in avoidance is not an admission for Rule 6. "I received the money, but it was a loan repayable in 2030" is not an admission of a presently due debt. "I am a tenant, but I have deposited the rent" is an admission of tenancy and may found a decree for possession if the ground of eviction is independently admitted, but not otherwise.

**(g) A Rule 6 decree is a decree.** Sub-rule (2) says so, and requires it to be dated as of the judgment. It follows that a first appeal lies under section 96, that it is executable, and that it operates as res judicata on what it decides. It also follows that where the court **refuses** a Rule 6 application, the order is not a decree, is not appealable under section 96 or Order XLIII, and can be challenged only under Article 227 of the Constitution.

**(h) Partial decrees and the surviving suit.** Where the court decrees on the admitted part, the suit continues for the balance. Two consequences follow that practitioners frequently miss: the decree on the admitted part is immediately executable and does not await the final decree; and the court fee already paid on the whole plaint covers it, so no fresh fee is payable.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

::: tip Illustration 1 — the tenant who admits everything but the rate
A suit for eviction and arrears. The written statement admits that the defendant is the plaintiff's tenant, admits the tenancy commenced in 2016, admits that no rent has been paid since April 2022, but pleads that the agreed rent was ₹18,000 and not ₹35,000 as claimed, and denies that any valid notice of termination was served.

**Sword (plaintiff).** He applies under Rule 6 for a decree for arrears at the admitted rate of ₹18,000 per month for the admitted period of default, leaving the differential of ₹17,000 per month to be tried. The admission is clear, unconditional and severable, and no plea in avoidance touches it. He does **not** ask for possession, because the termination notice is denied and possession therefore depends on a fact in issue.

**Shield (defendant).** He resists on the ground that he has pleaded a set-off of ₹4.2 lakh spent on structural repairs which the landlord was liable to make, so that on his own case nothing is presently due. A pleaded set-off, if it is a legal set-off within Order VIII Rule 6, converts the admission into a conditional one and is the standard and usually effective answer to a Rule 6 application. If it is only an equitable set-off, he must be prepared to argue that it arises out of the same transaction.
:::

::: tip Illustration 2 — the notice to admit that funds a costs order
A suit on twenty-two invoices. The defendant denies each invoice generally. The plaintiff serves a notice under Rule 2 calling on the defendant to admit all twenty-two, annexing copies. The defendant does not reply.

**Sword (plaintiff).** Rule 2A operates: the documents are deemed admitted, saving just exceptions. He does not need to summon the delivery clerk, the transporter or the accounts officer. He files an affidavit under Rule 7 proving service and puts the deemed admission on record. He then applies under Rule 6 for a decree, and in the alternative under Rule 2 for the costs of proving the invoices, payable whatever the result.

**Shield (defendant).** He must move quickly. He applies for an extension of the seven-day period, files a reply specifically not admitting eleven of the invoices on the ground that the signatures of receipt are not those of any of his employees, and invokes the proviso to Rule 2A(1), asking the court to require the plaintiff to prove those eleven notwithstanding the deemed admission, for reasons to be recorded. He admits the other eleven, thereby confining the trial and avoiding a costs order on them.
:::

::: tip Illustration 3 — an admission in correspondence, not in the pleadings
A partition suit among three brothers. The written statement denies that the plaintiff has any share, pleading that he was given his share by a family arrangement in 2009. The plaintiff produces a letter written by the contesting defendant to a bank in 2021 describing the suit property as "our joint ancestral property held by the three brothers in equal shares".

**Sword (plaintiff).** Rule 6 covers admissions made "otherwise, whether orally or in writing". He applies for a preliminary decree declaring one-third shares, relying on the bank letter. The strength of the application is that the letter is the defendant's own document, is contemporaneous and is inconsistent with the pleaded family arrangement.

**Shield (defendant).** He resists on three fronts: that the letter is a statement made for a collateral purpose and is not an unequivocal admission of a subsisting right; that Rule 6's discretion should not be exercised where the admission is contradicted by a pleaded case that requires evidence; and that a partition suit turns on the state of title at the date of suit, so a 2021 description cannot displace a 2009 arrangement without a trial. He is likely to succeed, because Rule 6 is not a substitute for a trial where the admission is contested on its meaning.
:::

::: info Where the money is
Rule 6 is the cheapest decree in the Code. There is no additional court fee, because the fee on the plaint has already been paid on the whole claim. There is no evidence to lead and no witness expense. And because sub-rule (2) produces a decree bearing the date of the judgment, interest under section 34 runs and execution can be levied immediately, while the contested balance of the suit continues on the trial board. In a money suit where part is admitted, an application under Rule 6 filed on the day the written statement is received is almost always the highest-value step available.

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

#### *Uttam Singh Duggal & Co. Ltd. v. United Bank of India*, (2000) 7 SCC 120 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A bank sued on a guarantee; the defendant's pleadings and correspondence contained admissions of liability for a substantial part of the claim, and a decree was sought under Order XII Rule 6.

**Ratio decidendi:** The object of Rule 6 is to enable a party to obtain **speedy judgment** at least to the extent of the relief to which, on the admissions of the defendant, he is entitled. Where a claim is admitted, the court has jurisdiction to enter a judgment for the plaintiff and to pass a decree on the admitted claim. The rule should not be narrowly construed; the words "or otherwise" are wide enough to cover admissions made otherwise than in the pleadings, including in documents and correspondence. The provision is discretionary but the discretion must be exercised to advance, not defeat, the object of speedy relief on what is not in dispute.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Order XII Rule 6 is not confined to admissions in the pleadings; the expression 'or otherwise' extends to admissions in documents and correspondence, and where the admission is clear the plaintiff is entitled to a speedy judgment for the admitted portion without waiting for the trial of the balance."
:::

#### *Karam Kapahi v. Lal Chand Public Charitable Trust*, (2010) 4 SCC 753 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A lessee's admissions in pleadings and in an application relating to arrears were relied on to obtain a decree for possession under Order XII Rule 6.

**Ratio decidendi:** Rule 6 is an **enabling provision** intended to expedite trials and it must be given a liberal construction. The admission need not be in the pleadings alone; admissions may be constructive and may arise from a party's own applications and conduct in the proceedings. The court's power under the rule is discretionary and very wide, and the discretion must be exercised having regard to the object of the rule, which is to obviate a trial where there is nothing to try.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Under *Karam Kapahi*, admissions for the purposes of Order XII Rule 6 may be constructive and may be gathered from a party's own applications and conduct in the suit; the rule is enabling, is to be construed liberally, and is not confined to express admissions in a written statement."
:::

#### *Himani Alloys Ltd. v. Tata Steel Ltd.*, (2011) 15 SCC 273 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A decree was sought on an alleged admission of liability in a reconciliation statement, where the admission was disputed as to its scope and effect.

**Ratio decidendi:** Rule 6 uses the word "may", not "shall", and being an enabling provision it confers a **discretion, not a right**. A judgment on admission is not a matter of course. The court should exercise the power only where the admission is **clear, unambiguous and unconditional**, and should be **wary** of passing a decree where the admission is vague, is conditional, is coupled with a plea in avoidance, or requires the court to construe a document to determine whether an admission exists at all. In such cases the party should be relegated to a trial.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"An admission relied on under Order XII Rule 6 must be clear, unambiguous and unconditional; where the admission is conditional, is qualified by a plea in avoidance, or requires construction of a disputed document, the discretion under the rule should not be exercised and the parties must go to trial."
:::

#### *S.M. Asif v. Virender Kumar Bajaj*, (2015) 9 SCC 287 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A suit for specific performance in which the defendant admitted the execution of the agreement but pleaded that the plaintiff had failed to perform his part; a Rule 6 decree was sought.

**Ratio decidendi:** The words "may" and "as it may think fit" in Rule 6 indicate that the power is discretionary and cannot be claimed as of right. Where the defendant has raised objections which go to the root of the matter, the court should not exercise the discretion to pass a judgment on admissions. Admission of execution of a document is not, without more, an admission of the plaintiff's entitlement to the relief claimed.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Admission of the execution of an agreement is not an admission of entitlement to a decree; where the defendant raises objections going to the root of the claim, the discretion under Order XII Rule 6 must be declined and the suit tried."
:::

#### *Rajiv Ghosh v. Satya Naryan Jaiswal*, (2024) SCC OnLine SC 4477 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A tenant admitted the landlord–tenant relationship and default in an eviction suit; a decree was passed under Order XII Rule 6 and challenged as being an impermissible short-circuiting of the trial.

**Ratio decidendi:** Where the relationship of landlord and tenant and the default are admitted, and no triable issue survives on the ground of eviction pleaded, a decree under Order XII Rule 6 is not merely permissible but is the appropriate course. The rule exists precisely to prevent a defendant with no real defence from obtaining the delay that a full trial affords. The discretion is nonetheless judicial, and reasons must be recorded showing what admission is relied on and why it is unequivocal.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Where the tenancy and the default are admitted and no triable issue survives on the pleaded ground of eviction, the court should decree the suit under Order XII Rule 6 rather than put the landlord to the delay of a trial that can have only one outcome."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
*Himani Alloys* and *Uttam Singh Duggal* point in opposite directions on liberality and are routinely cited against each other. They are reconciled by the distinction between the **existence** of an admission and its **effect**: *Uttam Singh Duggal* and *Karam Kapahi* widen the sources from which an admission may be drawn; *Himani Alloys* and *S.M. Asif* insist that whatever its source, the admission must be unequivocal and unqualified. Cite the pair that fits your side, but be ready for the other. Recent citations are still being reported — check the current neutral citation before filing.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

::: danger Six ways an Order XII Rule 6 application fails
1. **The admission is conditional.** "Received, but as a loan"; "tenant, but rent deposited"; "executed, but under coercion". Any plea in avoidance attached to the admission defeats the application.
2. **A set-off or counter-claim is pleaded.** A legal set-off under Order VIII Rule 6 is a defence to the money claim itself and converts the admission into a conditional one. Deal with it in the application or expect a refusal.
3. **The admission needs construction.** If the court must interpret a letter, a balance sheet or a reconciliation statement to decide whether it admits anything, *Himani Alloys* applies and the court will decline.
4. **The admitted part is not severable.** A decree on part must be capable of standing alone. In a specific performance suit, admitted execution does not sever from disputed readiness and willingness.
5. **Asking for too much.** The commonest failure is asking for the whole claim on an admission that covers part. Ask for exactly what is admitted; the partial decree is what the rule is for.
6. **Filing after arguments are closed.** Rule 6 permits an application at any stage, but a court asked to decree on admissions after the trial has concluded will simply decide the suit.
:::

::: warning Strategic timing
- **File the Rule 6 application the week the written statement is served.** Every month of delay is a month the client is out of the admitted money, and delay invites the response that you were content to await the trial.
- **Serve the Rule 2 notice with the plaint or immediately after the written statement.** It costs nothing, the seven-day clock starts at once, and Rule 2A does the rest if the opponent is careless. Twenty-two invoices deemed admitted removes three witnesses from the trial.
- **Serve the Rule 4 notice to admit facts more than nine days before the hearing.** A notice served later carries no costs consequence at all — the condition precedent is jurisdictional to the sanction.
- **Reply to every notice to admit, even to refuse.** A specific non-admission with reasons preserves your position; silence engages Rule 2A and hands the other side a deemed admission plus a costs claim.
- **Record "saving all just exceptions" in every reply admitting documents.** It preserves relevance, admissibility and stamping objections that you will otherwise be taken to have waived.
- **If the Rule 6 application is refused, do not appeal.** There is no appeal. Move on with the trial, or file under Article 227 only if the refusal is perverse.
:::

>
> **AND FURTHER TAKE NOTICE** that in the event of the defendant refusing or neglecting to admit the said documents, the plaintiff will apply for and rely upon Order XII Rule 2A of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, and will seek the costs of proving the said documents against the defendant whatever the result of the suit may be.

## Order XIII — production, impounding and return of documents

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **Rule 1. Original documents to be produced at or before the settlement of issues.** — (1) The parties or their pleaders shall produce, on or before the settlement of issues, all the documentary evidence in original where the copies thereof have been filed along with plaint or written statement.
>
> (2) The Court shall receive the documents so produced:
>
> *Provided that they are accompanied by an accurate list thereof prepared in such form as the High Court directs.*
>
> (3) Nothing in sub-rule (1) shall apply to documents —
>
> *(a)* produced for the cross-examination of the witnesses of the other party; or
>
> *(b)* handed over to a witness merely to refresh his memory.

> **Rule 2. [Omitted by Act 46 of 1999.]**

> **Rule 3. Rejection of irrelevant or inadmissible documents.** — The Court may at any stage of the suit reject any document which it considers irrelevant or otherwise inadmissible, recording the grounds of such rejection.

> **Rule 4. Endorsements on documents admitted in evidence.** — (1) Subject to the provisions of the next following sub-rule, there shall be endorsed on every document which has been admitted in evidence in the suit the following particulars, namely: —
>
> *(a)* the number and title of the suit,
>
> *(b)* the name of the person producing the document,
>
> *(c)* the date on which it was produced, and
>
> *(d)* a statement of its having been so admitted;
>
> and the endorsement shall be signed or initialled by the Judge.
>
> (2) Where a document so admitted is an entry in a book, account or record, and a copy thereof has been substituted for the original under the next following rule, the particulars aforesaid shall be endorsed on the copy and the endorsement thereon shall be signed or initialled by the Judge.

> **Rule 5. Endorsements on copies of admitted entries in books, accounts and records.** — (1) Save in so far as is otherwise provided by the Bankers' Books Evidence Act, 1891, where a document admitted in evidence in the suit is an entry in a letter-book or a shop-book or other account in current use, the party on whose behalf the book or account is produced may furnish a copy of the entry.

> **Rule 6. Endorsements on documents rejected as inadmissible in evidence.** — Where a document relied on as evidence by either party is considered by the Court to be inadmissible in evidence, there shall be endorsed thereon the particulars mentioned in clauses (a), (b) and (c) of rule 4, sub-rule (1), together with a statement of its having been rejected, and the endorsement shall be signed or initialled by the Judge.

> **Rule 7. Recording of admitted and return of rejected documents.** — (1) Every document which has been admitted in evidence, or a copy thereof where a copy has been substituted for the original under rule 5, shall form part of the record of the suit.
>
> (2) Documents not admitted in evidence shall not form part of the record and shall be returned to the persons respectively producing them.

> **Rule 8. Court may order any document to be impounded.** — Notwithstanding anything contained in rule 5 or rule 7 of this Order or in rule 17 of Order VII, the Court may, if it sees sufficient cause, direct any document or book produced before it in any suit to be impounded and kept in the custody of an officer of the Court, for such period and subject to such conditions as the Court thinks fit.

> **Rule 9. Return of admitted documents.** — (1) Any person, whether a party to the suit or not, desirous of receiving back any document produced by him in the suit and placed on the record shall, unless the document is impounded under rule 8, be entitled to receive back the same, —
>
> *(a)* where the suit is one in which an appeal is not allowed, when the suit has been disposed of, and
>
> *(b)* where the suit is one in which an appeal is allowed, when the Court is satisfied that the time for preferring an appeal has elapsed and that no appeal has been preferred or, if an appeal has been preferred, when the appeal has been disposed of:
>
> *Provided that a document may be returned at any time earlier than that prescribed by this rule if the person applying therefor delivers to the proper officer a certified copy to be substituted for the original and undertakes to produce the original if required to do so.*
>
> (2) On the return of a document admitted in evidence, a receipt shall be given by the person receiving it.

> **Rule 10. Court may send for papers from its own records or from other Courts.** — (1) The Court may of its own motion, and may in its discretion upon the application of any of the parties to a suit, send for, either from its own records or from any other Court, the record of any other suit or proceeding, and inspect the same.
>
> (2) Every application made under this rule shall (unless the Court otherwise directs) be supported by an affidavit showing how the record is material to the suit in which the application is made, and that the applicant cannot without unreasonable delay or expense obtain a duly authenticated copy of the record or of such portion thereof as the applicant requires, or that the production of the original is necessary for the purposes of justice.
>
> (3) Nothing contained in this rule shall be deemed to enable the Court to use in evidence any document which under the law of evidence would be inadmissible in the suit.

#### Statutory mechanics

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| R. 1(1) — "**shall** produce, **on or before the settlement of issues**" | Mandatory, with a fixed cut-off | The documentary record closes when issues are framed |
| R. 1(1) — "in **original** where the copies thereof have been filed" | Obligation to produce originals | Photocopies filed under O. VII R. 14 must be backed by originals |
| R. 1(2), proviso — "**Provided that** they are accompanied by an accurate list" | Condition to receipt | A bundle without a list may be refused |
| R. 1(3)(a), (b) — cross-examination documents and memory-refreshers | Carve-out | You need not disclose the document you will spring in cross-examination |
| R. 3 — "**may at any stage** … reject … **recording the grounds**" | Discretionary, reasons mandatory | Relevance and admissibility are decided when the document is tendered |
| R. 4(1) — "**there shall be endorsed** … and … signed or initialled by the Judge" | Mandatory exhibiting formality | An unmarked document is not "admitted in evidence" |
| R. 6 — endorsement on rejected documents | Mandatory | Preserves the appellate record of what was excluded and why |
| R. 7(1) — admitted documents "**shall form part of the record**" | Mandatory | Only exhibited documents travel to the appellate court |
| R. 7(2) — documents not admitted "**shall not form part of the record**" | Prohibitory | The rejected document is returned; it cannot be relied on in appeal |
| R. 8 — "**Notwithstanding** anything contained in rule 5 or rule 7 … or in rule 17 of Order VII" | Non-obstante | Impounding overrides the right of return |
| R. 8 — "if it **sees sufficient cause**" | Discretionary, fact-dependent | Typically insufficiently stamped, forged or suspect documents |
| R. 9(1) — "shall, **unless the document is impounded** … be **entitled** to receive back" | Right, subject to impounding | The producer's entitlement is absolute once appeals are exhausted |
| R. 9(1), proviso — earlier return on a certified copy plus an undertaking | Enabling | The practical route to recovering an original title deed mid-suit |
| R. 10(2) — application "**shall** … be supported by an affidavit" | Condition precedent | Must show materiality and that a certified copy will not do |
| R. 10(3) — "Nothing … shall be deemed to enable the Court to use in evidence any document which … would be inadmissible" | Saving clause | Sending for a record does not make its contents evidence |

::: warning Order XIII Rule 1 and Order VII Rule 14 are two halves of one obligation
Order VII Rule 14 (and Order VIII Rule 1A for the defendant) requires a party to **file copies** of the documents he relies on **with his pleading**, and provides that a document not so filed "shall not be received in evidence on his behalf at the hearing of the suit" without leave. Order XIII Rule 1 then requires the **originals** to be produced on or before the settlement of issues. A party who filed copies with the plaint but never produced the originals has complied with neither obligation fully, and faces an objection at the moment of tendering. Check both boxes for every document.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**(a) The Order fixes when the documentary record closes.** The Code's scheme is that documents are **listed and copied with the pleadings** (Order VII Rule 14, Order VIII Rule 1A), **produced in original by the settlement of issues** (Order XIII Rule 1), **tendered, admitted or rejected during evidence** (Order XIII Rules 3, 4 and 6), and **returned or impounded after disposal** (Rules 8 and 9). Anything produced outside that sequence needs leave, and leave needs an explanation.

**(b) Two exceptions, and they are narrow.** Rule 1(3) permits a party to hold back documents produced (i) for the **cross-examination** of the opponent's witnesses and (ii) merely to **refresh a witness's memory**. The first is the important one: it means you are not obliged to disclose in advance the contradictory document you intend to put to the opponent's witness. But it does not permit you to withhold a document that forms part of your own affirmative case and then reintroduce it under the guise of cross-examination.

**(c) "Admitted in evidence" is a technical event, not a matter of the file.** A document becomes evidence only when it is tendered through a witness, its admissibility is ruled on, and it is endorsed and exhibited under Rule 4 with the judge's signature or initials. A document that sits in the file with a serial number in a list is not evidence. Practitioners lose cases on this: the document is on the record, everyone has read it, and nobody exhibited it.

**(d) Objections to admissibility and objections to mode of proof are different.** An objection that a document is **inadmissible** — unregistered where registration is compulsory, insufficiently stamped, hit by section 91 or 92 of the Evidence Act — can be taken at any stage, including in appeal, because no amount of consent can make inadmissible evidence admissible. An objection to the **mode of proof** — that a photocopy is being tendered without laying the foundation for secondary evidence, or that the document has not been proved by its executant — must be taken **when the document is tendered**, or it is waived. This distinction decides a very large number of appeals.

**(e) Rule 3 rejection versus a mere refusal to exhibit.** Rule 3 lets the court reject a document as irrelevant or inadmissible at any stage, on recorded grounds. Rule 6 requires the rejection to be endorsed. Rule 7(2) then removes the document from the record and returns it. The endorsement matters because the appellate court must be able to see what was excluded and on what ground; a document returned without a Rule 6 endorsement leaves an unreviewable gap.

**(f) Impounding under Rule 8 has a distinct fiscal purpose.** The commonest reason a court impounds a document is that it is **insufficiently stamped**. Section 33 of the Indian Stamp Act, 1899 obliges every person having authority to receive evidence to impound an instrument that is not duly stamped, and section 35 makes it inadmissible until the duty and penalty are paid. Order XIII Rule 8 supplies the procedural vehicle. The second reason is suspicion of **forgery or fabrication**, where the court impounds to preserve the document for expert examination or for a complaint under section 340 of the Criminal Procedure Code (now section 379 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023).

**(g) Getting your original back.** Rule 9 gives the producer an entitlement to the return of his original, but only after appeals are exhausted — which in practice can mean a decade. The proviso is the working solution: apply at any time, deposit a **certified copy** to be substituted on the record, and give an **undertaking to produce the original if required**. This is how a party recovers a sale deed, a will or a share certificate that he needs for a transaction while the suit drags on.

**(h) Rule 10 is for records, not for documents.** It empowers the court to send for the record of **another suit or proceeding**, from its own registry or from another court. It is not a substitute for discovery against a party or for a summons to produce against a stranger. And sub-rule (3) makes plain that calling for the record does not make anything in it evidence: the documents in the called-for record must still be proved and exhibited in the present suit.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

::: tip Illustration 1 — the original that never arrived
A suit on a promissory note. A photocopy is filed with the plaint under Order VII Rule 14. Issues are framed. At the plaintiff's evidence the original is tendered for the first time.

**Sword (plaintiff).** He tenders the original through his own affidavit of evidence and asks that it be exhibited. Faced with the objection, he applies for leave under Order XIII Rule 1 read with Order VII Rule 14(3), explaining that the original was in the custody of the firm's auditors and producing the auditor's letter. Leave to produce a document at a later stage is routinely granted where the explanation is credible and no prejudice is caused, and courts prefer substance to forfeiture on a promissory note.

**Shield (defendant).** He objects at the moment of tender — which is essential, because this is an objection to the mode of proof and is waived by silence. He submits that the original was in the plaintiff's own possession throughout, that no application for leave was made before the settlement of issues, and that the belated production deprives him of the opportunity to have the signature examined. In the alternative he asks that if the original is received, the suit be adjourned and he be given liberty to apply for a handwriting expert under Order XXVI Rule 10A, at the plaintiff's costs.
:::

::: tip Illustration 2 — the insufficiently stamped agreement to sell
A suit for specific performance. The agreement to sell recites delivery of possession to the purchaser. In the State concerned an agreement to sell coupled with possession is chargeable as a conveyance. The document bears ₹100 stamp.

**Sword (plaintiff).** He does not wait to be ambushed. He applies at the outset to have the document impounded under Order XIII Rule 8 and sent to the Collector under section 38 of the Stamp Act, tenders the deficit duty with the penalty, and obtains the certificate under section 42, whereupon the document becomes admissible for all purposes. Paying the penalty is far cheaper than losing the suit.

**Shield (defendant).** He takes the objection at the moment of tender, invoking section 35 of the Stamp Act: an instrument not duly stamped **shall not be admitted in evidence for any purpose**. He resists the plaintiff's attempt to read the recital of possession, and he presses that the impounding be to the Collector rather than a summary assessment by the trial court, because the Collector's assessment on a conveyance basis will be substantially higher. He also notes that unlike an admissibility objection under the Registration Act, the stamp objection cannot be cured by consent and can be taken at any stage.
:::

::: tip Illustration 3 — the will the plaintiff needs back
A partition suit filed in 2019. The plaintiff produced the original registered will of his father in 2020, and it was exhibited. In 2024 he needs the original to mutate revenue records and to complete a sale of an unrelated property that the will also covers.

**Sword (plaintiff).** He applies under the proviso to Order XIII Rule 9(1). He annexes a certified copy of the exhibited will for substitution on the record, files an undertaking that he will produce the original whenever required by the court or by the appellate court, and offers to produce it before the Registrar for comparison at his own cost. This is the standard and usually successful route.

**Shield (contesting defendant).** He resists on the ground that the genuineness of the will is the central issue in the suit, that the original must remain on the record for expert comparison, and asks in the alternative that if the original is released, it be released only for a fixed period against a personal undertaking of the plaintiff recorded in court, with the certified copy attested by the Registrar after comparison with the original in his presence.
:::

::: info The stamp penalty is not a formality
Under section 35 of the Indian Stamp Act, 1899 an instrument not duly stamped is inadmissible **for any purpose**, and section 36 provides that once admitted, admission cannot be questioned later at the same stage — which is why the objection must be taken the moment the document is tendered. Where the court impounds under Order XIII Rule 8 and the deficiency is made good with penalty, the document becomes admissible for all purposes. The penalty is commonly ten times the deficient duty and is a genuine commercial consideration in specific-performance and lease litigation. Note also the distinct rule for **unregistered** documents: section 49 of the Registration Act renders them inadmissible to affect immovable property or as evidence of a transaction requiring registration, but the proviso preserves their use as evidence of a **collateral transaction** — for example, to prove the nature of possession.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

#### *R.V.E. Venkatachala Gounder v. Arulmigu Viswesaraswami & V.P. Temple*, (2003) 8 SCC 752 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* Objection was taken in appeal to documents that had been marked and read at trial without objection, on the ground that they had not been properly proved.

**Ratio decidendi:** Objections to documentary evidence fall into **two classes**. The first is that the document itself is **inadmissible** — as being unstamped, unregistered where registration is compulsory, or excluded by law; such an objection goes to the root and can be raised at any stage, even in appeal, because no waiver can make inadmissible material admissible. The second concerns the **mode of proof** — for example the tendering of a photocopy without laying the foundation for secondary evidence; such an objection must be taken **when the document is tendered**, so that the party tendering it has the opportunity to cure the defect, and if not then taken it is **waived** and cannot be raised later.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"An objection to the mode of proof of a document must be taken at the time it is tendered in evidence, failing which it is waived; only an objection that the document is inadmissible in law — for want of stamp or registration, or by force of a statutory bar — survives to be taken in appeal."
:::

#### *Bipin Shantilal Panchal v. State of Gujarat*, (2001) 3 SCC 1 — 3-Judge Bench

*Facts:* Trials were being delayed by objections to the admissibility of documents being argued and decided as and when each document was tendered.

**Ratio decidendi:** When an objection is raised to the admissibility of a document, the trial court should ordinarily **mark the document tentatively as an exhibit subject to the objection**, record the objection, and **decide it at the final stage** in the judgment, rather than halt the trial to rule on it. This avoids fragmentation of the trial and repeated interlocutory challenges. The procedure does not dilute the substantive law of admissibility; it only defers the ruling.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Where admissibility is disputed, the proper course is for the document to be marked subject to objection and the objection decided in the final judgment; a party's objection is preserved by having it recorded, and the trial is not to be interrupted to decide it."
:::

#### *Omprakash v. Laxminarayan*, (2014) 1 SCC 618 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A suit for specific performance founded on an agreement to sell which was insufficiently stamped and unregistered, and which recited delivery of possession.

**Ratio decidendi:** An agreement to sell that records the delivery of possession attracts stamp duty as a conveyance in States where the Stamp Act has been so amended. Such an instrument, if not duly stamped, cannot be received in evidence for any purpose under section 35 of the Stamp Act until the duty and penalty are paid; but the bar is curable, and on payment under section 42 the document is admissible for all purposes. The bar under the Registration Act is distinct: an unregistered agreement may nonetheless be received in a suit for specific performance by force of the proviso to section 49 of the Registration Act.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The stamp objection is curable and the registration objection is not — an insufficiently stamped agreement becomes admissible for all purposes on payment of duty and penalty under section 42 of the Stamp Act, while an unregistered agreement is admissible in a specific performance suit only by force of the proviso to section 49 of the Registration Act."
:::

#### *Rasiklal Manikchand Dhariwal v. M.S.S. Food Products*, (2012) 2 SCC 196 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* An application to bring documents on record after the settlement of issues in a passing-off action.

**Ratio decidendi:** The requirement of Order XIII Rule 1 that documents be produced on or before the settlement of issues is designed to prevent surprise and to fix the record; leave to produce documents later is not to be granted as a matter of course, and the party seeking it must explain why the document was not produced earlier. The court will weigh the explanation against the prejudice to the other side and the relevance of the document.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Leave to produce a document after the settlement of issues under Order XIII Rule 1 must be supported by an explanation on affidavit for the earlier non-production; the discretion is not exercised merely because the document is relevant."
:::

#### *Sudir Engineering Co. v. Nitco Roadways Ltd.*, 1995 SCC OnLine Del 190 — Delhi High Court

*Facts:* A dispute over whether a document that had been filed and referred to but never formally exhibited could be treated as evidence.

**Ratio decidendi:** A document is not "admitted in evidence" merely because it is on the file or has been referred to in argument. It becomes evidence only when it is **tendered, proved, ruled admissible and endorsed** under Order XIII Rule 4 with the judge's signature. Marking a document with an exhibit number is not a mere formality; it is the record of a judicial decision that the document is admissible.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A document on the record that has not been tendered, proved and exhibited under Order XIII Rule 4 is not evidence, and no finding can be based on it however often it has been referred to in argument."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
*Sudir Engineering* is a High Court decision and is persuasive, not binding, outside Delhi — but the proposition it states is orthodox and is followed generally. Note also that the Evidence Act, 1872 has been replaced by the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023: the documentary-evidence provisions are renumbered (ss. 61–73 → ss. 56–72; secondary evidence, ss. 63–66 → ss. 58–60) and the BSA expressly brings electronic records within "primary evidence". Section 340 CrPC is now section 379 BNSS, 2023. Stamp duty is a State subject and the chargeability of an agreement to sell with possession varies — always check the Schedule to your State's Stamp Act.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

::: danger Six documentary failures that lose otherwise good cases
1. **The document was never exhibited.** It is in the file, both sides read it, counsel argued on it — and it was never tendered through a witness or endorsed under Rule 4. No finding can rest on it.
2. **The original never came.** Copies filed with the plaint, originals never produced by the settlement of issues, no leave sought. The objection at tender is fatal or at best expensive.
3. **The mode-of-proof objection was not taken at tender.** Under *R.V.E. Venkatachala Gounder* it is waived. Conversely: if you are the objector, object then, and get the objection recorded.
4. **Stamp duty was ignored until trial.** Section 35 makes an unstamped instrument inadmissible for any purpose. Impound early and pay; do not discover the problem in the witness box.
5. **The rejected document was not endorsed under Rule 6.** It is returned under Rule 7(2), never forms part of the record, and the appellate court cannot see what was excluded or why.
6. **Secondary evidence led without foundation.** A photocopy is admissible only after the conditions in section 65 (now section 60, BSA) are pleaded and proved. Lay the foundation in the chief-examination affidavit, not in reply to the objection.
:::

::: warning Strategic timing
- **Audit stamp and registration on every document the day you draft the plaint.** These are the only two objections that cannot be cured by consent or waived by silence, and both are entirely predictable.
- **Produce originals before the settlement of issues, not on the day of evidence.** Rule 1's cut-off is the point at which a court's sympathy for a late producer runs out.
- **Keep the cross-examination documents back.** Rule 1(3)(a) expressly permits it, and the contradiction that arrives unannounced is worth more than the one that has been in the file for two years.
- **If you must produce late, file the application with an affidavit explaining the delay before you tender.** Applying after the objection has been taken looks like an afterthought.
- **Ask for the *Bipin Panchal* course when admissibility is contested.** Marking subject to objection keeps the trial moving and preserves everyone's position; it also avoids an Article 227 petition against an interlocutory admissibility ruling.
- **Recover your originals under the Rule 9 proviso.** Do not leave a client's title deed on a court file for the life of an appeal when a certified copy plus an undertaking will do.
:::

## Order XIV — settlement of issues and determination of suit on issues of law

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **Rule 1. Framing of issues.** — (1) Issues arise when a material proposition of fact or law is affirmed by the one party and denied by the other.
>
> (2) Material propositions are those propositions of law or fact which a plaintiff must allege in order to show a right to sue or a defendant must allege in order to constitute his defence.
>
> (3) Each material proposition affirmed by one party and denied by the other shall form the subject of a distinct issue.
>
> (4) Issues are of two kinds: —
>
> *(a)* issues of fact,
>
> *(b)* issues of law.
>
> (5) At the first hearing of the suit the Court shall, after reading the plaint and the written statements, if any, and after examination under rule 2 of Order X and after hearing the parties or their pleaders, ascertain upon what material propositions of fact or of law the parties are at variance, and shall thereupon proceed to frame and record the issues on which the right decision of the case appears to depend.
>
> (6) Nothing in this rule requires the Court to frame and record issues where the defendant at the first hearing of the suit makes no defence.

> **Rule 2. Court to pronounce judgment on all issues.** — (1) Notwithstanding that a case may be disposed of on a preliminary issue, the Court shall, subject to the provisions of sub-rule (2), pronounce judgment on all issues.
>
> (2) Where issues both of law and of fact arise in the same suit, and the Court is of opinion that the case or any part thereof may be disposed of on an issue of law only, it may try that issue first if that issue relates to —
>
> *(a)* the jurisdiction of the Court, or
>
> *(b)* a bar to the suit created by any law for the time being in force,
>
> and for that purpose may, if it thinks fit, postpone the settlement of the other issues until after that issue has been determined, and may deal with the suit in accordance with the decision on that issue.

> **Rule 3. Materials from which issues may be framed.** — The Court may frame the issues from all or any of the following materials: —
>
> *(a)* allegations made on oath by the parties, or by any persons present on their behalf, or made by the pleaders of such parties;
>
> *(b)* allegations made in the pleadings or in answers to interrogatories delivered in the suit;
>
> *(c)* the contents of documents produced by either party.

> **Rule 4. Court may examine witnesses or documents before framing issues.** — Where the Court is of opinion that the issues cannot be correctly framed without the examination of some person not before the Court or without the inspection of some document not produced in the suit, it may adjourn the framing of the issues to a day not later than seven days from the date of framing preliminary issues and may (subject to any law for the time being in force) compel the attendance of any person or the production of any document by the person in whose possession or power it is by summons or other process.

> **Rule 5. Power to amend, and strike out, issues.** — (1) The Court may at any time before passing a decree amend the issues or frame additional issues on such terms as it thinks fit, and all such amendments or additional issues as may be necessary for determining the matters in controversy between the parties shall be so made or framed.
>
> (2) The Court may also, at any time before passing a decree, strike out any issues that appear to it to be wrongly framed or introduced.

#### Statutory mechanics

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| R. 1(1) — "**affirmed by the one party and denied by the other**" | Definition | No denial, no issue; an admitted proposition never becomes an issue |
| R. 1(2) — "**must allege in order to show a right to sue**" / "to constitute his defence" | Definition of materiality | Immaterial averments, however hotly denied, do not generate issues |
| R. 1(3) — "**shall** form the subject of a **distinct** issue" | Mandatory | Composite, rolled-up issues are a recurring ground of remand |
| R. 1(5) — "the Court **shall** … ascertain … **and shall thereupon proceed to frame and record**" | Mandatory duty on the judge | Framing is a judicial act, not a stenographic adoption of a draft |
| R. 1(5) — "**after reading** the plaint and the written statements … **and after examination under rule 2 of Order X**" | Condition precedent | Order X Rule 2 examination logically precedes framing |
| R. 1(5) — "on which the **right decision of the case** appears to depend" | Governing standard | The test is decisiveness, not completeness |
| R. 1(6) — no issues where "the defendant … makes **no defence**" | Carve-out | Explains why *ex parte* suits proceed without issues |
| R. 2(1) — "**Notwithstanding** that a case may be disposed of on a preliminary issue, the Court **shall** … pronounce judgment on **all** issues" | Non-obstante plus mandatory | The general rule after 1976: decide everything, so that an appellate reversal does not force a remand |
| R. 2(2) — "**Where issues both of law and of fact arise**" | Condition precedent | If the "preliminary" question needs evidence, R. 2(2) is unavailable |
| R. 2(2)(a), (b) — jurisdiction, or "**a bar to the suit created by any law**" | Exhaustive list of two | No other question may be tried as a preliminary issue |
| R. 3 — "**may** frame the issues from **all or any**" | Enabling | Pleadings, sworn allegations, answers to interrogatories, documents |
| R. 4 — "a day **not later than seven days**" | Fixed period | Adjournment to procure a person or document is short |
| R. 5(1) — "**at any time before passing a decree** … **shall** be so made or framed" | Wide power, mandatory where necessary | Additional issues can be framed even during final arguments |
| R. 5(2) — "**strike out** any issues … **wrongly framed or introduced**" | Corrective power | The remedy for an issue that has no foundation in the pleadings |

::: warning Rule 2 was inverted in 1976 — do not cite pre-Amendment practice
Before the Amendment Act of 1976, a court could try any issue of law as a preliminary issue and dispose of the suit on it. The present Rule 2(1) reverses that: the court **shall pronounce judgment on all issues** notwithstanding that the case might be disposed of on a preliminary one. Rule 2(2) preserves only **two** exceptions — jurisdiction, and a bar to the suit created by any law for the time being in force. The rationale is appellate economy: if the whole suit is decided, an appellate court that disagrees on the preliminary point can decide the appeal finally instead of remanding for a trial that never happened. A trial court that disposes of a suit on limitation as a "preliminary issue" where limitation depends on disputed facts is acting outside Rule 2(2), and the decree will be set aside.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**(a) What an issue actually is.** An issue is a question, arising from a material proposition that one side asserts and the other denies, whose answer the decision of the case depends on. Three elements must all be present: the proposition must be **material** (something a party must plead to make out his case or defence); it must be **affirmed by one and denied by the other**; and it must matter to the outcome. A fiercely contested but immaterial point produces no issue. An admitted material point produces no issue either.

**(b) Framing issues is the most consequential ten minutes in a civil suit.** Everything downstream is determined by it. Evidence is admissible only on matters covered by an issue. The burden of proof is fixed by who bears the affirmative on each issue. The judgment must, under Order XX Rule 5, state the finding and the reasons on **each separate issue**. An appellate court under Order XLI Rule 31 must record its own findings on the points for determination. A case that is badly issued is badly tried and, very often, remanded.

**(c) Burden of proof rides on the framing.** The party who bears the burden on an issue leads evidence first on it and loses it if the evidence is evenly balanced. Framing therefore silently allocates risk. "Whether the plaintiff proves that the agreement dated 4 June was executed by the defendant?" puts the burden on the plaintiff. "Whether the defendant proves that the agreement dated 4 June is forged?" puts it on the defendant. The same dispute, two framings, opposite consequences. Argue about the wording, not merely the subject.

**(d) The three defective framings.** (i) The **composite issue**, rolling several propositions into one — "Whether the plaintiff is entitled to possession and mesne profits and the suit is within limitation?" — makes distinct findings impossible and violates Rule 1(3). (ii) The **omnibus issue**, "Whether the plaintiff is entitled to the relief claimed?", which is a conclusion rather than a question and decides nothing. (iii) The **issue with no foundation in the pleadings**, which invites evidence on an unpleaded case and is liable to be struck out under Rule 5(2).

**(e) Preliminary issues: the two permitted subjects.** Rule 2(2) allows an issue of **law only** to be tried first, and only if it concerns (a) the **jurisdiction** of the court, or (b) a **bar to the suit created by any law for the time being in force**. The second category covers res judicata under section 11, the bar in Order II Rule 2, limitation where it appears on the face of the plaint, statutory bars such as section 80 CPC notice or a bar under a special Act, and the like. The crucial qualification is "issue of **law only**": if deciding the point requires evidence, it is a mixed issue and cannot be taken as a preliminary issue at all.

**(f) Limitation is the classic trap.** Whether a suit is within time is often a mixed question. If the plaint itself shows the suit to be barred, the point is one of law and can go under Rule 2(2)(b) — indeed the plaint may simply be rejected under Order VII Rule 11(d). But where the plaintiff pleads acknowledgment, part-payment, disability, or fraud postponing the running of time, the answer depends on evidence and cannot be tried preliminarily.

**(g) Rule 5 keeps the issues alive to the end.** Issues can be amended, added to, or struck out **at any time before the decree**. This matters in two directions. If evidence has been led and no issue covers it, apply under Rule 5(1) for an additional issue rather than argue that the evidence should be read on an unframed point — the appellate answer to that argument is that no party was on notice. Conversely, where your opponent has led evidence on an unpleaded case, object at the time and resist any late Rule 5 application on the ground of prejudice.

**(h) The absence of an issue is not always fatal.** Section 99 of the Code provides that no decree shall be reversed on account of any error, defect or irregularity in the proceedings not affecting the merits or the jurisdiction of the court. Where the parties knew the point was in controversy, led evidence on it and argued it, an appellate court will not remand merely because the issue was not formally framed. But the party who relies on that principle carries the burden of showing that no prejudice was caused.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

::: tip Illustration 1 — the framing fight in a specific performance suit
A suit for specific performance of an agreement to sell dated 4 June 2019. The written statement pleads (i) that the signature is forged; (ii) that if executed, it was a loan security and not an agreement to sell; (iii) that the plaintiff was never ready and willing; and (iv) that the suit is barred by limitation because the refusal was communicated in 2019 and the suit was filed in 2024.

**Sword (plaintiff).** He proposes: (1) Whether the plaintiff proves the execution of the agreement dated 4 June 2019? (2) Whether the defendant proves that the said agreement was intended as security for a loan? (3) Whether the plaintiff proves that he has been ready and willing to perform his part of the agreement throughout? (4) Whether the suit is within limitation? (5) Relief. He deliberately frames issue 2 to put the burden of the loan plea on the defendant, since it is a plea in confession and avoidance. He resists limitation being taken as a preliminary issue, because the date of refusal is disputed and Article 54 depends on it.

**Shield (defendant).** He presses for issue 1 to be split: execution and genuineness of signature as one issue, and the plea of forgery as a separate issue so that he may apply for expert comparison. He asks that the readiness-and-willingness issue be framed to require the plaintiff to prove **continuous** readiness, tracking section 16(c) of the Specific Relief Act, 1963. He applies under Rule 2(2)(b) for limitation to be tried as a preliminary issue, relying on the plaint's own averment that refusal was communicated on 12 August 2019 — if that averment stands, the point is one of law on the face of the pleadings.
:::

::: tip Illustration 2 — a preliminary issue on res judicata that succeeds
A suit for partition of a house. The defendant pleads that the same plaintiff sued for partition of the same house in 2014, that the suit was dismissed on the merits, and that the present suit is barred by section 11. He files a certified copy of the earlier plaint, written statement, issues, judgment and decree.

**Sword (defendant).** He applies under Rule 2(2)(b) for a preliminary issue: "Whether the suit is barred by the principle of res judicata under section 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908?" Res judicata is a bar to the suit created by a law in force. Because the earlier record is before the court in certified copy and the identity of parties, property and issues appears from that record alone, no evidence is required and the point is one of law only. This is the paradigm case for Rule 2(2).

**Shield (plaintiff).** He resists on the only ground that works: that the question is not one of law **only**. He pleads that the earlier suit concerned a different portion of the property and that the cause of action in the present suit arose from a subsequent partition by metes and bounds in 2018, so that whether the matter was "directly and substantially in issue" in the former suit requires evidence about the identity of the properties. If he establishes that, Rule 2(2) is unavailable and the issue must go to trial with everything else.
:::

::: tip Illustration 3 — the additional issue framed during final arguments
An eviction suit tried on the ground of bona fide requirement. At final arguments the landlord's counsel realises that although the pleadings and evidence cover the tenant's acquisition of an alternative accommodation — which is a distinct statutory ground — no issue was framed on it.

**Sword (landlord).** He applies under Rule 5(1) for an additional issue, pointing to the specific paragraph of the plaint pleading the alternative accommodation, the tenant's denial in the written statement, and the cross-examination in which the tenant admitted the purchase. Rule 5(1) permits framing "at any time before passing a decree" and directs that all issues necessary for determining the matters in controversy "shall be so framed". Where the point is pleaded, denied and covered by evidence, the application will usually be allowed.

**Shield (tenant).** He resists on prejudice: that he did not lead evidence directed to that ground because no issue covered it, that his cross-examination admission was on a collateral matter, and that if the issue is framed he must be given an opportunity to lead further evidence and recall the landlord's witness. That is the realistic outcome — courts allow the issue but reopen evidence, and the landlord pays the costs of the exercise.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

#### *Makhanlal Bangal v. Manas Bhunia*, (2001) 2 SCC 652 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* An election petition was tried and decided without issues being properly framed on the pleaded grounds, and the judgment was challenged for that reason.

**Ratio decidendi:** The framing of issues is an **important stage** in the trial and casts a **duty on the court** which cannot be discharged mechanically. The court must read the pleadings, ascertain the material propositions on which the parties are at variance, and frame distinct issues. Recording of evidence and the entire course of the trial are shaped by the issues; a trial conducted without proper issues is liable to result in a miscarriage of justice, and the appellate court may remand for a fresh trial where the omission has caused prejudice.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The framing of issues under Order XIV Rule 1(5) is a judicial duty requiring the court to read the pleadings and ascertain the material propositions in variance; a trial conducted on issues that do not reflect the pleaded controversy is liable to be set aside as having occasioned a failure of justice."
:::

#### *Ramesh B. Desai v. Bipin Vadilal Mehta*, (2006) 5 SCC 638 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A suit was sought to be disposed of on limitation as a preliminary issue, where the question of when time began to run depended on disputed facts.

**Ratio decidendi:** Order XIV Rule 2(2) as it now stands permits an issue to be tried as a preliminary issue **only where it is an issue of law only**, and only where it relates to jurisdiction or to a bar to the suit created by any law. A **mixed question of law and fact cannot be tried as a preliminary issue**. Whether a suit is barred by limitation is frequently a mixed question, and where the answer depends on facts requiring evidence, the issue must be tried with the rest of the suit.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A plea of limitation may be tried as a preliminary issue under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(b) only where the bar appears from the plaint itself; where the date from which time runs is disputed, the question is mixed and must be tried along with all other issues."
:::

#### *Nusli Neville Wadia v. Ivory Properties*, (2020) 6 SCC 557 — 3-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A reference to a larger Bench on whether limitation could be decided as a preliminary issue under Order XIV Rule 2.

**Ratio decidendi:** Under the amended Order XIV Rule 2, the court is bound to pronounce judgment on all issues; the trial of a preliminary issue is confined to an issue of **law only** relating to jurisdiction or a statutory bar. A question of limitation may be decided as a preliminary issue **only if it can be decided on admitted facts** without recording evidence. Where the plea requires investigation of facts, it must be tried with the other issues, and the court has no jurisdiction to dispose of the suit upon it preliminarily.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Following *Nusli Neville Wadia*, a preliminary issue under Order XIV Rule 2(2) can be entertained only where it can be answered on admitted facts and pure law; if any disputed fact must be found, the court is denuded of the power to try it preliminarily."
:::

#### *Sayeda Akhtar v. Abdul Ahad*, (2003) 7 SCC 52 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A suit was decided without an issue having been framed on a plea that had nonetheless been pleaded, evidenced and argued.

**Ratio decidendi:** The omission to frame an issue is an irregularity, and its effect must be judged by whether it has caused **prejudice**. Where the parties knew the question was in controversy, led evidence upon it and addressed the court, the absence of a formal issue does not vitiate the decree, and section 99 of the Code protects it. Where, on the other hand, a party was misled into not leading evidence, the trial is vitiated and a remand or a reference under Order XLI Rule 25 is appropriate.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"An omission to frame an issue vitiates the decree only if it has caused prejudice; where the point was pleaded, evidence was led on it and it was argued, section 99 of the Code saves the decree notwithstanding the absence of a formal issue."
:::

#### *Kalyan Singh Chouhan v. C.P. Joshi*, (2011) 11 SCC 786 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* Findings were recorded on matters that were not covered by the pleadings and on which no issue had been framed.

**Ratio decidendi:** A court cannot record a finding on a matter which is neither pleaded nor covered by an issue. Evidence led on a plea not raised in the pleadings is to be ignored, since the object of pleadings and issues is to give the opposite party notice of the case he must meet. No amount of evidence can be looked into upon a plea that was never put forward.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"No amount of evidence can be looked into on a plea that was neither pleaded nor made the subject of an issue; a finding recorded on such a plea is without foundation and must be set aside."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Judgments delivered before the Amendment Act of 1976 on preliminary issues — permitting any issue of law to dispose of the suit — no longer reflect Rule 2 and are routinely and wrongly cited. Check the date of any authority relied on for a preliminary-issue argument. In commercial suits, Order XV-A of the Commercial Courts Act's Schedule substitutes a case management hearing at which issues, the number of witnesses and the trial calendar are all fixed together, and the practice under it differs materially from ordinary Order XIV practice.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

::: danger Six framing errors and what they cost
1. **The rolled-up issue.** "Whether the plaintiff is entitled to possession, mesne profits and damages?" makes it impossible to record separate findings under Order XX Rule 5 and invites a remand. Insist on Rule 1(3): one material proposition, one issue.
2. **The omnibus relief issue standing alone.** "Whether the plaintiff is entitled to the relief claimed?" is a conclusion, not an issue. It is a legitimate final issue, but it cannot carry the case.
3. **Accepting the opponent's burden allocation.** The wording of the issue decides who leads and who loses on an even balance. Argue the wording.
4. **Pressing limitation as a preliminary issue when the dates are disputed.** *Ramesh B. Desai* and *Nusli Neville Wadia* will defeat it, and you will have lost six months.
5. **Leading evidence on an unpleaded case.** Under *Kalyan Singh Chouhan* it will be ignored however compelling. Amend the pleading under Order VI Rule 17 first — and after trial has begun, you must satisfy the due-diligence proviso.
6. **Discovering a missing issue only in appeal.** Rule 5(1) lets you fix it any time before the decree. In appeal you are reduced to seeking a remand under Order XLI Rule 23A or a finding under Rule 25, both of which cost years.
:::

::: warning Strategic timing
- **Draft your issues before the hearing and hand them up.** A judge with a competing draft in front of him engages with your framing; a judge without one frames his own and you argue afterwards.
- **Frame the burden where you want it.** Every plea in confession and avoidance — payment, discharge, adverse possession, a family arrangement, a loan-not-sale plea — should be framed as an issue the **defendant** must prove.
- **Get Order X done first.** Rule 1(5) contemplates framing after the Order X Rule 2 examination, and admissions recorded there remove issues before they are born.
- **Ask for a preliminary issue only on jurisdiction or a true statutory bar, and only on admitted facts.** Anything else invites the *Nusli Neville Wadia* answer.
- **If the bar appears on the face of the plaint, prefer Order VII Rule 11 to a preliminary issue.** Rejection under Rule 11 is a decree, is appealable, and disposes of the suit without a trial.
- **Move under Rule 5(1) the moment you notice a gap** — before evidence closes if possible, since an application at final arguments will usually be allowed only on terms that reopen evidence at your cost.
:::

**Model draft issues (money suit with a set-off), to be handed up at the first hearing:**

> 1. Whether the plaintiff proves that the defendant placed the purchase orders dated \_\_\_\_ and received the goods covered by invoices Nos. \_\_\_\_ to \_\_\_\_?
> 2. Whether the plaintiff proves that a sum of ₹\_\_\_\_ is due and payable by the defendant on the said invoices?
> 3. Whether the defendant proves that the goods supplied under invoices Nos. \_\_\_\_ and \_\_\_\_ were defective, and if so, with what effect on the plaintiff's claim?
> 4. Whether the defendant proves the legal set-off of ₹\_\_\_\_ pleaded in paragraph \_\_\_\_ of the written statement?
> 5. Whether the plaintiff is entitled to interest, and if so, at what rate and for what period?
> 6. Whether the suit is within limitation?
> 7. Relief and costs.

**Model prayer — application for a preliminary issue under Order XIV Rule 2(2):**

> It is respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to:
>
> **(a)** frame the following as a preliminary issue under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(b) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908: *"Whether the present suit is barred by the principle of res judicata under section 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, having regard to the judgment and decree dated \_\_\_\_ passed in Civil Suit No. \_\_\_\_ of \_\_\_\_ by the Court of \_\_\_\_?"*;
>
> **(b)** record that the said issue is an issue of law only, capable of determination upon the admitted pleadings and the certified copies of the record of the said earlier suit filed herewith, without the recording of any oral evidence;
>
> **(c)** postpone the settlement of the remaining issues until after the determination of the said preliminary issue, as permitted by Order XIV Rule 2(2);
>
> **(d)** pass such further or other order as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit and proper.

| # | Framing checklist | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | List every material proposition in the plaint and every traverse in the written statement | R. 1(1) and (2) — the raw material |
| 2 | Delete admitted propositions | An admitted proposition is not an issue; it may found an O. XII R. 6 decree instead |
| 3 | Delete immaterial propositions however hotly denied | R. 1(2) — materiality is the filter |
| 4 | One proposition, one issue | R. 1(3); enables findings under O. XX R. 5 |
| 5 | Word each issue so the burden falls where you want it | Decides who leads and who loses on an even balance |
| 6 | Frame every plea in avoidance as the defendant's issue | Payment, discharge, adverse possession, family arrangement |
| 7 | Check whether any issue is a pure question of law on admitted facts | The only gateway to R. 2(2) |
| 8 | Add a limitation issue and a relief issue | Both are routinely omitted and both are required |
| 9 | Read your issues against the reliefs claimed in the prayer | Every relief must be supported by a findable issue |
| 10 | Re-read the issues when evidence closes and move under R. 5(1) if anything is missing | The last cheap opportunity to fix the framing |

## Order XV — disposal of the suit at the first hearing

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **Rule 1. Parties not at issue.** — Where at the first hearing of a suit it appears that the parties are not at issue on any question of law or of fact, the Court may at once pronounce judgment.

> **Rule 2. One of several defendants not at issue.** — (1) Where there are more defendants than one, and any one of the defendants is not at issue with the plaintiff on any question of law or of fact, the Court may at once pronounce judgment for or against such defendant and the suit shall proceed only against the other defendants.
>
> (2) Whenever a judgment is pronounced under this rule, a decree shall be drawn up in accordance with the judgment, and the decree shall bear the date on which the judgment was pronounced.

> **Rule 3. Parties at issue.** — (1) Where the parties are at issue on some question of law or of fact, and issues have been framed by the Court as hereinbefore provided, if the Court is satisfied that no further argument or evidence than the parties can at once adduce is required upon such of the issues as may be sufficient for the decision of the suit, and that no injustice will result from proceeding with the suit forthwith, the Court may proceed to determine such issues, and, if the finding thereon is sufficient for the decision, may pronounce judgment accordingly, whether the summons has been issued for the settlement of issues only or for the final disposal of the suit:
>
> *Provided that where the summons has been issued for the settlement of issues only, the parties or their pleaders are present and none of them objects.*
>
> (2) Where the finding is not sufficient for the decision, the Court shall postpone the further hearing of the suit, and shall fix a day for the production of such further evidence, or for such further argument as the case requires.

> **Rule 4. Failure to produce evidence.** — Where the summons has been issued for the final disposal of the suit and either party fails without sufficient cause to produce the evidence on which he relies, the Court may at once pronounce judgment, or may, if it thinks fit, after framing and recording issues, adjourn the suit for the production of such evidence as may be necessary for its decision upon such issues.

#### Statutory mechanics

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| R. 1 — "**not at issue on any question of law or of fact**" | Condition precedent | Absolute: any genuine dispute, however small, removes the power |
| R. 1 — "the Court **may at once** pronounce judgment" | Discretionary | Even where there is nothing at issue, the court is not compelled |
| R. 2(1) — "**any one** of the defendants is not at issue" | Enabling for severable defendants | Judgment for or against one; the suit continues against the rest |
| R. 2(2) — "a decree **shall** be drawn up" | Mandatory | The R. 2 judgment produces an appealable decree |
| R. 3(1) — "**no further argument or evidence than the parties can at once adduce is required**" | Condition precedent | Both sides must be able to close today |
| R. 3(1) — "and that **no injustice will result**" | Second condition precedent | An independent safeguard, not a restatement of the first |
| R. 3(1) — "upon such of the issues as **may be sufficient for the decision**" | Limiting | The court decides only the dispositive issues |
| R. 3(1), proviso — "the parties or their pleaders are present and **none of them objects**" | Condition on a settlement-of-issues summons | A single objection defeats the power |
| R. 3(2) — "the Court **shall** postpone the further hearing … **and shall fix a day**" | Mandatory | The suit cannot simply be dismissed for insufficiency of the finding |
| R. 4 — "**fails without sufficient cause** to produce the evidence" | Condition precedent | Sufficient cause is a complete answer |
| R. 4 — "**may at once pronounce judgment, or may** … adjourn" | Two-limbed discretion | The alternative to judgment is a further opportunity, not dismissal |

::: warning Rule 4 and Order XVII Rule 3 are cousins, and the difference matters
Order XV Rule 4 operates at the **first hearing**, where the summons was issued for final disposal and a party fails without sufficient cause to produce his evidence. Order XVII Rule 3 operates **later**, where a party to whom time has been granted fails to produce evidence or perform any other act necessary to the progress of the suit. Both permit the court to decide the suit forthwith on the material before it. The distinction has consequences for the remedy: a decree under Order XV Rule 4 or under Order XVII Rule 3 where the party was **present** is a decree on the merits, appealable under section 96 and **not** liable to be set aside under Order IX Rule 13; whereas if the party was **absent**, Order XVII Rule 2 applies and the Order IX remedies revive.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**(a) Order XV is the Code's summary-disposal chapter, and it is undeservedly forgotten.** The Order occupies three pages and is rarely invoked, largely because Order XII Rule 6 and Order VII Rule 11 have taken over the ground. But it does something neither of those does: it permits the court to **decide the whole suit** at the first hearing where the pleadings disclose no dispute at all, or where the only issues that matter can be decided on what the parties can put before the court that day.

**(b) Rule 1 requires the absence of any dispute whatsoever.** "Not at issue on any question of law or of fact" is an exacting test. It is satisfied where a defendant files a written statement that admits everything and pleads only for time, where the defence raises a plea that is no defence in law, or where a formal defendant has nothing to say. Any genuine dispute — even one that the court thinks the defendant will lose — takes the case out of Rule 1 and into a trial.

**(c) Rule 2 is the underused one.** In multi-defendant suits there is very often one defendant who is not really contesting: the bank that holds the mortgage and takes no position on the family dispute; the formal defendant joined because he is a co-sharer and consents; the government department joined for the record. Rule 2 permits judgment for or against that defendant at once, and the suit then proceeds only against the contesting defendants. This shortens the trial and removes a party from the witness list, and sub-rule (2) makes the resulting decree a real, appealable decree.

**(d) Rule 3 is not about the absence of issues but about the sufficiency of one.** Issues have been framed; there is a genuine dispute; but the court can see that one issue is dispositive and that both sides can argue and evidence it today. The court decides that issue and, if the finding disposes of the suit, gives judgment. The classic case is a suit that turns on the construction of a written document with no extrinsic evidence in play, or on an admitted set of dates.

**(e) The proviso protects the party who came expecting only a framing hearing.** Where the summons was issued **for the settlement of issues only**, a party may reasonably have come without his witnesses. Rule 3's proviso therefore requires both that the parties or pleaders be present and that **none of them objects**. A single objection ends the matter, and the objection need not be justified.

**(f) Two independent safeguards in Rule 3.** The court must be satisfied both that no further evidence or argument is needed **and** that "no injustice will result from proceeding with the suit forthwith". These are separate. A case can satisfy the first and fail the second — for example where a party's counsel is a substitute, or where the decisive issue was framed for the first time that morning.

**(g) Rule 4 is not a dismissal power.** Where a party fails without sufficient cause to produce his evidence at a final-disposal hearing, the court may "at once pronounce judgment" — meaning judgment on the material actually before it, which may be for or against that party depending on where the burden lies. Or it may frame issues and adjourn. What it may not do is dismiss the suit for default; the plaintiff who is present but unready is not absent, and Order IX has no application to him.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

::: tip Illustration 1 — the formal defendant disposed of under Rule 2
A partition suit among four siblings. The second defendant is a nationalised bank holding an equitable mortgage over one of the properties. Its written statement says only that it holds a subsisting mortgage for ₹62 lakh, gives particulars, and submits that any decree be made subject to its charge. The plaintiff admits the mortgage.

**Sword (plaintiff).** He applies under Rule 2(1) for a judgment declaring that any partition decree shall be subject to the bank's subsisting charge, and for the bank to be discharged from further attendance. The bank's officer is thereby removed from the witness list, and the suit proceeds only between the siblings.

**Shield (contesting sibling).** He objects that the extent of the mortgage debt is disputed as between the siblings, because he contends that the borrowing was for the plaintiff's personal business and must be charged to the plaintiff's share alone. That is a dispute between co-sharers, not with the bank, so the correct order is judgment under Rule 2 in favour of the bank on the existence and priority of its charge, expressly leaving open the inter-se question of which share bears the debt.
:::

::: tip Illustration 2 — Rule 3 disposal on the construction of a document
A suit for a declaration that a notice terminating a distributorship is invalid. The pleadings admit the agreement, admit the notice, admit the dates and admit service. The whole dispute is whether clause 14 required ninety days' notice or thirty.

**Sword (plaintiff).** He submits that the only issue is the construction of clause 14, that construction is a question of law on an admitted document, that no extrinsic evidence is admissible under section 92 of the Evidence Act, and that both sides can argue it today. He invokes Rule 3(1) and asks the court to determine that issue and pronounce judgment.

**Shield (defendant).** He resists on the second limb: that he pleads a consistent course of dealing under which thirty-day notices had been given and accepted on four previous occasions, which is admissible under the proviso to section 92 to explain the term, and that evidence of that course of dealing is necessary. If he establishes that, Rule 3(1)'s first condition fails and the suit must be tried. If he cannot point to a pleaded course of dealing, the Rule 3 disposal will follow.
:::

::: tip Illustration 3 — Rule 4 against the plaintiff who came empty-handed
Summons was issued for the final disposal of a small-cause style money suit. On the date fixed the plaintiff appears by counsel but has brought neither the original promissory note nor any witness, and offers no explanation beyond that the file is with his previous advocate.

**Sword (defendant).** He invokes Rule 4 and asks the court to pronounce judgment at once on the material before it. Since the burden of proving the note is on the plaintiff and there is no evidence, judgment would be one dismissing the suit — a dismissal **on the merits**, which is a decree, which bars a fresh suit by res judicata and is challengeable only in appeal, not by an application under Order IX Rule 9.

**Shield (plaintiff).** He must establish sufficient cause. He files an affidavit annexing the correspondence with his former advocate and the *vakalatnama* change, and asks the court to exercise the second limb of Rule 4 — to frame issues and adjourn for evidence — offering costs. Courts almost always take that course on a first occasion, but the plaintiff should understand what he narrowly avoided: a dismissal under Rule 4 is not curable under Order IX.
:::

::: info Why Order XV matters even though it is rarely used
Order XII Rule 6 gives you a decree on what is **admitted**. Order VII Rule 11 gives you rejection where the **plaint** is defective. Order XXXVII gives you a summary decree in a defined class of **money** suits. Order XV fills the remaining gap: the suit in which nothing is admitted in terms, the plaint is unimpeachable, the claim is not one for a liquidated sum on a written instrument — and yet there is genuinely nothing to try. It also supplies the only clean route to disposing of a **non-contesting co-defendant** without waiting for the final decree.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

#### *Prem Bakshi v. Dharam Dev*, (2002) 2 SCC 2 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A suit was disposed of at an early stage on the footing that no triable dispute survived on the pleadings.

**Ratio decidendi:** A court may pronounce judgment at the first hearing only where, on a fair reading of the pleadings, the parties are genuinely not at issue on any question of law or of fact. The power is not to be used to shut out a defence that raises a triable question merely because the court considers the defence weak. The strength of a defence is a matter for trial; its **existence** is what Order XV Rule 1 is concerned with.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Order XV Rule 1 is engaged by the absence of any triable question, not by the weakness of the defence; a plea that raises a question of fact or law, however unpromising, takes the suit out of the rule."
:::

#### *Arjun Singh v. Mohindra Kumar*, AIR 1964 SC 993 — 3-Judge Bench

*Facts:* The Court examined the distinction between a party's absence and a present party's failure to proceed, and the consequences for the remedies available.

**Ratio decidendi:** Where a party is **absent**, the consequences flow from Order IX and the remedies of restoration and setting aside are available. Where a party is **present but does not proceed** — does not produce evidence, does not conduct the case — the court proceeds under the provisions dealing with default in the conduct of the suit, and the resulting decision is a decision on the merits. The character of the order determines the remedy: an *ex parte* decree may be set aside under Order IX Rule 13, but a decree passed after the party has appeared and failed to prosecute is appealable only.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A decree passed where the party was present but failed to produce evidence is a decree on the merits, remediable only by appeal under section 96; the Order IX remedies are confined to cases of non-appearance."
:::

#### *Ishwar Bhai C. Patel v. Harihar Behera*, (1999) 3 SCC 457 — 2-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A judgment was given on the basis of admissions contained in the defendant's own pleading and documents at an early stage of the suit.

**Ratio decidendi:** Where the defendant's pleadings contain admissions sufficient to dispose of the claim, the court may give judgment without putting the plaintiff to proof of what is admitted. The Code's early-disposal provisions are to be used purposively to avoid the expense and delay of proving what nobody disputes.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Where the pleadings themselves leave nothing in controversy, the court should give judgment at once rather than require formal proof of admitted matters; the Code does not contemplate a trial of undisputed facts."
:::

#### *Salem Advocate Bar Association, Tamil Nadu v. Union of India (II)*, (2005) 6 SCC 344 — 3-Judge Bench

*Facts:* A general challenge to the amended Code, in the course of which the Court considered the early-disposal and case-management provisions.

**Ratio decidendi:** The provisions of the Code enabling early disposal are to be worked so as to secure expeditious justice, and courts should actively narrow suits at the earliest stage rather than allow every case to travel a full trial. Procedural provisions are the handmaid of justice and should be construed to advance, not obstruct, the disposal of what is genuinely undisputed.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The amended Code requires the trial court to take active steps at the first hearing to determine what is genuinely in dispute and to dispose of what is not; a mechanical progression to trial defeats the scheme of Orders X, XII, XIV and XV."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Order XV has been amended by several States — Uttar Pradesh in particular inserted **Rule 5**, requiring a defendant in a suit for eviction against a tenant to deposit the admitted rent and continue monthly deposits, failing which the defence may be struck out. That rule does not exist in the Code as it applies elsewhere. Always check your State amendments to Order XV before relying on or resisting a rule number in this Order.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

::: danger Five ways an Order XV application fails
1. **Any triable plea at all.** Rule 1 is defeated by the existence of a dispute, not by its merit. If the written statement raises one, use Order XII Rule 6 for the admitted part instead.
2. **Objecting too late under the Rule 3 proviso.** Where the summons was for settlement of issues only, a single objection defeats a Rule 3 disposal — but the objection must be taken there and then, not in appeal.
3. **Treating Rule 4 as a dismissal-for-default power.** A plaintiff present but unready is not absent; the court decides on the material before it, and the resulting dismissal is on the merits.
4. **Assuming an Order IX remedy after a Rule 4 judgment.** There is none. *Arjun Singh* is clear: presence plus failure to prosecute equals a merits decree, appealable only.
5. **Overlooking a State amendment.** Arguing Order XV Rule 5 outside Uttar Pradesh, or ignoring it inside, is a familiar and expensive error.
:::

::: warning Strategic timing
- **Sweep the defendant list at the first hearing.** Every formal, consenting or non-contesting defendant is a Rule 2 candidate, and removing one shortens the trial and the witness list immediately.
- **Ask for Rule 3 only where the decisive issue is one of construction or of admitted dates.** If any oral evidence is genuinely needed, the application will fail on the first limb and you will have shown your hand.
- **If you are served with a summons for final disposal, come ready.** Rule 4 attaches on the first date and sufficient cause is not always found.
- **If you are unready, file the affidavit of sufficient cause before the case is called.** Explaining afterwards, from the bar, converts a routine adjournment into a contested application.
- **Where you want the suit tried, object under the Rule 3 proviso.** You need no reason, and the proviso gives you an absolute veto where the summons was for settlement of issues only.
:::

**Model prayer — application under Order XV Rule 2:**

> It is respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to:
>
> **(a)** pronounce judgment under Order XV Rule 2(1) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 in respect of Defendant No. \_\_\_\_, the said defendant not being at issue with the plaintiff on any question of law or of fact, and there being no dispute between the plaintiff and the said defendant as to the existence, extent or priority of the charge pleaded in paragraph \_\_\_\_ of its written statement;
>
> **(b)** direct that a decree be drawn up in accordance with the said judgment under Order XV Rule 2(2), bearing the date of the judgment, declaring that any decree passed in this suit shall be subject to the subsisting charge of Defendant No. \_\_\_\_;
>
> **(c)** direct that the suit do proceed only against Defendants Nos. \_\_\_\_ and \_\_\_\_, and that Defendant No. \_\_\_\_ be discharged from further attendance;
>
> **(d)** expressly leave open all questions arising *inter se* between the plaintiff and Defendants Nos. \_\_\_\_ and \_\_\_\_ as to the incidence of the said charge upon their respective shares;
>
> **(e)** pass such further or other order as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit and proper.

|---|---|---|
| 1 | Read the written statement for any triable plea before invoking Rule 1 | Existence, not merit, is the test |
| 2 | Identify every non-contesting defendant | R. 2 removes them and shortens the trial |
| 3 | Ask whether any single issue disposes of the suit | The gateway to R. 3 |
| 4 | Check what the summons was issued for | The R. 3 proviso applies only to settlement-of-issues summonses |
| 5 | If you want a trial, object under the proviso at once | An unreasoned objection is enough |

| 7 | Come to a final-disposal hearing with originals and witnesses | R. 4 attaches on the first date |

## Chapter consolidation — Orders XII to XV together

These four Orders are the Code's answer to a single question: **how much of this suit actually needs to be tried?** They apply, in sequence, four different filters.

| Filter | Order | What it removes | What it produces |
|---|---|---|---|
| What is admitted | XII RR. 1–5, 2A | Facts and documents nobody disputes | Deemed admissions; costs of proof shifted |
| What can be decreed on admission | **XII R. 6** | The admitted part of the claim | An immediately executable partial decree |
| What documents exist | XIII RR. 1, 3–7 | Surprise documents; inadmissible material | A closed, exhibited documentary record |
| What is genuinely in dispute | XIV RR. 1, 3, 5 | Immaterial and admitted propositions | A finite list of issues with allocated burdens |
| What can be decided on law alone | XIV R. 2(2) | Suits barred by jurisdiction or statute | Disposal on a preliminary issue |
| What is left after all that | XV RR. 1–4 | Suits and defendants with nothing to try | Judgment at the first hearing |

::: tip The nine sentences worth memorising
1. Rule 6 of Order XII covers admissions made "in the pleading or otherwise, whether orally or in writing" — correspondence, affidavits and a party's own applications all qualify.
2. Rule 6 confers a discretion, not a right: the admission must be clear, unambiguous and unconditional, and a pleaded set-off or plea in avoidance usually defeats it.
3. A Rule 6 judgment produces a decree under sub-rule (2), and is therefore appealable under section 96 — but a **refusal** is not a decree and is challengeable only under Article 227.
4. Silence in the face of a notice to admit **documents** produces a deemed admission under Rule 2A; silence in the face of a notice to admit **facts** produces only a costs liability.
5. Originals must be produced on or before the settlement of issues under Order XIII Rule 1, except documents kept back for cross-examination or to refresh memory.
6. An objection to the **mode of proof** must be taken when the document is tendered or it is waived; an objection that the document is **inadmissible in law** survives to appeal.
7. An issue arises only where a **material** proposition is affirmed by one party and denied by the other, and each such proposition must form a **distinct** issue.
8. Since 1976 the court must pronounce judgment on **all** issues; a preliminary issue is confined to a question of **law only** relating to jurisdiction or a statutory bar, decidable on admitted facts.
9. A party who is present but fails to produce evidence suffers a decree **on the merits**; only a party who is **absent** has the Order IX remedies.
:::

::: warning Before you file
- **Serve the Order XII Rule 2 notice early**, and diarise the seven days. Under Rule 2A it may win you the documentary case without a witness.
- **Audit stamp duty and registration on every instrument at the drafting stage.** These are the only objections that cannot be waived, and Order XIII Rule 8 impounding plus penalty is cheaper than a lost suit.
- **Hand up draft issues at the first hearing**, worded so that every plea in confession and avoidance is the defendant's to prove.
- **Do not press limitation as a preliminary issue unless the bar appears on the plaint itself.** *Nusli Neville Wadia* denies the court jurisdiction to try a mixed question preliminarily.
- **Check State amendments to Order XV**, notably the Uttar Pradesh Rule 5 requiring a tenant-defendant to deposit admitted rent.
- **Re-read your issues when evidence closes.** Order XIV Rule 5(1) lets you fix a gap at any time before the decree; after the decree, you are asking an appellate court for a remand.
:::
