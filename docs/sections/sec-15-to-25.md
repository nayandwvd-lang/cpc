# Sections 15 to 25 — Place of Suing, Objections to Jurisdiction and Transfer of Suits

Sections 9 to 14 answered *whether a civil court may hear the dispute at all*. Sections 15 to 25 answer the next question: **which** civil court. This chapter is about pecuniary competence, territorial competence, the forum-selection war that precedes almost every commercial suit, and the machinery by which a suit is moved from one court to another.

The single most important idea in this chapter is a distinction the CPC draws and litigants constantly miss: **subject-matter incompetence is fatal and incurable; pecuniary and territorial incompetence are curable and, unless promptly objected to, forgiven.** Section 21 is the provision that forgives.

| Section | One-line function |
|---|---|
| **15** | Every suit shall be instituted in the court of the **lowest grade** competent to try it. |
| **16** | Suits concerning **immovable property** are to be instituted where the property is situate. |
| **17** | Immovable property within the jurisdiction of **different courts** — sue in any one of them. |
| **18** | Where it is **uncertain** in which court's local limits the property lies. |
| **19** | Suits for **compensation for wrongs** to person or movables — where the wrong was done, or where the defendant resides/carries on business. |
| **20** | The **residuary** rule — defendant's residence or place of business, or where the cause of action arose wholly or in part. |
| **21** | **Objections** to place of suing, pecuniary limits and competence of the executing court — when they may be taken, and the consequential-failure-of-justice requirement. |
| **21A** | **No substantive suit** to set aside a decree on the ground of an objection as to place of suing. |
| **22** | Power to **transfer** a suit which may be instituted in more than one court, on the defendant's application. |
| **23** | To **which court** the application under Section 22 lies. |
| **24** | **General power of transfer and withdrawal** vested in the High Court and the District Court. |
| **25** | Power of the **Supreme Court** to transfer suits, appeals and other proceedings between States. |

::: info The architecture in one breath
Sections 15 to 20 tell you where you **must** or **may** file. Section 21 tells you what happens when you file in the wrong place anyway — and the answer is usually "nothing, unless the defendant objected at the earliest possible opportunity **and** a failure of justice resulted". Sections 22 to 25 provide the corrective machinery when the forum, though lawful, is inconvenient or unfair.
:::

## Section 15 — Court in which suits to be instituted

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute The floor is made of concrete and the ceiling is made of paper.
Anita is owed ₹9,80,000 on a cheque that bounced. Her lawyer files the suit before the District Judge, because a senior court "takes these things more seriously."

She wins. Two years later, Bharat appeals — and his very first ground is that the suit had no business being in that court at all.

He is right about the rule. He loses anyway.

Section 15 is the rule Bharat was right about. Section 21 is the reason it did not save him. Understanding why is the whole of this section.
:::

::: story Why "file higher to be safe" is neither higher nor safe

There are two entirely separate questions hiding inside "which court do I go to", and confusing them is the most common jurisdictional error in practice.

The first is **vertical**: which rung of the ladder? Civil Judge (Junior Division), then Civil Judge (Senior Division), then District Judge. You climb this ladder by money — the value of what you are suing for, measured against a ceiling that each State fixes by notification. That is Section 15's question, and Section 15's only question.

The second is **horizontal**: which district? Where the property sits, where the defendant lives, where the wrong was done. That is Sections 16 to 20, and Section 15 has nothing to say about it.

Now the asymmetry that matters, because it is not symmetrical at all:

**File too low, and the decree is waste paper.** A Civil Judge (Junior Division) with a ceiling of ₹5,00,000 who tries a ₹9,80,000 suit is not being irregular. That court simply does not possess the power. The decree is a nullity — attackable in appeal, in execution, in a fresh proceeding, by anyone, at any time. No delay cures it, no consent creates the power, and no amount of acquiescence by Bharat will help Anita.

**File too high, and almost nothing happens.** This is Bharat's case above. It is an irregularity, not an absence of power. The District Judge always had the authority to try a ₹9,80,000 suit; the only complaint is that a cheaper court could have. Section 21(2) then imposes two conditions on anyone who wants to complain about it, and they are cumulative: the objection must have been taken **in the court of first instance, at the earliest possible opportunity**, and the objector must show a **consequent failure of justice**. Bharat took his point for the first time on appeal, and pleaded no prejudice at all. Both conditions failed.

So the rule reads in one direction only. The floor is made of concrete and the ceiling is made of paper.

This is also why "filing high to be safe" is bad advice rather than cautious advice. It buys nothing — there was never any risk in the direction you were guarding against — and it costs the client real money in court fees, a court that is further away and slower, and, most concretely, a first appeal that now runs to the High Court instead of to the District Judge. You have spent your client's appeal for nothing.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

One sentence, twenty words, and three separate ideas packed into it. Read the operative words in the order the court reads them: an obligation (**shall be instituted**), a direction on the vertical ladder (**lowest grade**), and the qualifier that does all the real work (**competent to try it**). A court that is low but not competent is not a candidate at all — which is why the "lowest grade" instruction can never push you below the pecuniary ceiling.

> **15. Court in which suits to be instituted.** — Every suit [[!shall be instituted]] in the Court of the [[=lowest grade]] [[=competent to try it]].

**Statutory mechanics.**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "Every suit **shall** be instituted" | **Mandatory in form** | The obligation is on the plaintiff, and it is unqualified in its language. |
| "Court of the **lowest grade**" | Hierarchical, not territorial | Refers to the vertical ladder — Civil Judge (Junior Division) → Civil Judge (Senior Division) → District Judge — not to geography. |
| "**competent** to try it" | Qualifying condition | Competence is measured by pecuniary value (read with the State's Civil Courts Act) and by any special jurisdiction conferred or withheld. |

**The critical structural point.** Despite the word "shall", Section 15 is consistently treated as a **rule of procedure and administrative convenience**, not a rule of jurisdiction. Its object is to prevent the higher courts from being clogged with petty litigation and to give the litigant the benefit of a cheaper, nearer forum. It follows that:

- Instituting a suit in a court of **higher** grade than necessary is an **irregularity**, curable, and cured by Section 21(2) unless objected to at the earliest opportunity with consequent failure of justice.
- Instituting a suit in a court of **lower** grade than competent — i.e. beyond that court's pecuniary ceiling — is a **nullity**. The lower court simply lacks the power. This is not a Section 15 problem at all; it is inherent want of pecuniary jurisdiction, and the decree is void.

**How "value" is fixed.** For the purpose of testing pecuniary competence, the court looks in the first instance to the **valuation stated in the plaint**, provided that valuation is not demonstrably arbitrary or made with the object of forum-shopping. The plaintiff is dominus litis on valuation, but not licensed to lie: an artificially deflated valuation invites a Section 21(2) objection, and an artificially inflated one invites an application to return the plaint.

::: proviso The unwritten proviso — "competent" outranks "lowest"

Section 15 contains no proviso in terms, but it carries one by necessary implication, and every practitioner must read it in.

The instruction is to go to the lowest grade **competent** to try the suit. Competence is the condition; lowest grade is only the tie-breaker among courts that satisfy it. Read in the other order — "go as low as you can" — the section becomes an instruction to file in a court that lacks the power to decide, which is precisely the one error Section 21 will not forgive.

So the sequence is fixed and cannot be reversed:

1. **First** eliminate every court whose notified pecuniary ceiling is below the value of your subject matter. They are not candidates. They never were.
2. **Then**, among the courts that survive, take the lowest.

A court excluded at step 1 does not become available because you would prefer it, because the defendant agrees to it, or because nobody notices for three years. Pecuniary competence is conferred by statute, and parties cannot confer it on each other by consent, waiver or silence.
:::

::: proviso Two valuations, one plaint — and they are frequently different numbers

Order VII Rule 1(i) requires the plaint to state the value of the subject matter **for the purpose of jurisdiction** and **for the purpose of court fees**. Practitioners routinely write one figure twice. In several classes of suit that is simply wrong.

| | Governed by | Fixed how |
|---|---|---|
| **Jurisdictional value** | Suits Valuation Act, 1887; State Civil Courts Act | Determines which rung of the ladder — the Section 15 question |
| **Court-fee value** | Court Fees Act, 1870, S. 7 (or the State equivalent) | Determines what you pay the State to file |

In a money suit the two coincide, which is why the error goes unnoticed for years. In a suit for declaration with consequential relief, in a suit for accounts under S. 7(iv), or in a partition suit where the plaintiff is in joint possession, they diverge — sometimes by an order of magnitude.

**Plead them separately, in separate sub-paragraphs, with the basis of each stated.** A plaint that states them as identical without explaining why invites an objection at the registry before a judge has read a word of it.
:::

### In plain English

**Sub-module A — Grade versus place.** Two independent axes govern where a plaint goes:

1. **Vertical (grade / pecuniary)** — governed by Section 15 read with the State Civil Courts Act. Which rung of the ladder?
2. **Horizontal (territorial / place of suing)** — governed by Sections 16 to 20. Which district or taluka?

A plaint must satisfy both. Section 15 speaks only to the first.

**Sub-module B — Why "lowest grade" is a benefit, not a burden.** The rule exists for the litigant and the system, not as a jurisdictional trap. Filing lower means lower cost, closer court, faster disposal, and — importantly — a preserved first appeal to the District Judge rather than straight to the High Court.

**Sub-module C — Valuation for jurisdiction versus valuation for court fee.** These are conceptually distinct and, in many classes of suit, numerically different. Court fee is governed by the Court Fees Act, 1870 (or the State equivalent); jurisdictional value by the Suits Valuation Act, 1887 and the State Civil Courts Act. In a suit for declaration with consequential relief, for instance, the court-fee valuation and the jurisdictional valuation may diverge sharply. **Always plead both, separately and expressly.**

**Sub-module D — What happens on a mid-suit change in value.** If the value of the subject matter appreciates during the suit, or an amendment enhances the claim beyond the trying court's ceiling, the court must return the plaint under Order VII Rule 10 for presentation to the proper court. Jurisdiction is tested continuously, not only at institution.

### Test your instinct

::::instinct The valuation that kept the suit downstairs

Anita sues Bharat for ₹9,80,000 due on a dishonoured cheque. In the plaint she values the suit at ₹4,00,000 "for the purposes of jurisdiction", which keeps it before the Civil Judge (Junior Division), whose notified ceiling is ₹5,00,000 and whose board is lighter. She pays court fee on the full ₹9,80,000.

Bharat files a written statement on the merits, contests the suit for four years, and loses. He does not object to jurisdiction at any stage. When Anita executes the decree, he applies to the executing court contending that the decree is void.

**Bharat never objected, and Section 21(2) forgives what is not promptly objected to. Is the decree safe?**

:::ruling

**Probably not — but the answer turns on how the defect is characterised, and this is the most misunderstood point in the chapter.**

There are two competing routes, and practitioners cite whichever suits them.

**Route one — this is a valuation error.** *Kiran Singh v. Chaman Paswan* holds that Section 11 of the Suits Valuation Act, 1887 makes a deliberate departure for over- and under-valuation: such a decree is **not** to be reversed unless the error prejudicially affected the disposal of the case on its merits. On this route Bharat must show prejudice, he has pleaded none, and the decree survives.

**Route two — this is want of pecuniary jurisdiction.** The court's power stopped at ₹5,00,000. It decreed ₹9,80,000. No valuation exercise was involved at all, because in a suit for a **liquidated sum** there is nothing to value — the jurisdictional value *is* the amount claimed, as a matter of law and not of the plaintiff's choice. What Anita did was not undervalue; she misdescribed. On this route the decree is a nullity and Bharat may say so in execution, which is exactly where he has said it.

**Route two is the better argument here, and the distinguishing feature is the objective yardstick.** *Kiran Singh* protects a valuation that was made and was wrong. It does not protect a figure that was never a valuation at all. *Commercial Aviation & Travel Co. v. Vimla Pannalal* supplies the dividing line: the plaintiff's discretion is real where the claim is genuinely unascertainable, and absent where an objective standard exists. A cheque for ₹9,80,000 is the clearest objective standard there is.

**The tell is the court fee.** Anita paid ad valorem on ₹9,80,000 and claimed jurisdiction on ₹4,00,000. Those two figures are irreconcilable in a money suit, and the inconsistency on the face of her own plaint is the best evidence that the lower figure was chosen for forum and not for value.

**What Bharat should have done anyway.** Taken the point at the first hearing under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a) and asked for return of the plaint under Order VII Rule 10. Winning in execution after four years of contest is an expensive way to be right, and it depends on persuading a court to adopt route two — which is an argument, not a certainty.
::::

::::instinct The safer court

Anita's claim is worth ₹6,00,000. The Civil Judge (Senior Division) is competent. Her counsel files before the District Judge instead, on the view that a senior court will treat the matter more seriously.

Bharat appears, files his written statement, and at the second hearing — before issues are settled — applies for return of the plaint, saying the suit belongs in the Senior Division. He says nothing about prejudice; his application asserts only that Section 15 uses the word "shall".

**He is on time. Does he win?**

:::ruling

**No. He has satisfied one condition out of two, and Section 15's "shall" does not supply the other.**

Section 21(2) is cumulative. The objection must be taken in the court of first instance at the earliest possible opportunity — Bharat has done that, and done it well — **and** there must be a **consequent failure of justice**. He has not pleaded one, so his application fails on the second limb alone.

**Why "shall" does not rescue him.** Section 15 is mandatory in form and procedural in substance. It regulates the distribution of business between courts that all possess the power; it does not withhold power from any of them. The District Judge was always competent to try a ₹6,00,000 suit. Nothing has been done that the court lacked authority to do, so there is nothing to declare void.

**What a pleaded failure of justice would have looked like.** The strongest available point was sitting in front of him: filing in the District Court converts a first appeal to the District Judge into a first appeal to the High Court. That is a concrete, articulable loss of a tier of appeal — more expensive, slower, and further away for his client. Pleaded in those terms, with the ceiling notification annexed, the application becomes properly arguable.

**The practical lesson runs against Anita's counsel, not Bharat.** Filing high gained her nothing — there was never a risk in that direction — and it handed the defence a preliminary objection, cost the client a cheaper forum, and spent a tier of appeal that belonged to the client and not to the advocate.
::::

::::instinct The amendment that outgrew the court

A specific-performance suit is valued at ₹4,50,000, the agreed consideration, and is properly before the Civil Judge (Junior Division) whose ceiling is ₹5,00,000. Two years in, Anita is permitted to amend to add claims for mesne profits and damages, taking the total to ₹12,00,000.

Her counsel takes the view that jurisdiction was correctly assumed at institution, that the court is seized of the matter, and that the trial should therefore continue to judgment.

**Is he right?**

:::ruling

**No. Jurisdiction is tested continuously, not once at the door.**

On allowing the amendment the court must return the plaint under Order VII Rule 10 for presentation to the Senior Division. The moment the claim as amended exceeds the ceiling, the court's power to decree it ends — and a court cannot acquire power by having once possessed it over a smaller claim. Being "seized of the matter" is not a source of jurisdiction.

**The endorsement matters.** Rule 10(2) requires the judge to endorse the date of presentation, the date of return, the name of the party presenting, and the reasons for return. Insist on a complete endorsement; it is the evidence of the period you will need to exclude.

**Now the trap that actually loses cases.** On re-presentation the suit is treated as instituted afresh **on the date of re-presentation**. Limitation does not travel with the plaint. If the original cause of action is by then time-barred, the suit dies on return unless Anita expressly pleads and proves **Section 14 of the Limitation Act, 1963** — prosecution in good faith, with due diligence, in a court unable to entertain it for defect of jurisdiction or other cause of a like nature.

**Plead Section 14 in the re-presented plaint itself.** Not in a reply, not when the point is taken in the written statement. Set out the dates of institution, of the amendment, of the return and of re-presentation, and annex the returned plaint bearing the Rule 10(2) endorsement. Section 14 is not applied by the court of its own motion — it is pleaded and it is proved.

**And ask for Rule 10A.** Where the return comes after the defendant has appeared, the court may fix a date for appearance before the transferee court, sparing a fresh round of summons and weeks of delay.
::::

::::instinct Two figures, one plaint

Anita sues for a declaration that a sale deed executed by Bharat is void, and for the consequential relief of possession of the house. Her counsel drafts the valuation paragraph as a single line: "The suit is valued at ₹5,00,000 for the purposes of court fee and jurisdiction, on which fee is paid."

**The registry accepts the plaint and it is numbered. Is there a problem?**

:::ruling

**Yes, and it is the single commonest drafting error in this area. Acceptance by the registry proves nothing.**

**Order VII Rule 1(i) requires two statements, not one.** The plaint shall state the value of the subject matter for the purpose of **jurisdiction** and for the purpose of **court fees**. Anita's counsel has written one figure and labelled it with both names, which is not the same thing as valuing the suit twice.

**In this suit the two are very unlikely to coincide.** This is a declaration **with consequential relief**, so court fee is not the fixed or notional fee payable on a bare declaration; it is attracted ad valorem under Section 7 of the Court Fees Act, 1870. Jurisdictional value, by contrast, is governed by the Suits Valuation Act, 1887 read with the State Civil Courts Act, and for a suit concerning immovable property is ordinarily referable to the value of that property. If the house is worth ₹40,00,000, a jurisdictional value of ₹5,00,000 is not a valuation — it is a placeholder.

**The two consequences.** Bharat gets a preliminary issue under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a) on pecuniary competence that Anita has no good answer to; and separately an objection that the plaint is **insufficiently stamped**, which engages Order VII Rule 11(c) — where the court gives time to make good the deficiency, and rejects only on failure to do so within the time fixed.

**Draft it as two sub-paragraphs, each with its basis stated.**

> **(a)** For the purposes of jurisdiction the suit is valued at ₹40,00,000, being the market value of the suit house as per the circle rate certificate at Annexure P-3.
> **(b)** For the purposes of court fee the suit is valued at ₹40,00,000 under Section 7(iv)(c) of the Court Fees Act, 1870, the declaration being sought with the consequential relief of possession, and ad valorem fee of ₹— is paid thereon.

Two figures may well be identical in a money suit. Say so deliberately, with the basis of each — never by writing one number and hoping it does duty for both.
::::

::::instinct The objection that waited for execution

Two decrees. In the first, Anita filed a ₹6,00,000 suit before the District Judge when the Senior Division was the lowest competent court; Bharat said nothing throughout and raises the point for the first time when the decree is put into execution. In the second, Anita filed a ₹9,80,000 suit before a Civil Judge (Junior Division) whose ceiling is ₹5,00,000; again Bharat said nothing until execution.

**Both objections arrive at the same moment, by the same silent defendant. Do they meet the same fate?**

:::ruling

**No. One is dead on arrival and the other is very much alive, and the difference has nothing to do with Bharat's conduct.**

**The first objection is barred, twice over.** Section 21(1) closes it because it was not taken in the court of first instance, and Section 21(2) closes it because no failure of justice has been pleaded. The District Judge had power to try that suit; filing there was an irregularity in the distribution of business, and irregularities are exactly what Section 21 was enacted to forgive. Bharat's silence has cost him the point permanently.

**The second is not affected by his silence at all.** The Junior Division had no power to decree ₹9,80,000. A decree passed without jurisdiction is a nullity, and *Kiran Singh* is explicit that a nullity may be set up whenever and wherever it is sought to be enforced — in appeal, in execution, or in collateral proceedings. Delay does not cure it because there is nothing to cure; consent does not save it because parties cannot confer jurisdiction the legislature has withheld.

**The organising principle, in one line: Section 21 forgives the irregular exercise of a power that existed. It cannot manufacture a power that never did.**

**Where the line genuinely blurs.** If the second suit had been one whose value was a matter of judgment rather than arithmetic — a suit for accounts, or for an unascertainable share — Anita's figure would have been a valuation, and *Kiran Singh* with Section 11 of the Suits Valuation Act would have pulled it back towards the first case, requiring Bharat to demonstrate prejudice on the merits. It is the presence of an **objective yardstick** that decides which side of the line a case falls, and that is the point to argue rather than assert.
::::

::: info Verify before citing
The characterisation running through these five problems — objective yardstick, valuation error, want of power — reflects the way these authorities are ordinarily applied, but the line has been drawn differently in different High Courts. Confirm *Kiran Singh* and *Commercial Aviation* in the official reports, and check for subsequent consideration, before putting this argument to a court.

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

**Commercial Aviation & Travel Co. v. Vimla Pannalal**, (1988) 3 SCC 423 — 2 Judges.

*Facts:* Valuation in a suit for accounts, where the plaintiff was unable to state a precise figure.

**Ratio decidendi:** In suits falling under Section 7(iv) of the Court Fees Act, the plaintiff has the right to place his own valuation and the court will not ordinarily interfere; **but where an objective standard of valuation is available, the plaintiff cannot arbitrarily choose a lower figure.**

**Why this is the ratio that wins Section 15 arguments.** Almost every pecuniary-competence fight reduces to a single question: was the figure in the plaint a *valuation* at all? If it was, *Kiran Singh* protects it and your opponent must prove prejudice on the merits — a burden very few discharge. If it was not — because the claim was a liquidated sum, an agreed consideration, or a figure fixed by an instrument — then there was nothing to value, the plaintiff has misdescribed rather than valued, and the protective rule never engages. *Commercial Aviation* is the authority that tells you which of those two worlds you are in.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The plaintiff's discretion as to valuation is real, but it operates only where the claim is genuinely unascertainable. Here an objective standard was available on the face of the instrument sued upon, and *Commercial Aviation & Travel Co. v. Vimla Pannalal* holds in terms that in such a case the plaintiff cannot arbitrarily choose a lower figure."
:::

#### Sword and shield

| | **As plaintiff (sword)** | **As defendant (shield)** |
|---|---|---|
| **At institution** | Fix the jurisdictional value on a stated, defensible basis and plead it separately from court fee. Where the claim is unascertainable, say so expressly and give your reasoning — that is what brings you within *Commercial Aviation*. | Read the valuation paragraph before the merits. A single figure doing duty for both valuations is the tell that no valuation exercise was performed. |
| **The objection** | Resist under Section 21(2): the objection is late, or was not taken in the court of first instance, or discloses no failure of justice. Put your opponent to proof of concrete prejudice. | Move at the **first hearing, before settlement of issues**, under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a), and ask for return under Order VII Rule 10. Plead the prejudice — do not merely assert it. |
| **The strongest prejudice** | Point out that no tier of appeal has been lost and no right affected; inconvenience is not failure of justice. | The loss of a first appeal to the District Judge where the suit was filed in the District Court. It is concrete, provable from the ceiling notification, and costs the client money. |
| **Court too LOW** | Nothing to argue. Return the plaint, re-present, and plead Section 14 of the Limitation Act at once. | Your best position in the chapter. The decree is a nullity; raise it in appeal, in execution, or collaterally, and delay does not hurt you. |
| **Court too HIGH** | Almost always survivable. Section 21(2) forgives an irregularity that harmed nobody. | Only worth taking if you can articulate the lost tier of appeal. Otherwise it consumes a hearing and signals that you have no defence on the merits. |

| Plaint returned under Order VII Rule 10 | Suit is deemed instituted afresh **on the date of re-presentation**. The original filing date does **not** travel with the plaint. | O. VII R. 10; the endorsement under R. 10(2) is your evidence |
| Time spent in the wrong court | **Excluded** — but only if pleaded and proved: prosecution in good faith, with due diligence, in a court unable to entertain the suit for defect of jurisdiction or other cause of a like nature. | **Limitation Act, 1963, S. 14** |
| Appearance after return | Court **may** fix a date for appearance before the transferee court, avoiding fresh summons. | O. VII R. 10A |
| Appeal against an order of return | The order is appealable as of right. | O. XLIII R. 1(a) |

::: warning The mistake that kills otherwise good suits
Section 14 of the Limitation Act is **not applied by the court of its own motion**. It must be pleaded, with dates, and proved. Plead it in the re-presented plaint itself — not in a reply after the point has been taken — and annex the returned plaint bearing the complete Rule 10(2) endorsement. A suit that was well within time when first filed can be dismissed as time-barred on re-presentation for no reason other than that nobody pleaded Section 14.
:::

#### Interlocking matrix

| Interlock | Provision | Practical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Return of plaint for want of pecuniary jurisdiction | **Order VII Rule 10** | Court endorses date of presentation, date of return, name of party presenting, and reasons. Plaint is re-presented, not re-filed. |
| Appeal against return | **Order XLIII Rule 1(a)** | An order under O.VII R.10 returning a plaint is appealable. |
| Fixing the day for appearance in the proper court | **Order VII Rule 10A** | Where the return is after the defendant has appeared, the court may fix a date for appearance in the transferee court, sparing fresh summons. |
| Statement of value in the plaint | **Order VII Rule 1(i)** | The plaint **shall** contain a statement of the value of the subject matter for jurisdiction *and* for court fees. |
| Valuation of the subject matter | **Suits Valuation Act, 1887**, Ss. 8, 9, 11 | Section 11 is the crucial one — an appellate court will not reverse for over- or under-valuation unless it prejudicially affected the disposal on merits. This is Section 21's twin. |
| Court fee | **Court Fees Act, 1870**, S. 7 | Ad valorem for money and possession suits; fixed or notional for pure declarations; consequential relief triggers ad valorem. |
| Limitation | **Limitation Act, 1963, S. 14** | Time spent bona fide prosecuting in a court **without jurisdiction** is excluded. This is the plaintiff's lifeline after a return of plaint. |
| Interlocutory application | Application under O.VII R.10 / R.11(d) with S.151 | The defendant's standard vehicle for a pecuniary-competence objection. |

::: warning The Section 14 Limitation Act trap
When a plaint is **returned** under Order VII Rule 10, the suit is deemed instituted afresh on the date of re-presentation — limitation is **not** automatically saved. You must expressly plead and prove Section 14 of the Limitation Act, 1963 (bona fides, due diligence, defect of jurisdiction or other cause of a like nature). Never assume the return carries your filing date with it.
:::

#### The authorities

**Kiran Singh v. Chaman Paswan**, AIR 1954 SC 340 — Constitution Bench (5 Judges).
*Facts:* A suit undervalued in the trial court, with the appeal consequently going to the District Court instead of the High Court; the objection was raised only at the second appellate stage.
**Ratio decidendi:** A defect of jurisdiction, *whether it is pecuniary or territorial, or in respect of the subject-matter of the action*, strikes at the very authority of the court and a decree passed without jurisdiction is a nullity. **But** Section 11 of the Suits Valuation Act (and, by parity, Section 21 CPC) enacts a deliberate departure for over- and under-valuation: such a decree is not to be reversed unless the error prejudicially affected the disposal of the case on its merits.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"An objection founded merely on over- or under-valuation cannot avail my learned friend at this stage: he must, under Kiran Singh, demonstrate that the error prejudicially affected the disposal of the suit on its merits, and he has pleaded no such prejudice."
:::

**Harshad Chiman Lal Modi v. DLF Universal Ltd.**, (2005) 5 SCC 791 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* A suit for specific performance of an agreement relating to immovable property situate outside the territorial jurisdiction of the court in which it was filed.
**Ratio decidendi:** Objections as to **territorial** and **pecuniary** jurisdiction are subject to Section 21 and can be waived; but an objection as to **subject-matter** jurisdiction goes to the root, cannot be waived, and consent cannot confer what the statute withholds. The court drew the line precisely where Section 21 draws it.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Harshad Chiman Lal Modi is authority for the proposition that competence over the subject matter can neither be conferred by consent nor cured by acquiescence — unlike place of suing, which Section 21 expressly forgives."
:::

Citations, bench strength and the precise scope of each holding should be confirmed against the official reports (SCC / AIR) and checked for subsequent consideration before being placed before a court. Bench strength for the two-Judge decisions above is stated from the reports and should be re-verified.
:::

#### Strategy and drafting

**Drafting traps that cause rejection or return.**

1. **Omitting the twin valuation statement.** Order VII Rule 1(i) requires a statement of value for jurisdiction *and* for court fees. A plaint carrying only one is defective and invites an objection at the registry stage itself.
2. **Copy-pasting the court-fee figure into the jurisdiction paragraph.** In declaratory and injunction suits these figures routinely differ. Stating them as identical without justification is the commonest drafting error in this area.
3. **Ignoring the State Civil Courts Act.** Pecuniary ceilings are State subjects and are periodically revised by notification. Check the *current* notified ceiling for the State and the district before filing.
4. **Filing high "to be safe".** It is not safe. It is a Section 15 irregularity, it costs the client a cheaper first appeal, and it hands the defence a preliminary objection.

**Strategic timing.**

- A pecuniary objection to a court of **lower** grade (inherent incompetence) may be taken at **any** time — including in execution, including for the first time in appeal — because the decree is a nullity.
- A Section 15 objection to a court of **higher** grade must be taken **at the earliest possible opportunity in the court of first instance and before settlement of issues** (Section 21(2)), and must be accompanied by a pleaded case of consequent failure of justice.
- Where you anticipate a valuation fight, raise it as a **preliminary issue under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a)** — jurisdiction of the court is one of the two categories on which a preliminary issue is permitted.

**Model prayer checklist — application for return of plaint (pecuniary).**

> It is therefore most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to:
> **(a)** frame a preliminary issue under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a) CPC on the pecuniary competence of this Hon'ble Court to try the suit;
> **(b)** hold that the suit as valued and as framed exceeds the pecuniary limits of this Hon'ble Court;
> **(c)** direct the return of the plaint under Order VII Rule 10 CPC with the endorsement required by Rule 10(2), for presentation to the proper court;
> **(d)** fix a date under Order VII Rule 10A CPC for the appearance of the parties before the proper court; and
> **(e)** pass such further or other order as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit in the circumstances of the case.

## Sections 16, 17 & 18 — Suits relating to immovable property

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute One sentence in the prayer clause decides which city you litigate in.
Anita lives in Mumbai. Bharat lives in Mumbai. They signed their agreement in Mumbai, negotiated it in Mumbai, and the advance moved between two Mumbai bank accounts.

The plot is in Nashik.

Whether Anita sues in Mumbai or in Nashik depends on none of that. It depends on a single question: can the decree she asks for be satisfied by Bharat signing a document — or will the court have to send its bailiff onto the land?

Add the words "and deliver vacant and peaceful possession" to her prayer clause, and the case moves 165 kilometres. Nothing else about the dispute changes at all.
:::

::: story The court cannot travel to the land, so the case must

Section 20 gives the plaintiff a menu — sue where the defendant lives, or where the cause of action arose, whichever suits. Section 16 gives no menu. It is a command, and the reason is entirely practical rather than doctrinal.

**A decree about land has to be executed on the land.** A court in Mumbai can order that a plot in Nashik be delivered up, partitioned by metes and bounds, or sold under a mortgage — but it cannot send its bailiff to Nashik to do any of those things. Only the court within whose local limits the property sits can. So the CPC sends the suit to the land rather than pretending the land can be brought to the suit. Lawyers call this *forum rei sitae*: the forum of the thing.

Section 16 then lists six categories of suit that must go to the situs — recovery, partition, mortgage remedies, determination of any other right or interest, compensation for wrong to the property, and recovery of movables under attachment. Clause **(d)** is the wide one: *any* suit whose object is to determine a right to or in land.

**And then the proviso reverses it, in one narrowly defined case.**

Some decrees about land need no bailiff at all. If all Anita wants is for Bharat to sign and register a conveyance, then Bharat's own hand does the whole job. There is nothing for a Nashik court to do that a Mumbai court cannot achieve by ordering Bharat — who is in Mumbai — to obey. In that situation the proviso lets her sue where Bharat resides or carries on business.

The condition is one word, and the entire commercial fight in this section is about it: the relief must be obtainable **entirely** through the defendant's personal obedience. Not mainly. Not substantially. Entirely.

So the test is a thought experiment you can run at the drafting stage, before you have chosen a court:

> *Assume the defendant does exactly what the decree tells him to do, willingly and immediately. Is my client now fully satisfied — without the court having to lay a finger on the land?*

If yes, the proviso is open and you may file where he lives. If any part of the relief requires the court to act **on the property** — deliver possession, demolish, partition, sell, restrain a stranger in occupation — the answer is no, "entirely" is defeated, and the suit belongs at the situs. This is why a prayer for possession, added by reflex at the end of a specific-performance plaint, is not a harmless extra. It is a forum-selection clause written by accident.

**Sections 17 and 18 are the two housekeeping rules that follow.**

Section 17 handles the property that will not sit inside one district — a holding straddling a boundary, or a mortgage over three flats in three districts. Sue in any court where *some portion* lies. Without it you would have to file three suits over one cause of action, which Order II Rule 2 forbids. But read its proviso: the court you choose must be pecuniarily competent for the **entire** claim, not merely the slice inside its limits. Section 17 relaxes territory and never touches grade.

Section 18 handles the boundary nobody can locate — shifting municipal limits, revenue maps that contradict each other, a building sitting astride a line. Sub-section (1) lets the court record, at the outset, that the uncertainty is genuine, and then proceed; that recorded statement makes the eventual decree unassailable on situs grounds. Sub-section (2) is the safety net for when nobody thought to record it, and it is drafted as a double negative that puts the burden squarely on the objector.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

Three sections, and they descend in force. Section 16 **commands** — a mandatory rule of place, qualified by an exception whose condition precedent is the word "entirely". Section 17 **permits** — an election given to the plaintiff, fenced by a proviso about value. Section 18 **forgives** — a prophylactic power in sub-section (1) and a curative one in sub-section (2), the latter drafted in almost exactly the language Section 21 uses.

Read the badged words below in that order. Note especially that Section 16 opens by subordinating itself — "**subject to** the pecuniary or other limitations prescribed by any law" — which is the section telling you, in its own first four words, that it fixes *place* and never *grade*. Section 15 continues to operate on top of everything here.

> **16. Suits to be instituted where subject-matter situate.** — Subject to the pecuniary or other limitations prescribed by any law, suits —
> **(a)** for the recovery of immovable property with or without rent or profits,
> **(b)** for the partition of immovable property,
> **(c)** for foreclosure, sale or redemption in the case of a mortgage of or charge upon immovable property,
> **(d)** for the determination of any other right to or interest in immovable property,
> **(e)** for compensation for wrong to immovable property,
> **(f)** for the recovery of movable property actually under distraint or attachment,
> [[!shall be instituted]] in the Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction the property is situate:
>
> **Provided that** a suit to obtain relief respecting, or compensation for wrong to, immovable property held by or on behalf of the defendant [[?may]], where the relief sought can be [[=entirely]] obtained through his [[=personal obedience]], be instituted either in the Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction the property is situate, or in the Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction the defendant actually and voluntarily resides, or carries on business, or personally works for gain.
>
> ***Explanation.*** — In this section "property" means property situate in India.

> **17. Suits for immovable property situate within jurisdiction of different Courts.** — Where a suit is to obtain relief respecting, or compensation for wrong to, immovable property situate within the jurisdiction of different Courts, the suit [[?may be instituted]] in any Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction any portion of the property is situate: Provided that, in respect of the value of the subject matter of the suit, the [[=entire claim]] is cognizable by such Court.

> **18. Place of institution of suit where local limits of jurisdiction of Courts are uncertain.** — **(1)** Where it is alleged to be uncertain within the local limits of the jurisdiction of which of two or more Courts any immovable property is situate, any one of those Courts [[?may]], if satisfied that there is ground for the alleged uncertainty, [[=record a statement]] to that effect and thereupon proceed to entertain and dispose of any suit relating to that property, and its decree in the suit shall have the same effect as if the property were situate within the local limits of its jurisdiction: Provided that the suit is one with respect to which the Court is competent as regards the nature and value of the suit to exercise jurisdiction.
> **(2)** Where a statement has not been recorded under sub-section (1), and an objection is taken before an Appellate or Revisional Court that a decree or order in a suit relating to such property was made by a Court not having jurisdiction where the property is situate, the Appellate or Revisional Court [[!shall not allow the objection]] unless in its opinion there was, at the time of the institution of the suit, no reasonable ground for uncertainty as to the Court having jurisdiction with respect thereto and there has been a [[=consequent failure of justice]].

**Statutory mechanics of Section 16.**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "**Subject to** the pecuniary or other limitations" | Subordinating clause | Section 16 fixes *place*, never *grade*. Section 15 still applies on top of it. |
| "**shall** be instituted in the Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction the property is situate" | **Mandatory** | The *forum rei sitae* rule. Unlike Section 20, this is not a menu — it is a command. |
| The **proviso** | **Enabling exception** | Converts the mandatory rule into an option, but only when the condition precedent is satisfied. |
| "where the relief sought can be **entirely** obtained through his **personal obedience**" | **Condition precedent** | The word "entirely" is the whole battleground. If any part of the decree requires the court to operate directly on the land, the proviso is unavailable. |
| **Explanation** | Territorial limiter | "Property" means property situate **in India**. Section 16 therefore has nothing to say about foreign immovable property; that is governed by the common-law *Moçambique* principle as adapted, and by Section 20. |

**Statutory mechanics of Section 17.** The operative word is "**may**" — Section 17 confers an *election* on the plaintiff where a single property, or a single relief, straddles two or more jurisdictions. But the proviso imposes a hard **condition precedent**: the court chosen must be competent, in terms of value, for the **entire** claim, not merely the portion within its limits. Section 17 relaxes territory; it does not relax pecuniary competence.

**Statutory mechanics of Section 18.** Two distinct limbs:

- **Sub-section (1)** — a **prophylactic** power. The court, before proceeding, *records a statement* that there is ground for uncertainty. Having done so, its decree is deemed as valid as if the property lay squarely within its limits. Recording the statement is the condition precedent to the deeming fiction.
- **Sub-section (2)** — a **curative** provision, and a near-twin of Section 21. Where no statement was recorded, the appellate or revisional court **shall not** allow the objection unless *both* (i) there was no reasonable ground for uncertainty at institution, **and** (ii) there has been a consequent failure of justice. Both conditions are cumulative; the double negative makes the burden squarely the objector's.

::: proviso The proviso to Section 16 — "entirely", and why one word carries the whole section

The proviso converts a command into an option, but only on a condition precedent that is drafted to be hard to satisfy: the relief sought must be capable of being obtained **entirely** through the defendant's personal obedience.

**Two things must both be true**, and practitioners routinely check only the first:

1. **The relief must be fully achievable by the defendant's own act.** Signing a conveyance, executing a document, rendering accounts, paying money. If the court must operate on the land — deliver possession, partition by metes and bounds, order a sale, demolish, restrain an occupant who is not before it — the condition fails.
2. **The property must be "held by or on behalf of the defendant".** This limb is in the proviso's own text and is regularly overlooked. Where the suit property is in the *plaintiff's* possession and the defendant is a stranger to its title — the classic injunction-against-interference suit — the proviso does not fit by its own language, whatever the answer to limb 1.

**The decisive line in practice** is between specific performance *simpliciter* and specific performance *with possession*:

| Relief claimed | Personal obedience enough? | Forum |
|---|---|---|
| Execute and register a sale deed | **Yes** — the defendant's own hand completes it | Proviso available; sue where he resides or carries on business |
| Execute a sale deed **and** deliver possession | **No** — possession needs the court's bailiff on the land | Situs court only |
| Permanent injunction restraining interference with the plaintiff's possession | **No** — and the property is not held by the defendant either | Situs court |
| Partition, foreclosure, sale, redemption | **No** — each requires the court to act on the property | Situs court |

**"Entirely" admits of no substantial compliance.** A prayer that is ninety per cent personal obedience and ten per cent bailiff is not within the proviso. This is why the possession prayer added by reflex at the foot of a plaint is not a harmless surplus — it is a choice of city.
:::

::: proviso The provisos to Sections 17 and 18 — what each one refuses to relax

The two later sections each carry a limiting clause, and each limits a **different** thing. Confusing them is common.

**Section 17's proviso is about value.** You may sue in any court where a portion of the property lies — but only if "in respect of the value of the subject matter of the suit, the **entire claim** is cognizable by such Court". Not the portion within its limits. The whole claim. So if an estate is worth ₹90,00,000 and the court you have chosen has a ceiling of ₹50,00,000, Section 17 gives you nothing; you must find a court competent for the entirety. **Section 17 relaxes territory and never touches grade** — which is Section 15 reasserting itself, exactly as Section 16's opening words ("subject to the pecuniary or other limitations") warned it would.

**Section 18's proviso is about competence too, but its real machinery is the recorded statement.** Sub-section (1) permits a court to proceed despite genuine doubt about where the property lies — but only if it *records a statement* that there is ground for the uncertainty. That recorded statement is the condition precedent to the deeming fiction, and it is armour: the decree afterwards has the same effect as if the property were squarely within the court's limits.

**Sub-section (2) is the safety net, and it is Section 21 in different clothing.** Where no statement was recorded, an appellate or revisional court **shall not** allow a situs objection *unless*, cumulatively:

- there was, at the time of institution, **no reasonable ground** for uncertainty; **and**
- there has been a **consequent failure of justice**.

The double negative places the burden squarely on the objector, and the first limb is usually where he dies: two conflicting official maps are, on their face, a reasonable ground for uncertainty.
:::

### In plain English

**Sub-module A — Why immovable property is treated differently.** Land cannot be moved to the court; the court must go to the land. Only the court of the *situs* can effectively execute a decree for possession, effect a partition by metes and bounds, or order a sale of mortgaged property. The rule is therefore one of practical enforceability, not of convenience.

**Sub-module B — The six categories in Section 16(a) to (f).**

| Clause | Covers | Typical suit |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | Recovery of immovable property, with or without rent/profits | Ejectment; suit for possession with mesne profits |
| (b) | Partition | Partition of coparcenary or co-owned property |
| (c) | Mortgage remedies | Foreclosure, sale, redemption |
| (d) | Determination of any other right to or interest in immovable property | Declaration of title; easement; permanent injunction restraining interference with possession |
| (e) | Compensation for wrong to immovable property | Damages for trespass, for nuisance, for waste |
| (f) | Recovery of movable property under distraint or attachment | Claim to goods seized under a distress warrant |

Clause **(d)** is the residuary net and it is wide: any suit whose object is to determine a right *to or in* land falls here.

**Sub-module C — The proviso: the "personal obedience" test.** This is the most commercially significant idea in the chapter. Ask a single question: *if the defendant simply obeys the decree, is the plaintiff fully satisfied — without the court having to act upon the land itself?*

- **Yes → the proviso applies.** The plaintiff may sue where the defendant resides or carries on business. Classic case: a suit for **specific performance** of an agreement to sell land, where the only decree needed is one directing the defendant to execute a sale deed. The defendant's own act completes the relief.
- **No → the proviso does not apply.** Classic case: specific performance **plus** a decree for delivery of possession. Possession requires the court to act on the land through its bailiff. The word "entirely" is defeated, and the suit must go to the situs court.

**Sub-module D — Section 17 in practice: the straddling property.** A single agricultural holding lies partly in District A and partly in District B; or a mortgage secures three flats in three districts. The plaintiff may institute in any one court where *some portion* lies. The purpose is to prevent the absurdity of splitting one cause of action into three suits — which would itself run headlong into Order II Rule 2. Note the proviso's discipline: if the entire claim is worth ₹90,00,000 and the chosen court's ceiling is ₹50,00,000, Section 17 does not help; you must go to a court competent for the whole.

**Sub-module E — Section 18 and the genuinely uncertain boundary.** Municipal boundaries shift; village revenue records conflict; a building sits astride a line. Section 18(1) allows a court to say, in terms, "there is real doubt where this property lies; I record that doubt and proceed." That recorded statement is armour: the decree cannot afterwards be attacked on situs grounds. If the court neglects to record it, sub-section (2) still protects the decree — but only through the harder, cumulative test.

### Test your instinct

::::instinct The prayer clause that moved the case 165 kilometres

Anita agreed to buy Bharat's plot at Nashik for ₹85,00,000. Both live and work in Mumbai; the agreement was signed there and the advance of ₹20,00,000 was paid from a Mumbai account. Anita has been let into possession under a licence deed and has been on the plot for two years. Bharat now refuses to convey.

Her counsel files in Mumbai, relying on the proviso to Section 16, and drafts the prayer clause as follows:

> **(a)** decree the specific performance of the agreement dated 4 March 2024 and direct the defendant to execute and register a sale deed in favour of the plaintiff;
> **(b)** direct the defendant to deliver vacant and peaceful possession of the suit property to the plaintiff;
> **(c)** costs, and such further orders as the Court deems fit.

**Bharat applies under Order VII Rule 10 for return of the plaint to Nashik. Who wins?**

:::ruling

**Bharat wins, and prayer (b) is the only reason.**

**Prayer (a) alone was safely within the proviso.** *Adcon Electronics Pvt. Ltd. v. Daulat* holds that a suit for specific performance **simpliciter** is not, in the strict sense, a suit for land: the relief rests on the defendant's personal obligation to execute the conveyance. If Bharat signs and registers, Anita has everything she asked for and no Nashik court has been troubled. That is the proviso operating exactly as designed.

**Prayer (b) destroys it.** Delivery of possession cannot be achieved by Bharat's signature. If he does not comply, the decree is executed by a bailiff walking onto the plot — and only the Nashik court can send one. The moment any part of the relief requires the court to act upon the land, the word **"entirely"** in the proviso is defeated. *Harshad Chiman Lal Modi v. DLF Universal Ltd.* is direct authority: where possession is sought, the proviso is inapplicable and the situs court alone has jurisdiction.

**The sharpest point is that prayer (b) was pointless.** Anita has been in possession for two years. She did not need a possession decree; she needed a conveyance. Counsel added the clause by reflex, as counsel almost always do, and in doing so chose Nashik over Mumbai without noticing that a choice was being made.

**How the plaint should have read.** Plead the possession fact in the body — "the plaintiff was put into possession on 12 May 2024 under the licence deed at Annexure P-2 and continues in possession to date" — then confine the prayer to execution and registration, and plead the proviso expressly:

> The plaintiff being already in possession, the entirety of the relief claimed is capable of being obtained through the personal obedience of the defendant, who resides and carries on business within the local limits of this Hon'ble Court, and the suit is therefore maintainable here under the proviso to Section 16 of the Code.

**A caution about *Babu Lal v. Hazari Lal Kishori Lal*.** It holds that possession ancillary to specific performance may be granted in execution without a separate suit. Do not read that as permission to omit the prayer and pick up possession later from a Mumbai court. The ancillary character of possession at the execution stage does not retrospectively cure a want of territorial competence at institution, and it cannot give a Mumbai court a bailiff in Nashik.
::::

::::instinct The injunction that looked personal

Anita owns and occupies a plot in Gurugram. Bharat, who lives in Delhi, has begun dumping construction debris across her access road and has twice threatened to wall it off. Anita sues in Delhi for a permanent injunction restraining Bharat from interfering with her possession.

Her counsel argues the proviso applies: the only relief sought is that Bharat should *stop doing something*, which is pure forbearance and the most personal form of obedience imaginable. No bailiff need ever enter Gurugram.

**The reasoning about forbearance is sound. Does the suit stay in Delhi?**

:::ruling

**No — and the argument fails on the limb of the proviso that counsel did not read.**

**First, the suit is within Section 16(d).** It is a suit "for the determination of any other right to or interest in immovable property". Its real subject is Anita's possessory title to the Gurugram plot, and the injunction is the machinery for protecting it. Clause (d) is the wide residuary net, and possessory injunction suits sit squarely inside it.

**Second — and decisively — the proviso by its own terms is confined to immovable property "held by or on behalf of the defendant".** Bharat does not hold this plot. He is a stranger to its title; Anita holds it. However personal his obedience would be, the proviso simply does not describe this situation. Counsel tested only whether the relief was obtainable by personal obedience and never checked whether the property answered the proviso's description.

**This is the most frequently missed limb in the section**, because the forbearance point is genuinely attractive and it is easy to stop once you have found a good argument. Run *both* limbs every time: (i) is the relief entirely obtainable through personal obedience, and (ii) is the property held by or on behalf of the defendant. Both must be yes.

**The practical answer.** File in Gurugram. There is a further reason beyond doctrine: an injunction is worth what its enforcement is worth, and contempt proceedings for breach of an injunction over Gurugram land are immeasurably more effective before the court that has the land within its limits.
::::

::::instinct The estate that would not fit in one district

A joint family estate consists of a house in District A worth ₹35,00,000 and agricultural land in District B worth ₹55,00,000. Anita, a coparcener entitled to a one-fourth share, sues for partition of the whole estate. She files in District A, where the Civil Judge (Senior Division) has a pecuniary ceiling of ₹50,00,000.

She pleads that a portion of the property lies within that court's limits, and values the suit at ₹22,50,000 — the value of her own one-fourth share.

**Bharat objects. Is Anita's Section 17 election good?**

:::ruling

**Her choice of district is right; her valuation is wrong, and the proviso to Section 17 defeats her.**

**Section 17 is correctly invoked as to territory.** The estate straddles two districts, and the section exists precisely so that one cause of action need not be split into two suits. Filing the whole partition in District A is not merely permitted — Order II Rule 2 makes it close to compulsory, because a partition of joint family property is a single cause of action and omitting the District B land would bar her from claiming it later.

**But the proviso is about value, and it is exacting.** The election is available only if "in respect of the value of the subject matter of the suit, the **entire claim** is cognizable by such Court". The subject matter of a partition suit is the whole estate sought to be partitioned — ₹90,00,000 — not the plaintiff's arithmetical share of it. The Senior Division's ceiling is ₹50,00,000. The court is therefore not competent for the entire claim, and Section 17 gives Anita nothing.

**Section 17 relaxes territory and never touches grade.** This is Section 15 reasserting itself, exactly as Section 16 promised in its opening words — "subject to the pecuniary or other limitations prescribed by any law". None of Sections 16 to 20 ever lifts the pecuniary ceiling; they answer a different question.

**What she must do.** File in the District Judge's court in District A — competent for the whole ₹90,00,000, and still a court within whose limits a portion of the property lies. Both conditions are then satisfied together.

**And expect the valuation to be fought.** Bharat's best move is to put the aggregate valuation in issue and seek a Commissioner under Order XXVI, because every rupee of movement changes which court may hear the case. Anita should annex circle-rate certificates or a valuer's report for **both** properties to the plaint, and plead the aggregate expressly — not merely her share.

::::instinct The boundary nobody could find

A strip of land lies on the line between two tahsils. The revenue map places it in Tahsil A; the municipal map, revised in 2019, places it in Tahsil B. Anita sues Bharat in the Tahsil A court for a declaration of title to the strip and wins after a four-year trial.

Bharat, who never raised any objection to jurisdiction at any stage, appeals. His first ground is that the strip lies in Tahsil B and the Tahsil A court therefore had no jurisdiction over the situs.

**No statement of uncertainty was ever recorded. Does Bharat's ground succeed?**

:::ruling

**Almost certainly not — and the two conflicting maps that make the case difficult are precisely what defeat him.**

**Section 18(2) governs, because no statement was recorded under sub-section (1).** It provides that the appellate court **shall not** allow the objection *unless*, cumulatively:

1. there was, at the time of institution, **no reasonable ground** for uncertainty as to which court had jurisdiction; **and**
2. there has been a **consequent failure of justice**.

**Limb 1 kills the appeal on its own.** Two official records — a revenue map and a revised municipal map — placing the strip in different tahsils are, on their face, a reasonable ground for uncertainty. Bharat must prove there was *no* reasonable ground, and he is asking the appellate court to make that finding while pointing at the very documents that create the doubt.

**Limb 2 he has not even pleaded.** He identifies no way in which trial in Tahsil A rather than Tahsil B affected the outcome — the same law, the same evidence, the same witnesses.

**Note how the drafting allocates the burden.** The double negative — "shall not allow the objection unless" — puts everything on the objector. This is Section 21 in different clothing, and the family resemblance is not accidental: both provisions exist to stop a party who fought and lost on the merits from re-opening the case on a forum technicality.

**What Anita's counsel should nevertheless have done at the threshold.** Applied under Section 18(1), annexed both maps, and asked the court to record a statement that there was ground for the alleged uncertainty. That recorded statement is the condition precedent to the deeming fiction in sub-section (1), and it makes the decree unassailable on situs grounds — armour, rather than the argument Anita is now obliged to run in appeal. It costs one paragraph and one hearing.

::::instinct The flat in Dubai

Anita and Bharat are Indian citizens, both resident in Bengaluru. Bharat holds a flat in Dubai which Anita says was bought with joint funds and is held by him in trust for them both. She sues in Bengaluru for a declaration that she is entitled to a half share, and for a direction that Bharat execute a deed transferring that half share to her.

Bharat says the property is immovable property, that Section 16 sends such suits to the situs, and that no Indian court can entertain the claim at all.

**Is he right?**

:::ruling

**No. He has cited a section that does not reach the property — but Anita's suit survives only because of how she has framed it.**

**The Explanation to Section 16 is the answer to Bharat.** It provides that in this section "property" means property situate **in India**. Section 16 therefore says nothing whatever about the Dubai flat: it neither sends the suit to Dubai nor bars it here. Bharat's objection is founded on a provision that by its own terms does not apply.

**So which section governs? Section 20.** With Section 16 out of the picture, the residuary rule applies, and Bharat resides and carries on business in Bengaluru. The Bengaluru court has territorial jurisdiction over him.

**But the framing of the relief is doing critical work.** Courts will not adjudicate upon title to foreign land — the principle inherited from *British South Africa Co. v. Companhia de Moçambique* — because no Indian decree can operate on land in Dubai. What an Indian court *can* do is act **in personam** against a defendant properly before it: order Bharat, who is in Bengaluru and subject to its authority, to execute a transfer. The obligation enforced is personal, arising from the trust and from his conduct; the court reaches the man, not the land.

**Which is the same idea as the proviso to Section 16**, arrived at from the other direction. Both turn on whether the relief can be delivered by compelling a person rather than by operating on the soil.

**The drafting consequence is severe and precise.** Anita must confine herself to relief *in personam* — a declaration of Bharat's obligation and a direction that he execute the transfer, enforceable against him personally by the ordinary means of Order XXI Rule 32. If she adds a prayer for possession of the Dubai flat, or asks for a declaration that operates directly on title as against the world, she invites a dismissal on *Moçambique* grounds. And she should plead expressly that Bharat is within the jurisdiction and that the relief sought is in personam only.
::::

::: info Verify before citing
The interaction between the Explanation to Section 16, the residuary rule in Section 20 and the *Moçambique* principle as received in India is a genuinely contested area, and the last problem states the mainstream position rather than a settled one. Confirm *Adcon Electronics*, *Harshad Chiman Lal Modi* and *Babu Lal* in the official reports and check for subsequent consideration before relying on any of this in court.

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

**Adcon Electronics Pvt. Ltd. v. Daulat**, (2001) 7 SCC 698 — 2 Judges.

*Facts:* A suit for specific performance of an agreement to sell immovable property, instituted otherwise than at the situs of the property.

**Ratio decidendi:** A suit for specific performance **simpliciter** is not a suit "for land" or for a right to or interest in immovable property in the strict sense. The relief is founded on the **personal obligation** of the defendant to execute the conveyance, and such a suit falls within the proviso to Section 16. **The distinction between specific performance simpliciter and specific performance with possession is decisive.**

**Why this is the ratio that wins Section 16 arguments.** Every contested forum question under this section reduces to one issue — whether the relief is obtainable *entirely* through the defendant's personal obedience — and the overwhelming majority of those disputes arise in specific-performance suits. *Adcon* states the dividing line in a form you can apply to a draft plaint before a word of it is filed: look at the prayer clause, and ask whether possession is in it. Read with *Harshad Chiman Lal Modi* — which supplies the converse, that where possession is sought the proviso is inapplicable and the defect is not curable by consent — the pair decides the question in both directions.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The suit is one for specific performance simpliciter. The plaintiff is already in possession and seeks no decree for delivery of possession; the entirety of the relief is obtainable through the personal obedience of the defendant, who resides within the local limits of this Hon'ble Court. On *Adcon Electronics Pvt. Ltd. v. Daulat* the proviso to Section 16 is squarely attracted, and this Court has jurisdiction."
:::

#### Sword and shield

| | **As plaintiff (sword)** | **As defendant (shield)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Drafting the prayer** | Ask for the least the client actually needs. If he is in possession, plead that fact in the body and omit the possession prayer — that single omission preserves your forum. | Read the prayer clause before the pleadings. Any relief requiring the court to act on the land — possession, demolition, partition, sale, injunction against occupants — defeats "entirely". |
| **The proviso, limb 1** | Plead expressly *why* the whole relief is obtainable through personal obedience. Do not leave the court to infer it. | Attack the omnibus prayer as well as the specific ones. A general prayer wide enough to cover possession is worth an argument. |
| **The proviso, limb 2** | Confirm the property is held by or on behalf of the defendant before relying on the proviso at all. | The most-missed point in the section: where the property is in the **plaintiff's** possession and your client is a stranger to its title, the proviso does not fit by its own language. |
| **Section 17 straddle** | Value and plead the **entire** estate, not your client's share. Annex circle-rate certificates or a valuer's report for every parcel. | Put the aggregate valuation in issue and seek a Commissioner under Order XXVI. Every rupee of movement can change which court may hear the case. |

::: warning The forum error that becomes a limitation error
A Section 16 objection is usually taken late — often after the written statement — and the consequence of losing it is a return of the plaint, not a dismissal. By the time the plaint is re-presented at the situs, an Article 54 period of three years has frequently run out. The return is therefore only survivable if Section 14 of the Limitation Act is pleaded, with dates, in the re-presented plaint itself, annexing the returned plaint bearing the complete Rule 10(2) endorsement. Choosing the wrong court under Section 16 does not merely cost time — it can cost the suit.

#### The authorities

**Harshad Chiman Lal Modi v. DLF Universal Ltd.**, (2005) 5 SCC 791 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* Suit for specific performance of an agreement for a plot at Gurgaon, instituted at Delhi where the defendant carried on business; the decree also involved delivery of possession.
**Ratio decidendi:** Section 16 mandates institution at the situs. The proviso is a narrow exception operating only where the relief sought can be obtained **entirely** through the defendant's personal obedience. Where the plaintiff seeks possession of the immovable property, the relief cannot be obtained by personal obedience alone, the proviso is inapplicable, and the situs court alone has jurisdiction. Territorial jurisdiction over the subject matter in such a case is not curable by consent.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"On Harshad Chiman Lal Modi, the moment a prayer for delivery of possession is added, the proviso to Section 16 is displaced and only the court within whose local limits the property is situate can try the suit."
:::

**Babu Lal v. Hazari Lal Kishori Lal**, (1982) 1 SCC 525 — 3 Judges.
*Facts:* Whether a decree for specific performance carries with it the right to possession without a separate prayer.
**Ratio decidendi:** A decree for specific performance does not automatically entitle the decree-holder to possession; but where the relief of possession is ancillary and incidental to the relief of specific performance, it may be granted in execution without a separate suit. (The interaction of this proposition with the forum question under Section 16 must be handled with care — the ancillary nature of possession in execution does not retrospectively cure a forum defect at institution.)
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The availability of possession in execution as an ancillary relief does not convert a situs-bound suit into a personal one at the stage of institution."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Bench strength and the precise ratio of Babu Lal, and the subsequent treatment of the Adcon line of authority, should be verified in the official reports before reliance. This area has attracted considerable later High Court elaboration.
:::

#### Strategy and drafting

**Drafting traps.**

1. **The reflex possession prayer.** Discussed above — the single most expensive drafting habit in this chapter.
2. **Pleading the proviso in the body but not in the cause-of-action paragraph.** Order VII Rule 1(e) requires the facts constituting the cause of action and *when it arose*; Rule 1(f) requires the facts showing that the court has jurisdiction. Your jurisdiction paragraph must state, in terms: the defendant resides / carries on business within these limits, and the entire relief claimed is obtainable through his personal obedience.
3. **Using Order VII Rule 11 for a territorial objection.** Rule 11 rejects; Rule 10 returns. Rejection is far more drastic and is not the remedy for want of territorial jurisdiction. Ask for return, in the alternative to rejection, and never the other way round.
4. **Forgetting the Section 17 proviso.** Choosing the convenient court where a portion of the property lies, without checking that the chosen court is competent for the *whole* claim's value.
5. **Failing to move under Section 18(1) at the threshold** where the boundary is genuinely disputed. It costs one paragraph at institution and saves the decree at appeal.

**Strategic timing.**

- Territorial objections are **Section 21 objections** — they must be taken in the court of first instance, at the earliest possible opportunity, and before settlement of issues. Raise them in the written statement itself as preliminary objection No. 1, and simultaneously move under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a).
- Do not wait for evidence. The right to object is spent once issues are settled, whatever the merits.
- Conversely, as plaintiff, **push for early settlement of issues**. Every day the defendant delays his territorial objection strengthens your Section 21(1) answer.

**Model jurisdiction paragraph — proviso to Section 16.**

> That the Defendant actually and voluntarily resides at, and carries on business from, [address], which falls within the local limits of the ordinary original civil jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court. The relief claimed in this suit is the specific performance of the Agreement to Sell dated [date] by the execution and registration of a conveyance in favour of the Plaintiff. The Plaintiff is, and has since [date] been, in lawful possession of the suit property, and no relief by way of delivery of possession is claimed or required. The entire relief sought in this suit is therefore capable of being obtained through the personal obedience of the Defendant, and this Hon'ble Court has jurisdiction to entertain, try and dispose of this suit by virtue of the proviso to Section 16 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908.

**Model prayer checklist — application under Section 18(1).**

> **(a)** be pleased to record a statement under Section 18(1) CPC that there is ground for the alleged uncertainty as to the local limits within which the suit property is situate;
> **(b)** be pleased to proceed to entertain and dispose of the present suit notwithstanding such uncertainty;
> **(c)** in the alternative, be pleased to issue a commission under Order XXVI Rule 9 CPC for local investigation and demarcation of the suit property.

## Section 19 — Suits for compensation for wrongs to person or movables

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute The victim of a tort picks the ring; the tortfeasor only picks the gloves.

Bharat, sitting in Delhi, does something to Anita in Chennai — he punches her, or he prints something about her, or he seizes her machinery. Anita now has a genuine choice of two courts, and it is **hers**, not his: she may sue **where the wrong happened** (Chennai) or **where Bharat lives, trades or works** (Delhi).

That is the whole of Section 19. It applies to **torts only** — wrongs to a **person** or to **movable property**. It does not apply to a breach of contract, no matter how badly the contract was broken. And it gives only **two** doors, not an open field.

Bharat cannot take the choice away from her. His remedy against an inconvenient-but-lawful choice is a **transfer** application under Sections 22 to 25 — not a jurisdiction objection.
:::

::: story Why the law lets the person who was hurt choose

Think about what the alternative would look like.

If the only forum were **where the defendant lives**, a man could beat someone in Chennai, take the flight home to Delhi, and force his victim to litigate two thousand kilometres from the hospital that treated her, the police station that recorded her complaint and every witness who saw it happen. Distance would become a defence. The further you travel to commit a wrong, the safer you are.

If the only forum were **where the wrong happened**, the mirror problem appears. A newspaper circulates in twenty States; a fraudster tours the country converting goods. The defendant could be dragged anywhere at all, on a bare assertion, with no anchor to any place he has ever been.

Section 19 refuses both extremes. It fixes exactly two anchors — the *situs* of the wrong and the *situs* of the defendant — and hands the key to the plaintiff. The reasoning is old and simple: as between the person who did the wrong and the person it was done to, the inconvenience of an extra journey should fall on the wrongdoer.

Two consequences follow, and both are constantly misunderstood.

**First, the election is real.** Neither forum outranks the other. A defendant who says "the Chennai court is more appropriate" has said nothing about jurisdiction; he has made a convenience argument, and convenience is dealt with by Sections 22 to 25, on an application, before the right judge — not by an objection to the plaint.

**Second, the election is fragile at its base.** Section 19 only bites if the claim is genuinely a **tort**. The single most effective answer to a Section 19 election is not to fight the election but to attack the label: to show that what is pleaded as a wrong is really a broken bargain. Recharacterise the claim as contractual and the section falls away, taking the plaintiff's chosen forum with it.

So the fight under Section 19 is almost never about *which* of the two courts. It is about whether there was a tort at all, and about whether the wrong really happened where the plaintiff says it did.

### The Act, decompiled

One sentence, two Illustrations. The sentence sets a **condition** (the two places must be different), names the **two candidate courts**, and then confers a **permission** on one party only. Read the Illustrations carefully — Illustration (b) is doing far more work than it looks, because it tells you that a defamation is "done" where it is *published*, not where it is written.

> **19. Suits for compensation for wrongs to person or movables.** — Where a suit is for compensation for [[=wrong done]] to the [[=person]] or to [[=movable property]], if the wrong was done within the local limits of the jurisdiction of one Court and the defendant [[=resides, or carries on business, or personally works for gain]], within the local limits of the jurisdiction of another Court, the suit [[?may be instituted]] [[=at the option of the plaintiff]] in [[=either]] of the said Courts.
>
> ***Illustrations***
> **(a)** A, residing in Delhi, beats B in Calcutta. B may sue A either in Calcutta or in Delhi.
> **(b)** A, residing in Delhi, publishes in Calcutta statements defamatory of B. B may sue A either in Calcutta or in Delhi.

**Statutory mechanics.**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "compensation for **wrong** done" | Defining phrase | Confined to **torts**. Section 19 does not apply to breach of contract, however wrongful; that is Section 20 territory. |
| "to the **person** or to **movable property**" | Subject-matter limiter | Assault, defamation, malicious prosecution, false imprisonment; conversion, detinue, damage to goods. Wrongs to *immovable* property go to Section 16(e). |
| "the suit **may** be instituted **at the option of the plaintiff**" | **Enabling / permissive** | A true election. Neither forum is superior; the defendant cannot compel a transfer merely because the other forum is more convenient (though S.22/S.24 remain available). |
| "in **either** of the said Courts" | Binary, not open-ended | Only two candidate fora: the place of the wrong, and the place of the defendant's residence/business/personal work for gain. |

**Condition precedent.** Section 19 is engaged only where the two places are **different**. If the wrong was done where the defendant resides, there is no election to make and the section is otiose.

::: proviso The condition the section never states

Section 19 contains no "provided that", but it has a condition precedent buried in its grammar, and it is the one most often missed:

> *if the wrong was done within the local limits of the jurisdiction of **one** Court **and** the defendant resides … within the local limits of the jurisdiction of **another** Court*

**One Court** and **another** Court. The section only operates where the two places are **different**. If Bharat punched Anita in Delhi and Bharat lives in Delhi, there is no election to make and Section 19 is silent — you are back in Section 20, and there you may also have Section 20(c) to work with.

This matters in drafting, not just in theory. A plaint that recites Section 19 as its jurisdictional foundation must plead **both** places and plead that they are different. A plaint that pleads only "the cause of action arose within the jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court" has pleaded a Section 20(c) case and should say so.
:::

::: proviso "Wrong" is a gate, not a description

The opening words — *compensation for **wrong** done* — are a gate. Pass through it and you get the election. Fail it and the section does not exist for you.

**Inside the gate:** assault, battery, false imprisonment, defamation, malicious prosecution, negligence causing personal injury; conversion, detinue, trespass to goods, negligent damage to movables.

**Outside the gate:** every claim whose source of duty is a **contract**, however wrongful the breach. Also every wrong to **immovable** property — that is Section 16(e) and it is not an election at all, it is a compulsion.

The word does not care what you call the claim. It cares where the duty came from. A plaint that pleads a failure to deliver goods and then adds "the said conduct being wrongful, high-handed and tortious" has not passed the gate; it has added an adjective. The court reads the source of the obligation, and if the obligation exists only because the parties made a bargain, the claim is contractual and the Section 19 forum collapses with it.

This is why the standard defence to a Section 19 election is not an attack on the election. It is an attack on the label.
:::

### In plain English

**Sub-module A — Why torts get an election.** A tortfeasor should not be able to insulate himself by committing the wrong far from home; equally, a plaintiff should not be forced to chase a defendant across the country. The Code therefore gives the victim the choice.

**Sub-module B — "Wrong done to the person".** Includes physical wrongs (assault, battery, false imprisonment) and wrongs to reputation and dignity (defamation, malicious prosecution). It does **not** extend to purely economic torts unconnected with person or movables — those are assessed under Section 20.

**Sub-module C — Where is a defamation "done"?** In the tort of libel, publication is the gist of the action. The wrong is done wherever the defamatory matter is **published** — that is, communicated to a third person. A single article circulated in five States is a wrong done in five places, giving the plaintiff five candidate fora under Section 19, plus the defendant's own. This is why defamation forum-shopping is endemic and why Sections 22 to 25 matter so much in this class of case.

**Sub-module D — Section 19 versus Section 20.** Section 19 is a *specific* provision for torts; Section 20 is the *residuary*. Where Section 19 applies, it is the governing provision, but note that its two options substantially overlap with Section 20's — "where the cause of action arises" (S.20(c)) will usually be the place of the wrong. The practical significance of Section 19 lies in its clarity, and in its express confirmation that the plaintiff, not the defendant, holds the election.

### Test your instinct

::::instinct The article nobody could prove anyone had read

A newspaper published from Delhi, with its registered office and printing press there, carries an article accusing Anita of siphoning charitable funds. Anita lives and works in Bengaluru. The paper has a Karnataka edition; it is sold at news-stands across the city; its website is accessible everywhere.

Anita sues in Bengaluru. Her plaint pleads, in a single sentence:

> "The said defamatory publication was widely circulated and read within the jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court, and the cause of action therefore arose within these local limits."

The defendant's written statement denies circulation within the local limits, denies that any identified person there read the article, and takes a preliminary objection to territorial jurisdiction.

**Anita's forum is lawful — a defamation is done where it is published, and this paper plainly circulates in Bengaluru. Is the objection hopeless?**

:::ruling

**No. The objection is not to her right of election; it is to the factual foundation she has failed to plead — and on this plaint it is a good objection.**

Two things are true at once, and Anita's counsel has conflated them.

**Her legal position is correct.** In libel, publication is the gist of the action. The wrong is done wherever the defamatory matter is communicated to a third person. A single article circulated in five States is a wrong done in five places, and Section 19 gives Anita the place of the wrong as one of her two doors. Bengaluru is a lawful forum.

**Her pleading is not.** "Widely circulated and read within the jurisdiction" is a conclusion of law dressed as a fact. *Dhodha House v. S.K. Maingi* is the direct answer: the mere possibility that goods might be sold, or an advertisement seen, within a jurisdiction does not by itself constitute part of the cause of action arising there — there must be a factual foundation pleaded and proved. The same logic, applied to a publication, means the bare assertion of circulation founds nothing.

**What the plaint had to say instead.** The date and number of the Karnataka edition. The print run and the circulation figures for the city. Named persons within the local limits who read the article and to whom Anita is known — her employer, her trustees, her neighbours — because publication to a person who does *not* know the plaintiff proves nothing about her reputation. The news-stands. If the online edition is relied on, the number of readers within the limits, not merely its accessibility.

**And the online point is separate.** Accessibility is not publication. A website reachable everywhere is not thereby published everywhere; there must be a download, a reading, by someone. Pleading "the article is available on the internet and hence within the jurisdiction of every court in India" is the purest form of the *Dhodha House* error.

**The remedy, and why it is urgent.** Anita should apply at once under Order VI Rule 17 to amend and supply the particulars. The urgency is not procedural politeness — it is Article 75 of the Limitation Act. Defamation carries a **one-year** period running from publication. If the plaint is returned under Order VII Rule 10 and re-presented at Delhi after that year has run, she is arguing Section 14 of the Limitation Act for exclusion of time, which requires her to show she was prosecuting the earlier proceeding in good faith in a court unable to entertain it for defect of jurisdiction. That is a case she can win, but it is a case she should never have had to fight.

::::instinct The seizure that was really a lien

Bharat trades at Kanpur, where he resides. Anita's consignment of textile machinery, worth ₹42,00,000, was lying in a godown at Surat. Bharat's men entered the godown, took the machinery and sold it to Chetan.

Anita sues at Surat for conversion. Surat is where the wrong was done; Kanpur is where Bharat lives; she elects Surat, where the transporter, the godown keeper and Chetan are all available.

Bharat's written statement does not deny taking the machinery. It pleads that the machinery was delivered to him under a works contract, that Anita defaulted on ₹31,00,000 of stage payments, that clause 14 of the contract gave him a lien and an express power of sale on default, and that he exercised it.

**Bharat has admitted the taking. Anita has elected a forum the Code expressly allows her. What is he actually achieving?**

:::ruling

**He is not defending the taking. He is attacking the label — and if he succeeds, Anita's forum disappears with it.**

Section 19 opens with the words *compensation for **wrong** done*. That is a gate. It admits claims in **tort**. If Bharat establishes that the machinery was in his hands under a contract, that the contract conferred a lien and a power of sale, and that he acted within it, then the dispute is about a **contract** — whether the default occurred, whether the clause was validly exercised, what the accounting is. There is no tort, and there never was a Section 19 election.

**Where the suit then goes.** Section 20. And on these facts Section 20 may well take Anita somewhere she did not choose: the defendant resides at Kanpur, and the cause of action on a contract analysis arises where the contract was made, where it was to be performed, and where it was breached — which on Bharat's version is wherever the works contract was performed and the payments fell due, quite possibly Kanpur as well. Surat may end up with nothing.

**Note what the court is examining.** Not the language of the plaint. The **source of the duty**. Anita's plaint may say "wrongful", "high-handed", "tortious" as often as it likes; adjectives do not create a duty in tort. The question is whether Bharat's obligation not to take the machinery arose independently of any bargain between them, or only because they had a bargain.

**The exception Anita should be pleading.** A lien is a defence to conversion only so far as it goes. If Bharat had a lien, a lien confers a right to **retain**, and a power of sale only if the contract expressly gives one. Anita should attack clause 14 on its own terms — whether it authorised sale at all, whether notice was a condition of exercising it, whether the sale to Chetan was at an undervalue, and whether the sale of machinery worth ₹42,00,000 to satisfy ₹31,00,000 was within the power. A sale that exceeds the contractual power is a conversion again, and the tort revives.

**The tactical order matters.** Bharat should press the characterisation point as a preliminary issue under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a), because if he wins it he gets a return of the plaint under Order VII Rule 10 before any evidence on the merits. Anita should resist a preliminary issue precisely because the characterisation cannot honestly be decided without evidence on what clause 14 permitted and what Bharat actually did.
::::

::: warning Drafting trap — an adjective is not a cause of action

A plaint that pleads a breach of contract and then adds "and the said conduct is also wrongful and tortious" has not converted a contract claim into a tort claim. The court looks at the substance of the pleading and the source of the duty breached. A forum founded on Section 19 collapses the moment the court holds the real claim to be contractual.

Where a genuine tort **does** co-exist with a contract — a bailee who destroys the goods, a professional whose negligence is independently actionable — plead the two causes of action in **separate, numbered paragraphs**, each identifying its own duty, breach and damage. Do not run them together in one narrative. The forum depends on the tort being visibly self-standing on the face of the plaint.
:::

::::instinct The prosecution that began in one city and ended in another

Bharat resides at Jaipur. From Jaipur he lodges a complaint against Anita, which is tried before a magistrate at Ajmer. Anita attends Ajmer for two years and is acquitted, the magistrate recording that the complaint was actuated by malice. She sues Bharat for malicious prosecution, at Ajmer.

Bharat objects. The complaint, he says, was lodged at Jaipur; the setting of the criminal law in motion — the act complained of — happened at Jaipur; what happened at Ajmer was the court's doing, not his. The *situs* of the wrong is Jaipur, and Ajmer has no territorial jurisdiction. Separately, and in the alternative, he applies under Section 24 for transfer to Jaipur, where he says all his witnesses are.

**Two objections, both aimed at Jaipur. How does each fare?**

:::ruling

**The jurisdiction objection fails. The transfer application is the real fight — and Bharat has damaged it by making both.**

**On jurisdiction.** Malicious prosecution is not complete when the complaint is lodged. Its ingredients are that the defendant prosecuted the plaintiff, without reasonable and probable cause, maliciously, and that the **prosecution terminated in the plaintiff's favour**, causing damage. The cause of action does not accrue until acquittal. A wrong whose final constituent element occurs at Ajmer is a wrong done, at least in part, at Ajmer.

Bharat's argument also proves too much. On his logic a complainant could lodge from home and thereafter be answerable only at home, however far he dragged his victim — which inverts the whole reason Section 19 gives the election to the person wronged.

**And even on his own framing, he loses.** Suppose the *situs* really is split between Jaipur and Ajmer. Section 19 gives Anita the place of the wrong *or* Bharat's residence. Bharat resides at Jaipur. If the wrong is done only at Jaipur, both of Anita's doors open onto the same court and Section 19 becomes otiose — but Anita is at Ajmer, so she must be relying on Ajmer as the place of the wrong, and the acquittal there is what founds it. His argument cannot both split the *situs* and deny her the half she has chosen.

**On transfer.** This is Bharat's genuine remedy, and it is a different question entirely: not *may* the suit be here, but *should* it be. Convenience of witnesses, the balance of hardship, the location of records. Section 22 would let him ask the court itself, but only where the suit **could have been instituted in more than one court** and only **before the settlement of issues**; Section 24 lets the High Court transfer at any stage; Section 25 is for the Supreme Court where the competing forums are in different States, which these are not.

**Why the two objections sit badly together.** A jurisdiction objection asserts the court has no power. A transfer application concedes it has power and asks it to be exercised elsewhere. Taking both is permissible, but leading with the bad point costs Bharat credibility on the good one, and every month spent losing the preliminary issue is a month closer to the settlement of issues, after which Section 22 closes to him permanently.

**Anita's own clock.** Article 74 gives her **one year** from the acquittal — not from the complaint, and not three years. That single date should be the first entry in the file.

::::instinct The election that was never there

Anita is knocked down by Bharat's delivery van in Pune. Bharat's transport business is registered in Pune, operates from a depot in Pune, and Bharat himself lives in Pune. Anita, who lives in Pune too, sues in Pune for ₹18,00,000.

Her plaint recites, under the heading of jurisdiction:

> "The wrong was done within the local limits of the jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court and the Defendant carries on business within the said limits. The plaintiff exercises her option under Section 19 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 to institute the present suit before this Hon'ble Court."

The defence takes no jurisdiction objection — there is nothing to object to. But an issue is framed on the jurisdictional averments.

**Is the recital merely surplus, or has Anita created a problem for herself?**

:::ruling

**Section 19 was never engaged, and pleading it is not free.**

The section applies only where the wrong was done within the limits of **one** Court *and* the defendant resides or carries on business within the limits of **another**. One court and another court. Here there is only one court. Both routes lead to Pune, there is no option to exercise, and Section 19 is otiose. The correct foundation is **Section 20** — clause (a), the defendant's residence and place of business, and clause (c), the place where the cause of action arose, and Anita has both.

**Why the wrong label is not harmless.** Three reasons, in ascending order of seriousness.

*It invites an issue.* A pleaded reliance on Section 19 puts the applicability of Section 19 in issue, and Anita must then explain at trial why she pleaded a provision that on her own facts does not apply. That is a small humiliation, but it is an avoidable one.

*It signals a misunderstanding.* Where a claim is genuinely both contractual and tortious — a carrier's liability, a bailment, a professional's default — a reflexive Section 19 recital tells the other side that the pleader has not thought about which cause of action the forum rests on. That is exactly the seam a competent defence will open.

*It obscures the fallback.* The reason to plead Section 20 in the alternative, always, is that Section 19 depends on the claim surviving as a tort. If it is recharacterised as contractual and the plaint has pleaded no Section 20 foundation, there is nothing left standing. A plaint that pleads Section 20(a) and 20(c) with particulars, and Section 19 only where it genuinely adds a forum, cannot be knocked out by a change of label.

**The rule to take away.** Plead the facts that found jurisdiction — where the defendant resides, where he carries on business, where each constituent of the cause of action arose — and let the section follow from the facts. Do not plead the section and hope the facts follow from it.

::::instinct The objection that arrived after the issues

Anita sues Bharat at Chennai for conversion of goods. Bharat resides and trades at Hyderabad; Anita says the goods were seized at Chennai; Bharat says they were seized at Hyderabad and that the Chennai court has no territorial jurisdiction.

But Bharat does not say so at first. He files a written statement contesting the seizure on the merits and pleading limitation, with a single sentence at the end: "The suit is otherwise not maintainable." Issues are settled. Anita's evidence is recorded over nine months. On the day Bharat's own evidence is to begin, he files an application contending that the Chennai court lacks territorial jurisdiction and that the plaint must be returned.

**Assume Bharat is factually right and the seizure did occur at Hyderabad. Does he get his return of plaint?**

:::ruling

**No — on either of two independent grounds, and he has also destroyed his better remedy.**

**Ground one — Section 21(1).** No objection as to the place of suing shall be allowed by any appellate or revisional court unless it was taken in the court of first instance **at the earliest possible opportunity** and, in all cases where issues are settled, at or before such settlement — *and* unless there has been a **consequent failure of justice**. The provision codifies a policy: territorial competence is for the benefit of the parties, not a matter of the court's fundamental power, and it can be waived. Bharat waived it. A written statement contesting the merits, followed by nine months of cross-examination, is waiver by conduct however the pleading is worded.

**Ground two — "otherwise not maintainable" is not an objection.** A general averment of non-maintainability does not preserve a specific plea. Order VI Rule 2 requires material facts; a jurisdictional objection must identify *which* jurisdiction is said to be absent and *why*, so that the plaintiff can meet it and the court can frame an issue on it. No issue was framed here because nothing was pleaded that an issue could be framed on.

**And note the second half of Section 21(1), which practitioners forget.** Even a timely objection is not enough on appeal. There must be a **consequent failure of justice**. Bharat has contested on the merits, led no evidence yet, and can point to no prejudice from the trial being at Chennai rather than Hyderabad beyond travel — which is inconvenience, not failure of justice.

**What he should have done.** Two things, at the threshold. Pleaded the objection specifically in the written statement, with the facts of the Hyderabad seizure, and applied under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a) for it to be tried as a preliminary issue. And in the alternative — because the point might fail — applied under **Section 22** for transfer to Hyderabad, which was open to him only while the suit could have been instituted in more than one court and only **before the settlement of issues**. Both doors were open on the day he filed his written statement. Both are shut now.

**What remains to him.** Section 24, in the High Court, at any stage — but a transfer application filed on the eve of his own evidence, after nine months of the plaintiff's, will be read as delay and will almost certainly be refused. The costs of a wasted trial, if the point had merit, are his own.

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

**The one case to have ready:** *Dhodha House v. S.K. Maingi*, (2006) 9 SCC 41 — 2 Judges.

*Facts:* The forum for passing-off and infringement actions, and whether the availability of goods within a territory is enough to found jurisdiction there.

**The ratio to plead:** the mere possibility that goods might be sold, or an advertisement might be seen, within a jurisdiction does not by itself constitute a part of the cause of action arising there. There must be a factual foundation **pleaded and proved**.

**Why this is the sentence that wins Section 19 cases.** Almost no Section 19 fight is about which of the two courts the plaintiff may choose — the Code is clear that the choice is hers. The fights are about whether the wrong was really *done* where she says it was. In a defamation, a passing off, a conversion of goods that moved, the plaintiff's jurisdictional averment is nearly always a conclusion: "the said publication was widely circulated within these limits", "the said goods were available for sale within these limits". *Dhodha House* is the authority that a conclusion is not enough.

> "A bare assertion of circulation, availability or accessibility within the forum, unsupported by pleaded particulars, cannot found territorial jurisdiction; the constituent facts of the cause of action must be pleaded and proved, not asserted."

**Use it in both directions.** As **defendant**, it converts a hopeless-looking objection into a demand for particulars the plaintiff often cannot supply. As **plaintiff**, read it before you draft: it tells you exactly what the plaint must contain — names, dates, editions, figures, transactions — so that the objection never gets off the ground.

::: info Verify before citing
*Dhodha House* arises in the intellectual-property context and has been considered in later decisions on the situs of a cause of action. Verify the report, the bench strength and the subsequent treatment before placing it before a court on a pure Section 19 point, and be ready to explain the analogy from an IP forum question to a tort forum question.
:::

#### Sword and shield

| | Plaintiff (sword) | Defendant (shield) |
|---|---|---|
| **The claim's character** | Plead the tort as a self-standing cause of action in its own numbered paragraphs — the duty, the breach, the damage — independent of any contract between the parties | Recharacterise: show the duty arose only from a contract, lien, bailment or statutory relationship, so that Section 19 never applied and the forum falls with the label |
| **The situs of the wrong** | Plead constituent facts with particulars: named readers, the local edition, circulation figures, the place of seizure, the place of the sale, the date of acquittal | Deny circulation, availability or occurrence within the limits, and put the plaintiff to strict proof — *Dhodha House* |
| **The election** | It is yours alone. Neither forum outranks the other, and inconvenience to the defendant is irrelevant to jurisdiction | Do not attack the election. Attack its factual base, or move for transfer — those are the only two things that work |
| **Fallback pleading** | Always plead **Section 20(a) and 20(c)** in the alternative, with facts, so the forum survives a recharacterisation | Where the plaint has pleaded Section 19 alone, say so: a successful recharacterisation then leaves no pleaded foundation at all |
| **Convenience** | Resist a Section 22 application by pointing to the balance of hardship on **your** side — a plaintiff already wronged should not be sent to the wrongdoer's home court | **Section 22** — before the settlement of issues, where the suit could have been instituted in more than one court; **Section 24** — High Court, any stage; **Section 25** — Supreme Court, inter-State |
| **Timing of the objection** | If the objection comes late, invoke **Section 21(1)**: not taken at the earliest opportunity, and no consequent failure of justice | Take it specifically in the written statement, with facts, and apply under **Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a)** for a preliminary issue. "The suit is otherwise not maintainable" preserves nothing |
| **Quantum** | Value unliquidated damages honestly and defensibly — the figure drives both court fee and the Section 15 grade | Attack an arbitrary round figure as a device to choose the forum, and ask for the valuation to be tried as an issue |

#### The limitation clock

Section 19 cases die of limitation more often than of any defence on the merits, because the periods are **one year**, not three, and because the consequence of a forum error is a return of the plaint after the year has run.

| Event | Effect |
|---|---|
| Defamation — libel (**Art. 75**), slander (**Art. 76**), malicious prosecution (**Art. 74**), wrongful confinement (**Art. 78**), wrongful seizure under legal process (**Art. 79**) | **One year.** Diarise the date of publication, of acquittal or of release on the day the brief arrives |
| Plaint returned under **Order VII Rule 10** | The suit is instituted afresh on **re-presentation**. The earlier period is excluded only by pleading and proving **Section 14, Limitation Act, 1963** — good faith, due diligence, and inability to entertain for defect of jurisdiction |
| Order returning a plaint | Appealable under **Order XLIII Rule 1(a)**. Where the limitation position is fatal, appeal rather than re-present and hope |
| Amendment supplying jurisdictional particulars | **Order VI Rule 17**. Amendments correcting particulars of an existing cause of action generally relate back; an amendment introducing a **new** cause of action after the period has run does not |

::: danger Fatal error — the one-year clock and the returned plaint
A defamation plaint filed on day 300 in the wrong court, objected to on day 400 and returned on day 700, is re-presented in a court that will treat it as instituted on day 700 — four hundred days after publication, on a one-year article. Every part of that sequence is ordinary and none of it is anyone's fault but the pleader's. The two defences are to plead the jurisdictional facts with particulars in the original plaint, and to plead Section 14 of the Limitation Act with dates, annexing the returned plaint bearing the complete Rule 10(2) endorsement, in the re-presented plaint itself.
:::

#### The authorities

**Kusum Ingots & Alloys Ltd. v. Union of India**, (2004) 6 SCC 254 — 3 Judges.
*Facts:* Whether the situs of a legislative or executive act confers jurisdiction, in the context of the "cause of action" doctrine under Article 226(2).
**Ratio decidendi:** The court laid down that a *part* of the cause of action arising within a forum confers jurisdiction, but the doctrine of *forum conveniens* permits the court to decline to exercise it. Though decided in the writ context, the reasoning on the constituent facts of a cause of action is regularly applied to Sections 19 and 20.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Even where a fraction of the cause of action arises within these limits, this Hon'ble Court retains the discretion, on forum conveniens principles, to decline to exercise jurisdiction."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Kusum Ingots is a writ-jurisdiction decision, applied to Sections 19 and 20 only by analogy — its *forum conveniens* reasoning is a discretion recognised in Article 226 practice, and it does not translate directly into a power to decline a Section 19 election, which the Code confers on the plaintiff in terms. Verify the report, the bench strength and the subsequent treatment before placing it before a civil court on a pure Section 19 point.
:::

#### Strategy and drafting

**Drafting traps.**

1. **Asserting circulation without particulars.** "The said defamatory publication was widely circulated within the jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court" is a conclusion, not a fact. Plead named readers, distribution figures, the local edition, the date.
2. **Mixing contract and tort in one undifferentiated narrative.** If the forum depends on Section 19, keep the tortious cause of action separately and distinctly pleaded, with its own paragraph identifying the duty, the breach, and the damage.
3. **Missing the one-year limitation.** See the danger box above.
4. **Claiming unliquidated damages without a valuation basis.** In tort suits the plaintiff must still value the claim for jurisdiction and pay court fee; an arbitrary round figure invites objection.

**Strategic timing.**

- As **plaintiff**, elect early and irrevocably. Filing in two fora invites Section 10 (*res sub judice*) and a Section 22 or Section 25 transfer application against you.
- As **defendant**, your Section 19 forum objection must be taken at the earliest opportunity under Section 21; but where the forum is lawful and merely inconvenient, the objection is misconceived — move instead under Section 22 (before settlement of issues) or Section 24.

**Model prayer checklist — plaint for defamation.**

> **(a)** decree a sum of ₹[amount] together with interest at [rate]% per annum from the date of publication till realisation, by way of compensation for the defamatory publication dated [date];
> **(b)** a decree of permanent injunction restraining the Defendants, their servants and agents, from further publishing, republishing, circulating or disseminating the said defamatory imputations or any imputation of like effect;
> **(c)** a mandatory injunction directing the Defendants to publish an unconditional apology and retraction with the same prominence, in the same publication, within [number] days;
> **(d)** a direction for delivery up and destruction of all unsold copies containing the impugned publication;
> **(e)** costs of and incidental to the suit; and
> **(f)** such further or other relief as the nature and circumstances of the case may require.

## Section 20 — Other suits to be instituted where defendants reside or cause of action arises

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Three doors, and the third one is wide enough to drive a lorry through.

Everything that is not a suit about land (Section 16) and not a suit for a tort (Section 19) ends up here. Section 20 is where most litigation in India actually lives — every contract, every recovery suit, every commercial dispute.

It gives Anita three doors, and they are **alternatives**, not steps:

- **(a)** the court where **the defendant** — or *every* defendant, if there are several — actually and voluntarily resides, carries on business, or personally works for gain;
- **(b)** the court where **any one** of several defendants does so — but only with the **leave of the court**, or the others' acquiescence;
- **(c)** the court where **the cause of action, wholly or in part, arises.**

Clause (c) is the workhorse. A *fraction* of the cause of action is enough. And the Explanation adds a fourth route against companies: a corporation is suable at its **principal office** always, and at a **subordinate office** only where the cause of action arose there.

Two traps sit at the front of the section and catch people every week. If you are suing several defendants and only one is local, **you must ask for leave in the plaint**. And "this Hon'ble Court has jurisdiction" is not a pleading — Order VII Rule 1(f) demands *facts*.
:::

::: story The residuary rule, and why the drafting is upside-down

Sections 15 to 19 each answer a narrow question. Section 15 sets the *grade* of court. Section 16 deals with land. Section 19 deals with torts. Section 20 opens with the words *"Subject to the limitations aforesaid"* and then catches everything left over — which turns out to be almost everything.

Read the section once and it looks like a command: *every suit **shall** be instituted in a Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction —*. Read it twice and you see the trick. The frame is mandatory; the content is **disjunctive**. Clause (a) **or** clause (b) **or** clause (c). The plaintiff is obliged to pick one of the three — but which one is entirely her choice, and the three overlap constantly. In a supply contract gone wrong, the defendant's registered office, its branch, the place of the purchase order, the place of delivery and the place where payment was due may sit in four different States, and every one of them may be a lawful forum.

That breadth is deliberate, and it is also the source of every abuse. Once a *fraction* of the cause of action is enough, plaintiffs start manufacturing fractions. The classic manoeuvre is to plead a fact that is local but not **material**: *"the plaintiff received the repudiation letter at his residence at Chandigarh"*, *"the plaintiff issued the legal notice from Chandigarh"*, *"the plaintiff's bank is at Chandigarh"*, *"the plaintiff suffered loss at Chandigarh"*. None of these is a constituent of the cause of action. The cause of action is the bundle of **material facts the plaintiff must plead and prove to obtain the decree** — not the evidence, not the aftermath, and certainly not the plaintiff's own address.

The Code answers this abuse in three ways, and they are worth holding together in the mind, because a defendant confronted with a manufactured forum usually reaches for the wrong one.

**First, the materiality filter.** If the local fact is not one the plaintiff has to prove, it founds nothing, and the objection is a true jurisdictional objection.

**Second, the parties' own agreement.** Where two or more courts are competent, the parties may contract to use only one of them, and that is not a restraint on legal proceedings — it is a selection among lawful fora. But the limit is absolute in the other direction: **consent cannot manufacture jurisdiction.** A clause naming Delhi in a Kolkata-to-Kolkata loan is void and inoperative, and the Delhi court must return the plaint however emphatically both parties signed.

**Third, transfer.** Where the forum is lawful but oppressive, the answer is Sections 22 to 25, not an objection to the plaint.

And then, on top of all of it, sit the modern gatekeepers that have nothing to do with Section 20 and will still get your plaint rejected: **Section 80** notice where the Government is a defendant, and **Section 12A** pre-institution mediation where the dispute is a commercial dispute of Specified Value and no genuine urgent interim relief is sought. A perfect Section 20 forum does not save a plaint that fails either of them.

### The Act, decompiled

Three clauses joined by **or**, a proviso hidden inside clause (b), and an Explanation that is narrower than it looks. Watch four things as you read: the subordinating opening, the double qualifier *actually and voluntarily*, the temporal fix at the *commencement of the suit*, and the words *wholly or in part*.

> **20. Other suits to be instituted where defendants reside or cause of action arises.** — Subject to the limitations aforesaid, every suit [[!shall be instituted]] in a Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction —
> **(a)** the defendant, or each of the defendants where there are more than one, [[=at the time of the commencement of the suit]], [[=actually and voluntarily]] resides, or carries on business, or personally works for gain; or
> **(b)** any of the defendants, where there are more than one, [[=at the time of the commencement of the suit]], [[=actually and voluntarily]] resides, or carries on business, or personally works for gain, provided that in such case either the [[!leave of the Court is given]], or the defendants who do not reside, or carry on business, or personally work for gain, as aforesaid, [[=acquiesce]] in such institution; or
> **(c)** the [[=cause of action]], [[=wholly or in part]], arises.
>
> ***Explanation.*** — A corporation [[!shall be deemed]] to carry on business at its [[=sole or principal office in India]] or, in respect of any cause of action arising at any place where it has also a [[=subordinate office]], at such place.

**Statutory mechanics.**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "**Subject to the limitations aforesaid**" | Subordinating clause | Section 20 is the **residuary**. It yields to Sections 15 to 19. If the suit is one for immovable property, Section 16 governs and Section 20 is irrelevant. |
| "every suit **shall** be instituted in a Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction — (a) … **or** (b) … **or** (c)" | Mandatory frame, **disjunctive** content | The obligation is mandatory, but the three limbs are alternatives. The plaintiff elects among them. |
| "**actually and voluntarily** resides" | Double qualifier | Excludes both fictional residence (a mere postal address) and involuntary residence (imprisonment, hospitalisation, forced posting). |
| "at the **time of the commencement of the suit**" | Temporal fixing | Jurisdiction is tested on the date of institution. A defendant who moves house the following week cannot unseat the forum. |
| Clause (b) proviso — "**either the leave of the Court is given, or** the [other] defendants … **acquiesce**" | **Condition precedent** | Where you sue several defendants and only one is within the limits, you must obtain leave *before or at institution*, or plead acquiescence. This is routinely forgotten. |
| "the cause of action, **wholly or in part**, arises" | Deliberately wide | A *fraction* suffices. This is the busiest limb in commercial litigation. |
| **Explanation** (corporations) | Deeming provision | Principal office always works. A subordinate office works **only** if the cause of action arose at that place. |

**The Explanation is narrower than it looks.** Read carefully, it gives two rules, not one:

1. A corporation is deemed to carry on business at its **sole or principal office in India** — always, for any cause of action.
2. A corporation is deemed to carry on business at a **subordinate office** — but only "in respect of any cause of action arising at any place where it has also a subordinate office".

So the mere existence of a branch within the forum does not confer jurisdiction. The cause of action must have arisen at that branch.

::: proviso The proviso inside clause (b) — leave, or acquiescence, and nothing else

Clause (b) is the only limb of Section 20 that carries an express proviso, and it is the single most-forgotten condition precedent in the Code:

> *provided that in such case either the **leave of the Court is given**, or the defendants who do not reside … **acquiesce** in such institution*

The structure is strict. You are suing several defendants. **Only one of them** is within the local limits. That one defendant does not carry the suit by himself — the section requires, in addition, **either**:

- the **leave of the court**, which must be sought at institution, not after the objection is taken; **or**
- the **acquiescence** of the non-resident defendants, which means submitting without objection, and which you cannot know about when you file.

Since you cannot rely on acquiescence in advance, the only safe course is to ask for leave. Plead it in a numbered paragraph and carry it into the prayer.

**Is the omission fatal?** Not automatically — Section 21 treats objections to the place of suing as waivable, so a defendant who says nothing until appeal has lost the point. But a defendant who objects at the first hearing and shows real prejudice will succeed, and the cure was three lines of drafting. This is a trap that costs nothing to avoid and years to survive.

**And do not confuse it with Letters Patent leave.** The Original Side of the Bombay, Calcutta and Madras High Courts has its own leave regime under Clause 12 of the Letters Patent, on different conditions. Obtaining one is not obtaining the other.
:::

::: proviso The Explanation is a sword for the plaintiff, not a shield for the company

Read the Explanation slowly, because it does two separate things and defendants routinely quote only the half that suits them:

> *A corporation shall be deemed to carry on business **at its sole or principal office in India** **or**, **in respect of any cause of action arising at any place where it has also a subordinate office**, at such place.*

**Rule one:** the principal office in India always counts — for any cause of action, whatever its situs.

**Rule two:** a subordinate office counts **only** where the cause of action arose at that place. The mere existence of a branch within the forum confers nothing. A company with fifty branches is not suable in fifty districts.

That much is the plain reading, and it cuts against plaintiffs. But the second half of the rule has a sting that cuts the other way, and it is the point of *Patel Roadways*: where the cause of action **did** arise at the branch, the corporation **cannot** insist on being sued at its registered office instead. The Explanation exists to spare the plaintiff a journey to the company's head office. It is not a device by which the company selects its own forum.

So the Explanation produces a rule with two edges. A branch that had nothing to do with the transaction is irrelevant. A branch that did everything is decisive — and the location of the registered office then becomes beside the point.
:::

### In plain English

**Sub-module A — "Actually and voluntarily resides".** Residence means something more settled than presence and something less permanent than domicile. The test is a real, physical, habitual abode, chosen freely. A person may have more than one residence; he may not have a merely notional one. A defendant who maintains a locked flat he never occupies does not "actually" reside there; a defendant confined in prison within the limits does not reside there "voluntarily".

**Sub-module B — "Carries on business".** Three ingredients are usually required: (i) a real, substantial and systematic course of business activity, not an isolated transaction; (ii) carried on at a fixed place within the limits; and (iii) carried on by the defendant himself or through an agent whose acts bind him. An agent who merely canvasses orders subject to acceptance elsewhere does not make his principal carry on business at the place of canvassing.

**Sub-module C — "Personally works for gain".** Directed at the employed and the self-employed who have no "business" as such — a salaried manager, a practising professional, a labourer. The word **personally** excludes work done through others.

**Sub-module D — Clause (b) and the leave requirement.** This is the most-litigated procedural trap in Section 20. Where there are several defendants and only one of them satisfies the residence/business test within the forum, the plaint is maintainable **only if**:

- the court's **leave** has been obtained (ordinarily applied for in the plaint itself or by a separate application at presentation), **or**
- the non-resident defendants **acquiesce** — that is, submit without objection.

The consequence of omitting leave is not automatically fatal, because Section 21 forgives place-of-suing defects; but a defendant who objects promptly, before settlement of issues, and shows prejudice, will succeed. The cure is trivial and the omission is inexcusable: **ask for leave in the plaint**.

**Sub-module E — "Cause of action, wholly or in part".** The cause of action is the bundle of *material facts* which the plaintiff must plead and prove to obtain the decree — not the evidence by which he proves them, and not facts that are merely incidental. Contrast:

| Fact | Part of the cause of action? |
|---|---|
| Place where the contract was negotiated and concluded | **Yes** |
| Place where the contract was to be performed | **Yes** |
| Place where the goods were delivered or rejected | **Yes** |
| Place where the payment was to be made | **Yes** |
| Place where the breach occurred | **Yes** |
| Place where the plaintiff resides and "suffered loss" | **Ordinarily no** — mere residence of the plaintiff is not a constituent fact |
| Place from which a notice of demand was posted by the plaintiff | **Ordinarily no** |
| Place where the plaintiff's bank is situated | **Ordinarily no**, unless payment was contractually to be made there |

**Sub-module F — Ouster clauses and exclusive jurisdiction agreements.** Where two or more courts have jurisdiction under Section 20, the parties may by contract agree that only one of them shall be resorted to. Such a clause is **not** hit by Section 28 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, because it does not absolutely restrain legal proceedings — it merely selects among competent fora. But the converse is fundamental: **parties cannot by agreement confer jurisdiction on a court that has none.** A clause naming a court where neither defendant resides and no part of the cause of action arose is void and inoperative.

The construction rule matters: where the clause uses words of exclusivity — "only", "alone", "exclusively", "and no other court" — the ouster is clear. Where it merely says "the courts at Mumbai shall have jurisdiction", the *expressio unius* maxim is generally applied to read in exclusivity, but the point remains arguable and turns on the whole contract.

### Test your instinct

::::instinct The branch that did all the work

Anita manufactures components at Pune. She sues Bharat Industries Ltd. for ₹1,20,00,000 unpaid on a supply contract. The company's registered office is at Chennai. It has a branch at Pune.

Everything happened at Pune. The purchase order was issued on the Pune branch's letterhead, by the Pune branch manager. The goods were delivered to the Pune godown. Clause 9 said payment was to be made at Pune. The cheques that bounced were drawn on a Pune bank.

Anita files at Pune. The written statement takes two points. First, clause 22 of the standard terms says: *"All disputes shall be subject to the jurisdiction of the courts at Chennai."* Second, the company says it is suable only where its registered office is.

**Two objections from a company that did all of its business with Anita in Pune. How far does each get?**

:::ruling

**The second objection is bad on the statute. The first is a real fight, and it is the one that decides the case.**

**The registered-office point fails, and it fails on the Explanation's own words.** A corporation is deemed to carry on business at its principal office in India **or**, in respect of a cause of action arising at a place where it also has a subordinate office, at that place. The cause of action arose at Pune and the company has a subordinate office at Pune. *Patel Roadways Ltd. v. Prasad Trading Co.* is directly in point: where a corporation has a subordinate office at the place where the cause of action arises, it cannot be heard to say that it is suable only at its principal office. The Explanation exists to spare the plaintiff a journey to the head office; it is not a shield for the company.

Anita also has an entirely independent route that does not need the Explanation at all. **Clause (c)**: the order, the delivery, the place of payment and the breach are each constituent facts, and each arose at Pune. Not a fraction — the whole of it. She should plead both limbs, separately, so that a failure on one does not take the suit with it.

**The jurisdiction clause is the real problem.** Test it in two steps, in this order.

*Step one — does Chennai independently qualify?* Yes. The registered office is there, so clause (a) and the first half of the Explanation both reach it. This is therefore a clause **selecting among competent courts**, not one manufacturing competence, and it is not hit by Section 28 of the Contract Act.

*Step two — is the selection exclusive?* The clause says "subject to the jurisdiction of the courts at Chennai" without the word "only" or "exclusively". Under *A.B.C. Laminart*, the absence of exclusionary words meant the ouster was not to be readily inferred. Under *Swastik Gases Pvt. Ltd. v. Indian Oil Corporation Ltd.*, a three-Judge bench, the absence of such words is **not decisive** — where a clause names a competent court, the intendment is to exclude the others, because otherwise the clause means nothing. The later and larger bench is against Anita.

**So how does Anita actually win?** Not by arguing about "only". By attacking the clause's application:

- **Was it a term of this contract at all?** Clause 22 is in the *standard terms*. Were they incorporated into the purchase order the Pune branch issued? Were they on the reverse, referred to on the face, signed, or merely printed on an invoice sent afterwards? A clause that never became a term cannot exclude anything.
- **Does its language cover this dispute?** "Disputes" under the contract may not reach a claim on dishonoured cheques, which has its own cause of action.
- **Is it a contract of adhesion on unequal terms?** A live argument, though not a strong one standing alone.

**And note what Anita must not do.** She must not treat the clause as beneath notice because Pune is so obviously the natural forum. Convenience is irrelevant to an exclusive jurisdiction clause between competent courts. If the clause is a term and it covers the dispute, Pune goes and Chennai stays, however absurd the journey looks.
::::

::::instinct The clause naming a court nobody had ever been to

Bharat, a lender at Kolkata, advances ₹75,00,000 to Anita, a borrower at Kolkata. The loan agreement is executed at Kolkata. The money is disbursed from a Kolkata branch to a Kolkata account. Repayment is due at Kolkata. Anita defaults.

Clause 18 of the agreement reads: *"The Courts at New Delhi shall have exclusive jurisdiction in respect of all disputes arising out of this Agreement."*

Bharat sues at New Delhi, relying on the clause. Anita is a small borrower who cannot easily travel; Bharat's counsel has advised that the clause is binding, that Anita signed it, and that *Swastik Gases* makes exclusive-jurisdiction clauses effective without the word "only" — and here the clause even has the word "exclusive".

**Anita signed it. Both sides agreed. Is Bharat right?**

:::ruling

**No, and the error is fundamental. This is the one situation in which a jurisdiction clause simply fails.**

Every authority on ouster clauses rests on a precondition that is stated so often it stops being read: parties may agree to confine a dispute to **one of the courts that already has jurisdiction under the Code**. *A.B.C. Laminart* and *Swastik Gases* are both about choosing *among competent fora*. Neither says anything about a court that has no competence at all, because **consent cannot confer territorial jurisdiction**.

Run New Delhi through Section 20 and it fails every limb. Anita does not reside, carry on business or personally work for gain at Delhi — clause (a) and (b) are out. No part of the cause of action arose at Delhi: the agreement was made at Kolkata, disbursed at Kolkata, repayable at Kolkata, and breached at Kolkata — clause (c) is out. There is no corporation and no branch, so the Explanation is irrelevant.

The clause is therefore **void and inoperative**, and the Delhi court must return the plaint under Order VII Rule 10. Section 28 of the Contract Act is not even reached; the clause fails before that, on the simple ground that there is nothing for it to select between.

**Two refinements worth having ready.**

*The clause is not wholly useless to Bharat.* If a Delhi connection had existed — a guarantor at Delhi, a disbursement from a Delhi account, a place of performance at Delhi — the clause would spring to life, because then Delhi would independently qualify. So test the facts before conceding, and test them again if the plaint is amended.

*Anita must still object properly.* Section 21(1) applies to this defect as to any other place-of-suing objection. If she files a written statement on the merits and litigates for two years before taking the point, she will have waived it, and on appeal she would additionally have to show a consequent failure of justice. A void clause does not object on her behalf. She must take the point at the earliest possible opportunity, before the settlement of issues, and apply under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a) to have it tried as a preliminary issue.

**The drafting lesson runs the other way too.** Before you insert a jurisdiction clause naming your client's home city, check that the city independently qualifies under Section 20 for the transaction you are papering. A clause naming a court with no connection to the deal is not a mild overreach — it is a nullity that will cost your client the return of his own plaint years later.
::::

::::instinct The three guarantors and the three missing lines

Anita sues three guarantors on a defaulted facility: Bharat at Lucknow, Chetan at Indore, Deepak at Nagpur. The facility was sanctioned, disbursed and defaulted at Indore — the whole cause of action arose there.

Anita files at Lucknow, where Bharat resides. Her jurisdiction paragraph says: *"Defendant No. 1 resides within the local limits of the jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court, and this Hon'ble Court therefore has jurisdiction to try the present suit."* She does not apply for leave.

Chetan and Deepak file a joint written statement at the first hearing. They deny acquiescence, point to the absence of leave, and say the cause of action arose wholly at Indore. They set out that their businesses, their records, their auditors and their witnesses are all at Indore and Nagpur, and that defending at Lucknow would require each of them to maintain counsel and travel roughly a thousand kilometres for every hearing.

**Anita will say the defect is curable and that Section 21 forgives place-of-suing errors. Does it forgive this one?**

:::ruling

**No. Section 21 forgives objections that are not taken. This one was taken at the first hearing, and prejudice has been pleaded with particulars.**

Work through it in the order the Code does.

**Clause (a) is unavailable.** It requires *each* of the defendants to be within the limits. Chetan and Deepak are not.

**Clause (c) is unavailable.** The whole cause of action arose at Indore. Not a fraction of it arose at Lucknow. Anita cannot reach clause (c) at all.

**So Anita is necessarily in clause (b)** — one of several defendants within the limits — and clause (b) carries its proviso: *either the leave of the Court is given, or* the non-resident defendants *acquiesce*. She has neither. Acquiescence is gone the moment Chetan and Deepak object, and they objected at the first hearing.

**What Section 21(1) actually does.** It bars an *appellate or revisional* court from allowing a place-of-suing objection unless it was taken in the trial court at the earliest possible opportunity, before the settlement of issues, **and** there has been a consequent failure of justice. It is a rule about **timing and prejudice**, not a general dispensation. Chetan and Deepak have satisfied both halves: they objected before issues, and they have pleaded concrete prejudice rather than the bare fact of the defect.

**Can Anita cure it now?** She can apply for leave belatedly, and a court may in a proper case grant it. But she is applying against a contested objection, with the cause of action wholly elsewhere and the balance of convenience against her, and she has no answer to the question why the suit was not filed at Indore in the first place — where *all three* defendants could have been sued under clause (c) without any leave at all.

**That is the real indictment.** Indore was available, unobjectionable and obvious. Anita chose Lucknow, needed leave to do it, and did not ask. The cost of asking would have been three lines.
::::

::: warning Drafting trap — the leave paragraph, and where it goes

When suing multiple defendants where only one is within the local limits, insert an express paragraph in the body of the plaint:

> *"Only Defendant No. 1 resides within the local limits of the jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court. The Plaintiff therefore seeks the leave of this Hon'ble Court under clause (b) of Section 20 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 to institute the present suit against all the Defendants in this Hon'ble Court."*

Then carry it into the prayer as a separate lettered relief, and press for an order on it at admission — a leave that is pleaded but never granted is worth very little when the objection comes.

Do not confuse this with **leave under Clause 12 of the Letters Patent** on the Original Side of the Bombay, Calcutta and Madras High Courts. That is a distinct regime with distinct conditions, and obtaining one is not obtaining the other.
:::

::::instinct The letter that arrived at home

Anita lives at Chandigarh. She contracts with Bharat of Hyderabad for consultancy services. The contract was negotiated over meetings at Hyderabad, executed at Hyderabad, and performed at Hyderabad; her fee was payable into an account she nominated, and she nominated her Hyderabad account.

Bharat repudiates by letter. Anita receives it at Chandigarh, opens it there, and instructs counsel there. She sues at Chandigarh, pleading four jurisdictional facts:

> the Plaintiff received the Defendant's letter of repudiation within the local limits of this Hon'ble Court; the Plaintiff issued her legal notice dated 14 May from within the said limits; the Plaintiff's bankers are situate within the said limits; and the Plaintiff has suffered loss and damage within the said limits.

**Four pleaded facts, all admittedly true, all local. Is that not "part" of the cause of action?**

:::ruling

**All four are true and none of them is material. Clause (c) asks for constituent facts, not local ones.**

The cause of action is the bundle of **material facts which the plaintiff must plead and prove to obtain the decree** — not the evidence by which she proves them, and not the consequences that follow. *Kusum Ingots* states the filter: only facts which are material, and which the plaintiff must prove to succeed, form part of the cause of action. Apply it to each averment.

*Receipt of the repudiation.* Anita's best point, and it still fails on these facts. Where a contract is repudiated by communication, the breach may occur where the repudiation takes effect — but Anita does not have to prove **where she happened to be standing** when she opened the envelope in order to succeed. She has to prove the contract, its terms, performance by her, and Bharat's refusal. The place of her receipt is incidental. Had the contract required notices to be given at a nominated address within the forum, the answer might differ — but that would be a term of the contract, not an accident of the post.

*The legal notice she sent.* An act of the plaintiff, after the cause of action was complete. It cannot be a constituent of the very cause of action that made it necessary.

*The location of her bankers.* Irrelevant unless payment was contractually to be made there. She nominated a Hyderabad account, so it points the other way.

*The situs of her loss.* The consequence of the breach, not an element of it. If the residence of the plaintiff founded jurisdiction, every plaintiff would sue at home and clause (c) would swallow clauses (a) and (b) whole.

**What Anita has really done.** She has pleaded a **manufactured forum** — the single most common abuse of clause (c) and the one most reliably struck down. The plaint will be returned under Order VII Rule 10 for presentation at Hyderabad.

**What she should do instead, today.** Concede the forum and file at Hyderabad, before the point is decided against her and the time is lost. If she genuinely believes a material fact arose at Chandigarh — that the contract obliged Bharat to render services or make payment there, that an instalment was payable there, that a part of the work was to be delivered there — she must plead **that**, with the clause number and the date, not the four facts she has pleaded.

**And the general drafting rule this illustrates.** Write the jurisdiction paragraph **last**, after the prayer clause is final, and write it by asking of each averment: *would I have to prove this to get my decree?* If the answer is no, it does not belong in the jurisdiction paragraph, and putting it there advertises that the forum is weak.
::::

::::instinct The perfect forum and the rejected plaint

Anita's company sues Bharat's company for ₹4,60,00,000 on a software development agreement. The jurisdiction paragraph is a model of its kind: the agreement was executed at Bengaluru, performance was at Bengaluru, payment was due at Bengaluru, both companies have their principal offices at Bengaluru, and the exclusive jurisdiction clause names Bengaluru. The claim is a commercial dispute of Specified Value. It is filed before the Commercial Court at Bengaluru.

There is no application for interim relief. The plaint pleads no urgency; the dispute has been running through correspondence for fourteen months.

At the same time, Anita's company has a second, unrelated claim against a State department for ₹90,00,000 on works executed. That plaint too is filed at Bengaluru, where everything happened. No notice was issued to the Government before filing.

**Both jurisdiction paragraphs are impeccable. What happens to the two plaints?**

:::ruling

**Both are liable to be rejected, and neither has anything to do with Section 20. This is the trap of getting the forum exactly right and losing anyway.**

**The commercial suit — Section 12A, Commercial Courts Act, 2015.** Where a suit is a commercial dispute of Specified Value and **does not contemplate any urgent interim relief**, pre-institution mediation under Section 12A is a **condition precedent**. A plaint filed without exhausting it is liable to be rejected. Fourteen months of correspondence and no interim application is the clearest possible case that no genuine urgency exists.

Note precisely where the danger lies. The carve-out is not a formality to be recited. A plaint that tacks on an interim application solely to escape Section 12A invites the court to examine whether the urgency is genuine and apparent on the pleadings — and if it is not, the plaint is rejected notwithstanding the application. The remedy is not cleverness; it is to commence mediation, obtain the non-starting report, and plead compliance with the dates.

**The Government suit — Section 80 CPC.** Where the Government or a public officer acting in official capacity is a defendant, **two months'** prior written notice is a condition precedent. The plaint must either plead compliance and annex the notice, or plead and obtain **leave under Section 80(2)** for urgent or immediate relief — and leave under Section 80(2) has to be applied for, and granted, not merely asserted.

**The unifying point.** Sections 15 to 20 tell you which court may **try** the suit. They say nothing about whether the plaint may be **presented** at all. Sitting outside the Code are a growing set of pre-institution gatekeepers — Section 80 notice, Section 12A mediation, statutory notices under special enactments, arbitration clauses that will meet you under Section 8 — and each of them can defeat a plaint whose forum is beyond criticism.

**The practical habit.** Keep two separate checklists and run both before presentation. *Which court?* — Sections 15 to 20, the jurisdiction facts under Order VII Rule 1(f), leave under clause (b), the jurisdiction clause. *May I file at all yet?* — Section 80, Section 12A, any statutory notice, limitation, court fee, and whether an arbitration clause makes the whole exercise futile.

**And on limitation, note the asymmetry that catches people.** Time spent in a Section 12A mediation is excluded from limitation by the statute itself. Time spent waiting out a Section 80 notice is accommodated by Section 15(2) of the Limitation Act. But time lost because a plaint was rejected for failing either is **not** excluded by anything — a rejection under Order VII Rule 11 is not a return under Rule 10, and Section 14 of the Limitation Act does not reach it.

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

**The one case to have ready:** *Patel Roadways Ltd. v. Prasad Trading Co.*, (1991) 4 SCC 270 — 2 Judges.

*Facts:* A corporation with its principal office in one State and a subordinate office in another State, where the cause of action arose. The corporation contended it was suable only at its principal office.

**The ratio to plead:** on the true construction of the Explanation to Section 20, where a corporation has a subordinate office at the place where the cause of action arises, it **cannot** be heard to say that it is suable only at its principal office. The purpose of the Explanation is to protect the plaintiff from being compelled to travel to the corporation's head office; it is not a shield for the corporation.

**Why this is the sentence that wins Section 20 cases.** Most defendants in commercial litigation are companies, and the first line of nearly every jurisdictional written statement is that the company's registered office is elsewhere. *Patel Roadways* answers it in one move — and it answers it on the **text of the section itself**, not by analogy from another jurisdiction, which is more than can be said for most of the authorities in this area.

> "Under the Explanation to Section 20, and on the authority of Patel Roadways, where the cause of action has arisen at the place where the Defendant has a subordinate office, the Defendant cannot insist upon being sued at its registered office; the Explanation protects the plaintiff and does not arm the corporation."

**Know its limit, because the other side will find it.** The Explanation cuts both ways. A branch confers jurisdiction **only** where the cause of action arose at that place. A company with fifty branches is not suable in fifty districts, and a plaint that pleads nothing more than "the Defendant has a branch office within the local limits of this Hon'ble Court" is pleading a fact that founds nothing. *Patel Roadways* is a weapon against the registered-office objection; it is not a licence to sue wherever the defendant has a signboard.

::: info Verify before citing
*Patel Roadways* has been applied and distinguished in a long line of decisions, and its interaction with *Indian Performing Rights Society v. Sanjay Dalia* — which restrains a **plaintiff** with a branch at the situs of the cause of action from suing at its own head office — is worth reading in full before either is relied on. Confirm the citation, the bench strength and the current status in the official reports.
:::

#### Sword and shield

| | Plaintiff (sword) | Defendant (shield) |
|---|---|---|
| **The jurisdiction paragraph** | Plead **facts** under Order VII Rule 1(f): the defendant's address within the limits, or the dated constituent facts of the cause of action that arose there | Attack a conclusory paragraph first. "This Hon'ble Court has jurisdiction" pleads nothing and can never be pinned down at trial |
| **Clause (c) — materiality** | Plead where the contract was negotiated and concluded, where it was to be performed, where goods went, where payment was due, where the breach occurred | Apply the *Kusum Ingots* filter: the plaintiff's own residence, the posting of her notice, the situs of her bank and the situs of her loss are not constituent facts |

#### The limitation clock

The Section 20 limitation risk is not usually the article — it is the **cost of a forum error**, because the two ways a plaint can leave the court have completely different limitation consequences.

| Event | Effect on time |
|---|---|
| Plaint **returned** under **O. VII R. 10** for want of jurisdiction | Suit instituted afresh on **re-presentation**. The earlier period is excluded only by pleading and proving **S. 14, Limitation Act, 1963** — good faith, due diligence, and inability to entertain for defect of jurisdiction |
| Plaint **rejected** under **O. VII R. 11** — no S. 12A mediation, no S. 80 notice, undervaluation, no cause of action | **Nothing is excluded.** S. 14 does not reach a rejection, and the three-year clock has run throughout. A fresh plaint under R. 13 is competent but may well be time-barred |
| **S. 80** notice period | Accommodated by **S. 15(2), Limitation Act** — the two months of statutory notice are excluded |
| **S. 12A** pre-institution mediation | The period of mediation is excluded by the Commercial Courts Act itself |
| Ordinary contractual claims | **Art. 55** — 3 years from breach; **Art. 113** residuary — 3 years from accrual; **Arts. 14, 15, 18, 19** for goods, work and money lent |
| **S. 18** acknowledgment / **S. 19** part payment | A **fresh period** runs, but only if made **before expiry** and in the required writing. Check the date on the balance confirmation before you rely on it |

::: danger Fatal error — return versus rejection
Practitioners treat "the plaint went back" as one event. It is two, and the difference is the suit. A **return** under Order VII Rule 10 preserves the possibility of Section 14 exclusion, and the order is appealable under Order XLIII Rule 1(a). A **rejection** under Order VII Rule 11 — for want of Section 12A mediation, want of Section 80 notice, or want of a cause of action — excludes nothing, and by the time it is pronounced the limitation period has usually gone. Before presentation, run the pre-institution checklist as carefully as the jurisdiction paragraph; a defect that produces rejection is far more dangerous than one that produces return.
:::

#### The authorities

**A.B.C. Laminart Pvt. Ltd. v. A.P. Agencies, Salem**, (1989) 2 SCC 163 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* A contract for supply of metallic yarn contained a clause that "any dispute arising out of this sale shall be subject to Kaira jurisdiction"; suit was filed at Salem.
**Ratio decidendi:** Where more than one court has jurisdiction under the Code, parties may by agreement confine the dispute to one of them, and such an agreement is not hit by Section 28 of the Contract Act. But the ouster must be clear: where the clause does not use words such as "alone", "only", "exclusively", the maxim *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* may be applied, though the intention must be gathered from the whole clause. Crucially, the court also held that a cause of action for breach may arise at the place where the goods were to be delivered and payment made.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Under A.B.C. Laminart, an ouster clause operates only where the chosen court is itself one of the courts having jurisdiction under the Code; and absent words of exclusivity, the ouster of the other competent forum is not to be readily inferred."
:::

**Swastik Gases Pvt. Ltd. v. Indian Oil Corporation Ltd.**, (2013) 9 SCC 32 — 3 Judges.
*Facts:* An agreement provided that "the agreement shall be subject to jurisdiction of the courts at Kolkata"; suit filed at Jaipur.
**Ratio decidendi:** The absence of words like "alone", "only" or "exclusive" is **not decisive**. Where a jurisdiction clause names a particular court and that court has jurisdiction under the Code, the intendment is that the parties intended to exclude all other fora; otherwise the clause would be rendered meaningless. *Expressio unius est exclusio alterius* applies.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Swastik Gases settles that a jurisdiction clause naming one competent court excludes the others even without the word 'exclusively' — the clause must be given meaning, not rendered otiose."
:::

**Indian Performing Rights Society Ltd. v. Sanjay Dalia**, (2015) 10 SCC 161 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* The plaintiff sought to sue at its principal place of business under the special jurisdiction provisions of the Copyright and Trade Marks Acts, though the cause of action arose where it had a branch.
**Ratio decidendi:** The additional forum conferred on a plaintiff by special statutes is not a licence for forum-shopping. Where the plaintiff has a branch office at the place where the cause of action arose, it must sue there and cannot drag the defendant to the place of its head office. The provisions must be read to avoid absurdity and abuse.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Sanjay Dalia forecloses forum-shopping by a plaintiff who has a branch at the situs of the cause of action, whatever the location of its head office."
:::

**Kusum Ingots & Alloys Ltd. v. Union of India**, (2004) 6 SCC 254 — 3 Judges.
**Ratio decidendi (as applied here):** Only those facts which are *material* and which the plaintiff must prove to succeed form part of the cause of action; and even where a part of the cause of action arises within the forum, the court retains a discretion on *forum conveniens* grounds.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A fact which is merely incidental, and which the plaintiff need not prove to obtain the decree, does not form part of the cause of action and cannot found jurisdiction."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
The relationship between A.B.C. Laminart and Swastik Gases on the necessity of exclusionary words has been the subject of continued elaboration, as has the reach of Sanjay Dalia. Confirm the citations, bench strength and current status of each decision in the official reports before reliance.
:::

#### Strategy and drafting

**Drafting traps that cause rejection or return.**

1. **The conclusory jurisdiction paragraph.** "This Hon'ble Court has jurisdiction to try the present suit" satisfies nothing. Order VII Rule 1(f) demands *facts*. Plead the address of the defendant within the limits, or the specific constituent facts of the cause of action that arose within them, with dates.
2. **Omitting leave under clause (b).** Discussed above. Three lines in the plaint, three years of litigation saved.
3. **Relying on a subordinate office alone.** The Explanation requires the cause of action to have arisen at that office. A branch that had nothing to do with the transaction confers nothing.
4. **Pleading a jurisdiction clause naming a court that has no jurisdiction under the Code.** Void and unhelpful; check first that the named court independently qualifies under Section 20.
5. **Ignoring Section 80 CPC.** Where the Government or a public officer acting in official capacity is a defendant, the two-month notice is a condition precedent; the plaint must either plead compliance and annex the notice, or plead and obtain leave under Section 80(2).
6. **Ignoring Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act.** See the danger box above.
7. **Pleading residence as at some past date.** The test is residence "at the time of the commencement of the suit". Plead present-tense facts.

**Strategic timing.**

| Move | When it must be made | Provision |
|---|---|---|
| Plaintiff's leave application under clause (b) | At presentation of the plaint | S. 20(b) proviso |
| Defendant's territorial objection | In the written statement, and in any event **at the earliest possible opportunity and before settlement of issues** | S. 21(1) |
| Application to try jurisdiction as a preliminary issue | Immediately after the written statement, before evidence | O. XIV R. 2(2)(a) |
| Application for return of plaint | With, or immediately after, the written statement | O. VII R. 10 |
| Application under S. 8 of the Arbitration Act | Not later than the date of submitting the first statement on the substance of the dispute | Arbitration Act, S. 8 |
| Transfer application by defendant where two or more courts are competent | After notice to the defendant and **before settlement of issues** | S. 22 |

::: tip The single most useful habit in Section 20 practice
Draft the jurisdiction paragraph **last**, after the prayer clause is final. The forum depends on the reliefs claimed, and the reliefs are often revised late. A jurisdiction paragraph written before the prayer was settled is a jurisdiction paragraph that no longer matches the suit.
:::

**Model jurisdiction paragraph — cause of action limb.**

> That the Agreement dated [date] was negotiated and executed at [place], which is within the local limits of the ordinary original civil jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court; the goods thereunder were delivered by the Plaintiff to the Defendant at [place], within the said limits; the consideration was, in terms of clause [x] of the said Agreement, payable by the Defendant to the Plaintiff at [place], within the said limits; and the Defendant's failure and refusal to make payment, being the breach complained of, occurred within the said limits. The cause of action has therefore arisen, wholly and in any event in part, within the local limits of the jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court, and this Hon'ble Court has jurisdiction to entertain, try and dispose of the present suit under Section 20(c) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908.

**Model prayer clause — leave under Section 20(b).**

> **(a)** be pleased to grant leave to the Plaintiff under clause (b) of Section 20 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 to institute the present suit against all the Defendants in this Hon'ble Court, Defendant No. 1 alone residing and carrying on business within the local limits of the jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court;

## Sections 21 & 21A — Objections to jurisdiction

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute You cannot fight a case for four years, lose, and then say the court was the wrong one.

Sections 15 to 20 tell you where to file. Section 21 tells you what happens when you got it wrong — and the answer, for most kinds of error, is **nothing**, provided nobody complained in time.

Section 21 forbids an **appellate or revisional** court from allowing an objection about the **place of suing** (sub-s. 1), the **pecuniary limits** of the court (sub-s. 2), or the **local limits of the executing court** (sub-s. 3) unless the objector proves three things, **cumulatively**:

1. he took the objection **in the court of first instance**;
2. **at the earliest possible opportunity**, and where issues were settled, **at or before settlement**;
3. **and** there has been a **consequent failure of justice**.

Miss any one and the objection is dead. Most die on the third.

**Section 21A** shuts the last door: no separate suit lies to set aside a decree on a place-of-suing ground.

And the whole scheme has a hole in it, deliberately: **subject-matter** jurisdiction is not in the list. A court that has no power over the *class* of dispute passes a decree that is a **nullity** — challengeable anywhere, at any time, by anyone, forever.
:::

::: story Two kinds of "no jurisdiction", and why lawyers confuse them

Ask a court to hold a decree void and you will be told, correctly, that a decree passed without jurisdiction is a nullity and its invalidity may be set up whenever and wherever it is sought to be enforced — in appeal, in execution, in a collateral proceeding, at any distance of time. That proposition comes from *Kiran Singh v. Chaman Paswan*, a Constitution Bench, and it is quoted in every second written submission in India.

The trouble is that the very same judgment, a few lines later, takes most of it back.

Because the legislature has made a **deliberate departure** for certain classes of defect. Not all jurisdictional errors are the same. Sort them into three tiers and the whole chapter becomes legible.

**Tier one — subject matter.** The court has no power over this *kind* of dispute at all. A civil court decreeing what only a Rent Controller may decide; a civil judge deciding what is exclusively for a Debts Recovery Tribunal. This is want of power at the root. It is **never** curable, consent cannot supply it, waiver cannot supply it, and the decree is a nullity that can be ignored.

**Tier two — pecuniary, downward.** A suit worth ₹90 lakh decreed by a court whose ceiling is ₹20 lakh. The court had power over the class of dispute but not over this one. Substantially a nullity too — though Section 21(2) and Section 11 of the Suits Valuation Act intervene where the real complaint is over- or under-**valuation**, which is a different thing from a court exceeding its ceiling.

**Tier three — territorial, and pecuniary upward.** The wrong district. A court of higher grade than necessary. Here the decree is **perfectly valid and binding** unless the three conditions of Section 21 are made out. These are not defects of power at all. They are **irregularities**, and the Code treats them as conditions of *regularity*, not of *validity*.

Why the difference? Because tier three exists for the **convenience of the parties**, not to limit the court's authority. Territorial rules say which of several competent courts should hear a case; they do not say whether the case can be heard. A benefit conferred on a party can be waived by that party — and the Code decides that a party who says nothing has waived it.

Behind that lies a policy about **ambush**. Without Section 21, a defendant could file a written statement, settle issues, lead evidence for three years, wait for judgment, and — if he lost — produce a forum objection that wipes out the entire trial. He would be litigating with a free option: heads he wins on the merits, tails he wins on the technicality. Section 21 removes the option by making him choose at the start.

The third condition is where the section does its real work, and it is the one practitioners forget. It is a **causation** requirement. The objector must show not that the forum was wrong, but that being in the wrong forum **caused** a miscarriage of justice — witnesses he could not compel, records he could not reach, a different substantive law wrongly applied. "I had to travel" is inconvenience, and inconvenience had its own remedy: a transfer application under Sections 22 to 25, which he did not make either.

Then in 1976 Parliament added **Section 21A** to close the last escape. Read the three provisions together and a place-of-suing objection not taken before the settlement of issues is, for all practical purposes, dead: Section 21 stops it in appeal, Section 21A stops a fresh suit, and Section 11 stops any re-run of the merits.

### The Act, decompiled

Three sub-sections built on the same skeleton, then a separate section that bars a suit. Read for the **double negative** — *no objection … shall be allowed* — which puts the entire burden on the objector, and for the two **ands**, which make the conditions cumulative. Then notice what is missing from the list of three.

> **21. Objections to jurisdiction.** — **(1)** [[!No objection]] as to the [[=place of suing]] [[!shall be allowed]] by any Appellate or Revisional Court unless such objection was taken in the Court of first instance [[=at the earliest possible opportunity]] and in all cases where issues are settled [[=at or before such settlement]], and unless there has been a [[=consequent failure of justice]].
> **(2)** [[!No objection]] as to the competence of a Court with reference to the [[=pecuniary limits]] of its jurisdiction [[!shall be allowed]] by any Appellate or Revisional Court unless such objection was taken in the Court of first instance [[=at the earliest possible opportunity]], and in all cases where issues are settled, [[=at or before such settlement]], and unless there has been a [[=consequent failure of justice]].
> **(3)** [[!No objection]] as to the competence of the executing Court with reference to the [[=local limits]] of its jurisdiction [[!shall be allowed]] by any Appellate or Revisional Court unless such objection was taken in the executing Court [[=at the earliest possible opportunity]], and unless there has been a [[=consequent failure of justice]].

> **21A. Bar on suit to set aside decree on objection as to place of suing.** — [[!No suit shall lie]] challenging the validity of a decree passed in a former suit between the same parties, or between the parties under whom they or any of them claim, [[=litigating under the same title]], on any ground based on an objection as to the [[=place of suing]].
>
> ***Explanation.*** — The expression "former suit" means a suit which has been decided prior to the decision in the suit in which the validity of the decree is questioned, whether or not the previously decided suit was instituted prior to the suit in which the validity of such decree is questioned.

**Statutory mechanics of Section 21.**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "**No** objection … **shall be allowed**" | **Prohibitory**, double negative | The appellate court is *forbidden* to entertain the objection unless the conditions are met. The burden lies entirely on the objector. |
| "by any **Appellate or Revisional** Court" | Forum limiter | Section 21 does not stop the trial court from entertaining a prompt objection. It bars the *belated* objection at the appellate stage. |
| "as to the **place of suing**" (sub-s. 1) | Subject-matter of the bar | Territorial jurisdiction only — Sections 16 to 20. |
| "**pecuniary** limits" (sub-s. 2) | Subject-matter of the bar | Pecuniary competence — Section 15. |
| "**local limits** of the **executing** Court" (sub-s. 3) | Subject-matter of the bar | Territorial competence at the execution stage — Sections 38, 39. |
| "at the **earliest possible opportunity**" | **Condition precedent (1)** | A pure question of promptness, judged on the record. |
| "and **in all cases where issues are settled, at or before such settlement**" | **Condition precedent (2) — an absolute ceiling** | Even an otherwise "prompt" objection is too late if issues have been settled. Note sub-section (3) has no issue-settlement clause — execution proceedings have no issues. |
| "**and** unless there has been a **consequent failure of justice**" | **Condition precedent (3)** | Conjunctive. Promptness alone is not enough. The objector must show prejudice *caused by* the wrong forum. |

**Three cumulative conditions.** The word "and" appears twice. The objector must establish, cumulatively: **(i)** the objection was taken in the court of first instance; **(ii)** at the earliest possible opportunity and, where issues were settled, at or before settlement; **and (iii)** a consequent failure of justice. Failure on any one limb defeats the objection.

**What Section 21 conspicuously does *not* cover.** Jurisdiction over the **subject matter**. Section 21 lists three things — place of suing, pecuniary limits, and the executing court's local limits. Subject-matter competence is absent by deliberate omission. A decree passed by a court having no jurisdiction over the subject matter is a **nullity**, may be questioned at any stage, in any proceeding, including in execution and in collateral proceedings, and is not saved by consent, waiver or acquiescence.

**Statutory mechanics of Section 21A.** Inserted by the Amendment Act of 1976. It is a **substantive bar on a separate suit**, complementing the procedural bar in Section 21. The three conditions are: (i) a decree in a *former suit*; (ii) between the same parties or their privies; (iii) litigating under the same title. The Explanation makes the section date-agnostic — what matters is which suit was *decided* first, not which was *instituted* first. This mirrors Explanation I to Section 11.

::: proviso The three "ands" — why almost every objection dies on the third

Section 21(1) is one sentence, and its whole force lies in two little words:

> *unless such objection was taken in the Court of first instance at the earliest possible opportunity **and** in all cases where issues are settled at or before such settlement, **and** unless there has been a consequent failure of justice*

Conjunctive, not disjunctive. The objector must establish **all three**, and failure on any one is the end of it:

1. **Taken in the court of first instance.** Not first raised in appeal, not first raised in revision.
2. **At the earliest possible opportunity — and in any event at or before the settlement of issues.** These are two separate tests, not one. A defendant may honestly have raised it as soon as he could and still be too late, because issues had already been settled. The settlement of issues is an **absolute ceiling**, and *Pathumma* treats the conditions as cumulative.
3. **A consequent failure of justice.** A **causation** test. Not that the forum was wrong — that is the defect, not the prejudice. The objector must show what the wrong forum *cost* him.

Note the asymmetry in sub-section (3). Objections about the **executing court's local limits** have no settlement-of-issues ceiling, because execution proceedings have no issues; and the objection must be taken **in the executing court**, not the trial court.

**The practical consequence for a memorandum of appeal.** A ground reading "the learned trial court had no territorial jurisdiction" pleads one third of a ground. The appellate court is *statutorily forbidden* to allow it. Plead promptness and prejudice as separate, particularised grounds, or do not plead the point at all.
:::

::: proviso What is missing from the list, and why it changes everything

Section 21 names exactly three things: the **place of suing**, the **pecuniary limits** of the court, and the **local limits of the executing court**.

**Jurisdiction over the subject matter is not there.** The omission is deliberate, and it is the most important negative space in the Code.

A court that has no power over the *class* of dispute — a civil court deciding what is exclusively for a Rent Controller, a Debts Recovery Tribunal, a Consumer Commission, a statutory authority under a special Act — is not merely sitting in the wrong place. It is exercising a power it does not have. Its decree is a **nullity**. It may be ignored; its invalidity may be set up whenever and wherever it is sought to be enforced or relied on, including in execution and in collateral proceedings; consent cannot supply the power and waiver cannot cure its absence. *Harshad Chiman Lal Modi* draws the line in terms: place and money are forgiven, subject matter never is.

**Two consequences follow, and they point in opposite directions.**

*For a defendant who has slept on a territorial point*, the only escape is to recharacterise the defect as one of subject matter — to show that Section 9 excludes the civil court's cognisance altogether, not merely that Sections 16 to 20 pointed elsewhere. That is a completely different argument, resting on a different provision, and it is available at any stage.

*For a plaintiff facing that manoeuvre*, the answer is that the two are not interchangeable. A dispute that a civil court may try, filed in the wrong district, is a tier-three irregularity. Dressing it up as an ouster of civil jurisdiction requires a statute that excludes it, expressly or by necessary implication — and the presumption is against ouster.
:::

### In plain English

**Sub-module A — The policy.** A litigant should not be permitted to take his chance on the merits, lose, and then spring a technical forum objection to wipe out years of trial. Section 21 converts territorial and pecuniary jurisdiction from *conditions of validity* into *conditions of regularity* — irregularities which must be objected to promptly or are treated as waived.

**Sub-module B — The three-tier hierarchy of jurisdictional defects.** This is the organising idea of the whole chapter:

| Tier | Type of defect | Effect of the decree | Curable? | When objectionable |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Subject-matter** — the court has no power over this class of dispute at all | **Nullity**; void; can be ignored | **Never** | At any stage, in any proceeding, including execution and collateral attack |
| **2** | **Pecuniary** — suit filed in a court of *lower* grade than competent | **Nullity** as to that court's power, but Section 21(2) and Section 11 of the Suits Valuation Act intervene on over/under-valuation | Substantially, yes | Must satisfy S.21(2) if raised in appeal |
| **3** | **Territorial (place of suing)** and **pecuniary (higher grade)** | **Valid and binding** unless S.21 conditions satisfied | **Yes** | Court of first instance, at the earliest opportunity, before settlement of issues |

**Sub-module C — "At the earliest possible opportunity".** Not a fixed date; a standard measured against the record. In practice:

- If the defect is apparent on the face of the plaint, the earliest opportunity is the **first appearance** or the **written statement**.
- If the defect emerges only from facts pleaded in the written statement or discovered later, the earliest opportunity is **immediately upon discovery**, and the party must move at once.
- A defendant who files a written statement on the merits without a preliminary objection, and raises the point only at final arguments, has failed this limb absolutely.

**Sub-module D — The absolute ceiling: "at or before such settlement".** This is the harder of the two temporal limbs and it is *not* co-extensive with "earliest possible opportunity". Even if a defendant can honestly say he raised it as soon as he could, if issues have already been settled the door is shut. The rationale: once issues are framed, the parties and the court have committed to a trial plan; forum objections thereafter waste judicial time.

**Sub-module E — "Consequent failure of justice" — the limb that defeats most objections.** This is a *causation* requirement, and it is the one most often overlooked. The objector must show not merely that the forum was wrong, but that being in the wrong forum **caused** a miscarriage of justice. Examples of what is and is not enough:

| Pleaded prejudice | Sufficient? |
|---|---|
| "The suit ought to have been filed at Nagpur, not Nashik" | **No** — that is the defect, not the prejudice |
| "I was put to expense in travelling to a distant forum" | **Ordinarily no** — inconvenience is not a failure of justice; the remedy was a transfer application |
| "My material witnesses could not be produced because they were beyond the process of that court and I was thereby prevented from proving my defence" | **Yes, if proved** |
| "The wrong forum applied a different State amendment / rent legislation which does not apply to the property, to my detriment" | **Yes** — a real, substantive detriment |
| "The suit was tried by a court of higher grade, and I therefore lost my first appeal to the District Judge and had to appeal to the High Court at far greater cost" | **Arguable and frequently pressed** — plead it specifically |

**Sub-module F — Section 21(3) and execution.** Sub-section (3) protects execution proceedings taken in a court whose *local limits* did not, strictly, cover the matter. Note two differences from sub-sections (1) and (2): there is no "settlement of issues" ceiling (execution has no issues), and the objection must be taken *in the executing court*, not the trial court. But **beware**: Section 21(3) covers only the *local limits* of the executing court. It does not save an execution taken by a court to which the decree was never transferred under Section 39, nor an execution of a decree that is itself a nullity — those objections survive under Section 47.

**Sub-module G — Section 21A and the closing of the last door.** Before 1976, a defeated party might file a fresh suit to have the earlier decree declared a nullity for want of territorial jurisdiction. Section 21A closes that route. Read together:

- **Section 21(1)** — you cannot raise it in appeal or revision (unless the three conditions are met).
- **Section 21A** — you cannot raise it in a fresh suit at all.
- **Section 11** — you cannot re-agitate the merits.

The combined effect is that a place-of-suing objection not taken before settlement of issues is, for all practical purposes, dead.

**Sub-module H — What Section 21A does *not* bar.** It bars a suit founded on "an objection as to the **place of suing**". It does not bar a suit to set aside a decree on the ground of **fraud** (Section 44 of the Evidence Act; the principle in *S.P. Chengalvaraya Naidu*), nor a challenge founded on want of **subject-matter** jurisdiction, nor a challenge that the decree was passed against a dead person or without service. Section 21A is narrow, and the pleading in a set-aside suit must be framed to fall outside it.

### Test your instinct

::::instinct The objection that waited three years

Anita sues Bharat at Ludhiana on a contract. The contract was negotiated, signed and performed entirely at Amritsar. Bharat lives at Amritsar. Nothing whatever happened at Ludhiana except the filing.

Bharat files a written statement. It denies the contract, pleads discharge by payment, and takes limitation as a preliminary objection. It says nothing about jurisdiction. Issues are settled. Evidence is led over three years — six witnesses for Anita, four for Bharat, two rounds of cross-examination.

At final arguments, Bharat's counsel rises and submits that the court at Ludhiana never had territorial jurisdiction and the whole trial is a nullity.

**He is right on the facts. Does he win?**

:::ruling

**No — and he loses without the court ever needing to decide whether Ludhiana was the wrong forum.**

That is the point of Section 21 that practitioners keep missing. The section does not ask the appellate court to weigh the strength of the jurisdictional point. It forbids the court from entertaining it at all unless three separate conditions are made out, and Bharat fails on two of them before the merits of his objection are reached.

**Limb one — earliest possible opportunity.** The defect was apparent on the face of the plaint. The plaint itself must state the facts founding jurisdiction under Order VII Rule 1(f), and if it did so honestly it disclosed an Amritsar contract; if it did not, Bharat's own written statement pleaded his Amritsar residence. Either way he knew on the day he was served. The earliest opportunity was his first appearance, and at the very latest the written statement. He let both pass.

**Limb two — the settlement of issues.** This is the one that is fatal, because it is not a question of diligence at all. Issues were framed. Section 21(1) shuts the door at that moment "in all cases where issues are settled". Even a defendant who could show he was genuinely unable to raise it earlier is caught. Bharat cannot show even that.

**Limb three — failure of justice.** He has pleaded none, and on these facts he has none. He contested the merits fully, led four witnesses, and cross-examined at length. He has not been prevented from doing anything.

**The one escape, and what it costs him.** Bharat's only route out is to stop calling this a place-of-suing objection and recharacterise it as an objection to **subject-matter** competence — that Section 9 excludes the civil court from this class of dispute altogether, because it belongs exclusively to a Rent Controller, a Debts Recovery Tribunal, a Consumer Commission or a statutory authority. That objection is outside Section 21, available at any stage, and cannot be waived.

But it is a completely different argument. It rests on a different provision, requires a statute that ousts the civil court expressly or by necessary implication, and runs against a settled presumption in favour of civil jurisdiction. A dispute the civil court could plainly have tried, filed in the wrong district, will not become an ouster of civil jurisdiction because it is convenient to Bharat that it should.

**And Anita should say all of this in one breath, in this order:** the objection was not taken at the earliest opportunity; it was not taken before issues were settled, which is an absolute bar; no failure of justice is pleaded, let alone proved; and Section 21 is a command to the court, not a discretion to be exercised in the defendant's favour.
::::

::::instinct The objection taken on day one that still lost

Bharat does everything right. His written statement opens with preliminary objection No. 1: the court has no territorial jurisdiction. He simultaneously applies under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a) for the jurisdictional issue to be tried as a preliminary issue.

The trial court declines. It frames the issue but says it will be decided along with the suit. The suit proceeds. Bharat loses on the merits.

In appeal he presses the jurisdictional ground. He has cleared limb one and limb two cleanly — the objection was taken at the first opportunity and long before issues were settled.

**Two limbs down, one to go. What does he have to prove, and what usually goes wrong?**

:::ruling

**He must prove causation, and the ground as usually drafted proves nothing.**

The third limb is not "was the forum wrong". That is the *defect*. The third limb asks what the wrong forum **cost** him. It is a causation test, and Section 21 places the entire burden on him.

**What actually satisfies it.** Bharat must plead and prove something like this: two material witnesses resided at the correct forum and beyond the trial court's process under Order XVI Rule 19; he applied for summons and they could not be served or compelled; he applied for a commission under Order XXVI Rule 4 and it was refused; and his defence of payment therefore failed for want of precisely the evidence the wrong forum denied him. That is a chain — forum, to inability, to evidentiary gap, to adverse finding. Each link has to be on the record.

Other things that can do it: records or registers that lay beyond the court's reach; a different State amendment or rent statute wrongly applied to the property because of where the case was tried, to his detriment; or, in a pecuniary case, the loss of a first appeal to the District Judge because the suit was tried by a court of higher grade.

**How Anita defeats it.** She attacks the chain, not the conclusion. She shows Bharat never applied for summons for those witnesses; never applied for a commission; never filed an affidavit saying they were unwilling; and never sought an adjournment on that ground. Once the causal link is broken the third limb collapses, and with it the whole objection — however impeccable the first two limbs were.

**The asymmetry to notice.** Bharat did everything a careful defendant is supposed to do and still lost the point. That is not an accident of drafting. Section 21 is designed so that a forum objection succeeds only where the forum actually mattered. If the trial would have gone the same way in the right district, the section treats the error as what it is: an irregularity, not a defect of power.

::::instinct The suit that was worth three times what it said

Anita sues for possession of land and values the suit at ₹2,950 for jurisdiction and court fee. On that valuation the suit goes to the Subordinate Judge, and the first appeal lies to the District Court.

She loses before the Subordinate Judge. She appeals to the District Court and loses again.

In second appeal it emerges that the property was in truth worth about ₹9,980. On the correct valuation the suit was still within the Subordinate Judge's competence — but the **first appeal** would have lain to the High Court, not to the District Judge. Anita therefore says she has lost a first appeal before the High Court, and that the District Court's decree is a nullity.

**Is it a nullity? And does the lost forum of first appeal amount to a failure of justice?**

:::ruling

**It is not a nullity, and the lost forum by itself is not enough. This is the fact pattern of *Kiran Singh v. Chaman Paswan* and it is the best illustration in the books of how the prejudice limb bites.**

**First, separate two things that sound identical.** A court *exceeding its pecuniary ceiling* — a ₹90 lakh suit decreed by a court whose limit is ₹20 lakh — is a defect of power, and substantially a nullity. That is not this case. This is a dispute about **valuation**, where the court had power over suits of this class and this size and the parties disagree about the figure. Over- and under-valuation is governed by Section 21(2) of the Code and by **Section 11 of the Suits Valuation Act, 1887**, and both are built on the same architecture: the objection is not entertained in appeal unless it was taken in the court of first instance at the earliest opportunity, **and** the over- or under-valuation has prejudicially affected the disposal of the case on its merits.

**Second, the words "on its merits" are doing the work.** The prejudice must go to the *decision*, not to the *route*. Anita's complaint is that she got a District Judge instead of a High Court Judge on first appeal. But she got a full first appeal on facts and law, with the same powers of reappraisal, before a court competent to hear appeals. That she would have preferred a different appellate forum is not a demonstration that the disposal on the merits was affected.

**Third, when the same complaint does succeed.** It is not a hopeless ground — it is a ground that has to be built. If Anita can show that the appellate court applied a materially different standard, refused to receive evidence a High Court would have received, or that some substantive rule turned on the identity of the forum, she is in the territory the section contemplates. What she cannot do is treat the change of forum as self-evidently prejudicial and leave it there.

**Fourth, the trap on the other side.** Deliberate undervaluation to reach a convenient forum is a separate and worse problem. It exposes the plaint to rejection under **Order VII Rule 11(b)** if the relief is undervalued and not corrected within the time allowed, and it is not cured by Section 21(2), which protects a party against the *consequences* of a valuation error, not a party who engineered one.
::::

::::instinct Going behind the decree

Anita holds a money decree from the court at Court A. She takes out execution. Bharat, the judgment-debtor, files objections under Section 47 saying Court A never had territorial jurisdiction, that its decree is therefore a nullity, and that a nullity may be challenged at any time in any proceeding — citing *Kiran Singh* for the proposition.

He is quoting the case correctly. The sentence exists.

**Does the executing court entertain the objection?**

:::ruling

**No. The sentence is real; the use he is putting it to is not.**

*Kiran Singh* does say that a decree passed by a court without jurisdiction is a nullity whose invalidity may be set up whenever and wherever it is sought to be enforced, including in execution and in collateral proceedings. It also says, in the same judgment, that the legislature has made a deliberate departure from that rule for certain classes of defect. Bharat is quoting the first half and hoping nobody reads the second.

**What an executing court may and may not notice.** An executing court cannot go behind the decree. The narrow exception is a nullity apparent on the face of the record — want of **subject-matter** competence, or want of **pecuniary** competence in the sense of a court exceeding its ceiling. Those are defects of power, and a court asked to execute a decree that was never a decree may say so.

**A territorial defect is not in that class.** It is the paradigm tier-three irregularity, and it is precisely what Sections 21(1) and 21A were enacted to foreclose. If Bharat could raise it in execution he would have the whole benefit that Section 21 exists to deny him — a free option, exercisable after judgment, on a point he never took at trial. *Harshad Chiman Lal Modi* draws the line in terms: place and money are forgiven, subject matter never is.

**What Bharat could legitimately raise under Section 47.** Not the place of suing, but: that Court A lacked competence over the subject matter; that the decree was obtained by fraud; that it was passed against a person already dead; that it was passed without service, for which Order IX Rule 13 is the proper vehicle; or that it has been adjusted or satisfied.

**And one objection that is genuinely open to him and often missed.** If his complaint is about the **local limits of the executing court itself** — that this court cannot execute here — that is Section 21(3), a different sub-section with a different shape. It has no settlement-of-issues ceiling, because execution proceedings have no issues; the objection must be taken in the executing court, not the trial court; and it still requires promptness and a consequent failure of justice.

But note the limit of even that. Section 21(3) forgives a defect in the executing court's **local limits**. It does not forgive executing a decree that was never transferred to that court under **Section 39** at all. That is not an irregularity of place; it is an absence of authority to execute, and it survives under Section 47.

::::instinct The second suit

Bharat has lost a partition suit at Court A. The property, he says, lay outside Court A's local limits, so Section 16 — which is mandatory — was breached and the decree is void.

He does not appeal. Instead he files a fresh suit at Court B against Anita for a declaration that the decree of Court A is a nullity, with a consequential injunction restraining her from acting on it.

**What happens to that plaint?**

:::ruling

**It is rejected at the threshold, and the years spent on it are gone.**

**Section 21A is a complete answer on its own terms.** It bars a suit challenging the validity of a decree passed in a former suit between the same parties or their representatives litigating under the same title, on any ground based on an objection as to the place of suing. Every element is present: same parties, same title, a former suit already decided, and a ground that is nothing but a place-of-suing objection dressed as a declaration.

Note the Explanation. It makes the section **date-agnostic** — what matters is which suit was *decided* first, not which was filed first. A defendant cannot escape by filing his declaratory suit early and letting it lie.

**The procedural consequence is rejection, not dismissal after trial.** A suit hit by Section 21A is barred by law on the face of the plaint, and the plaint is liable to be rejected under **Order VII Rule 11(d)**. Anita should apply at once, and should plead **Section 11** in the alternative, since the partition decree will usually bar the merits over again.

**Why this is worse than losing.** Rejection under Order VII Rule 11 comes with costs, and it comes *late*. While Bharat's declaratory suit was pending, limitation was running on every legitimate remedy he still had — the appeal he did not file, an application under Order IX Rule 13 if there was one to be made, a suit founded on fraud under Article 59 of the Limitation Act, which runs three years from discovery. Section 14 of the Limitation Act will not readily rescue him, because it requires prosecution of the earlier proceeding *in good faith in a court unable to entertain it for defect of jurisdiction or other cause of a like nature* — and a suit expressly barred by Section 21A is a hard case to describe that way.

**What Section 21A does not touch.** It is a narrow section, and the pleading in any set-aside suit must be framed to fall outside it. Untouched grounds: **fraud** — Section 44 of the Evidence Act, particulars mandatory under Order VI Rule 4, three years from discovery under Article 59; want of **subject-matter** jurisdiction; a decree passed against a dead person; and a decree passed without service. If the ground is genuinely one of these, plead it as such and do not mention the place of suing at all — a stray averment that the property lay elsewhere invites the Order VII Rule 11(d) application that would otherwise not have been available.
::::

::: warning Drafting trap — pleading the defect without pleading the prejudice
The overwhelming majority of Section 21 objections die on the third limb, not the first two. A memorandum of appeal that pleads "the learned trial court had no territorial jurisdiction" and stops there has pleaded one third of a ground, and the appellate court is *statutorily forbidden* to allow it.

Plead three separate, numbered, particularised grounds: **when** the objection was taken and where it appears on the record; **that it preceded the settlement of issues**, with the date of settlement; and **what the wrong forum cost the appellant**, witness by witness and document by document. Remember Order XLI Rule 1(2) and Rule 2 — a ground not set out in the memorandum cannot be urged except with leave.
:::

::: danger Fatal error — the fresh suit that cannot survive Order VII Rule 11(d)
Before drafting any suit to set aside a decree, ask one question: is the ground "based on an objection as to the place of suing"? If it is, the suit is not available. It will not merely fail — it will be rejected at the threshold with costs, while the limitation clock on every legitimate alternative remedy runs.
:::
### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

**Kiran Singh v. Chaman Paswan**, AIR 1954 SC 340 — **Constitution Bench (5 Judges)**.

*Facts in one line:* a suit was under-valued, with the consequence that the first appeal went to the District Court instead of the High Court, and the objection was pressed only at the second appellate stage.

**Ratio decidendi.** A decree passed by a court without jurisdiction is a nullity, and its invalidity may be set up whenever and wherever it is sought to be enforced or relied upon, including at the stage of execution and in collateral proceedings. **But** the legislature has made a deliberate departure from that rule for a defined class of defect: by Section 21 of the Code and Section 11 of the Suits Valuation Act, 1887, objections founded on over- or under-valuation — and, by the same logic, on the place of suing — are not to be entertained by an appellate court unless there has been a **consequent failure of justice**, that is, prejudice to the disposal of the case **on its merits**.

**Why this is the ratio to lead with.** It is the only authority that gives you both halves of the doctrine from a bench of five. Whichever side you are on, your opponent will quote one half of it at you, and the answer is always the other half.

**The sentence to plead.**

> "*Kiran Singh* is the source of the rule that a decree without jurisdiction is a nullity challengeable anywhere and at any time — and it is equally the source of the exception, that valuation and place-of-suing defects stand outside that rule and require proof of a consequent failure of justice on the merits. My learned friend has quoted the rule and omitted the exception, and it is the exception that governs this case."

**Its limits, which you should know before your opponent tells you.** *Kiran Singh* does not make every jurisdictional defect curable. It leaves subject-matter incompetence exactly where it was — a nullity, unwaivable, unconfirmable by consent, challengeable in execution and collaterally. And the prejudice it requires is prejudice **on the merits**; a party who shows only that he would have preferred a different appellate forum has not shown it.

::: info Verify before citing
Confirm the citation and bench strength in the official reports, and check the subsequent line of authority — *Hira Lal Patni*, *Pathumma*, *Bahrein Petroleum* and *Harshad Chiman Lal Modi* each refine the application of *Kiran Singh* to a particular class of defect, and the proposition you need is usually stated more narrowly, and therefore more usefully, in one of them.
:::

#### Sword and shield

| | **Plaintiff (sword)** | **Defendant (shield)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Before filing** | Plead the jurisdictional facts under Order VII Rule 1(f) as **facts**, not conclusions; if two limbs are available, plead both separately | Read the plaint for the jurisdictional averment on the day of service — not the day the written statement is due |
| **First pleading** | — | Preliminary objection **No. 1**, before the para-wise reply, with the prejudice pleaded in the same breath |
| **Interlocutory** | Push for early settlement of issues; every day tightens the Section 21 net | Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a) for a preliminary issue **plus** Order VII Rule 10 for return of the plaint, filed together |
| **If the objection is deferred** | Let it be deferred; the guillotine is falling | Get the objection to the deferral **into the order sheet** — that note is your proof of promptness three years later |
| **In appeal** | Answer all three limbs separately, with dates: defect apparent from day one; issues settled on [date] without objection; no prejudice pleaded or proved | Three numbered grounds — the defect, the promptness, the prejudice. Order XLI Rule 27 if the prejudice needs material not on record |
| **In execution** | An executing court cannot go behind the decree; a territorial defect is not a nullity | Section 47 is open only for subject matter, pecuniary excess, fraud, a dead defendant, or want of service |
| **Against a second suit** | Order VII Rule 11(d) at once, pleading Section 21A **and** Section 11 in the alternative | Frame the ground so it is not "based on an objection as to the place of suing" — or do not file |

#### The limitation clock

Section 21 runs on two clocks that are easy to confuse, and neither is measured in days.

- **The promptness clock** starts when the defect became known or knowable — service, if the plaint disclosed it; discovery, if it did not. It is a standard, not a period.
- **The guillotine** is the **date of settlement of issues** under Order XIV Rule 1. It is a fixed date on the record, it is absolute, and it does not care how diligent the objector was. Order XIV Rule 5 lets issues be amended at any time before the decree — amending them does **not** reopen the Section 21 window.

Three consequences that cost cases:

1. **Diarise the settlement date in every defended suit.** It is the single most consequential procedural date in this chapter.
2. **When the objection succeeds, the ordinary relief is return of the plaint under Order VII Rule 10, not dismissal** — but by then years have gone. On re-presentation, **Section 14 of the Limitation Act** must be *pleaded and proved* to exclude the time spent, and it requires prosecution in good faith in a court unable to entertain the suit for defect of jurisdiction or other cause of a like nature. It is not automatic.
3. **A declaratory suit is not a way of buying time.** While a suit barred by Section 21A is pending, limitation runs on the appeal not filed, on Order IX Rule 13 (30 days, Article 123), and on a fraud action (3 years from discovery, Article 59).

#### The authorities

**Hira Lal Patni v. Sri Kali Nath**, AIR 1962 SC 199 — 3 Judges.
*Facts:* A party who had submitted to the jurisdiction of the Bombay High Court and taken the benefit of proceedings there later challenged that court's jurisdiction on the ground that leave under Clause 12 of the Letters Patent had not been obtained.
**Ratio decidendi:** The objection as to *local* jurisdiction of a court stands on a different footing from an objection to competence with reference to the subject matter. Competence over the subject matter goes to the root and cannot be waived; but an objection as to territorial jurisdiction, or the absence of leave to sue, **can be waived**, and where a party has consented to the jurisdiction and taken the chance of a decision in his favour, he cannot afterwards turn round and challenge it.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"On Hira Lal Patni, a party who submits to the jurisdiction and takes his chance on the merits waives any objection as to place of suing, and is precluded from resurrecting it once the result is known."
:::

**Harshad Chiman Lal Modi v. DLF Universal Ltd.**, (2005) 5 SCC 791 — 2 Judges.
**Ratio decidendi (on this point):** The court expressly restated the dividing line: objections as to territorial and pecuniary jurisdiction fall within Section 21 and are curable by waiver or acquiescence; an objection as to jurisdiction over the subject matter is a different matter altogether, cannot be waived, and consent cannot confer such jurisdiction.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The line drawn in Harshad Chiman Lal Modi is the line drawn by Section 21 itself: place and money are forgiven; subject matter never is."
:::

**Pathumma v. Kuntalan Kutty**, (1981) 3 SCC 589 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* A belated objection to territorial jurisdiction raised in appeal.
**Ratio decidendi:** All three conditions of Section 21 are cumulative and must be satisfied; the absence of a demonstrated consequent failure of justice is by itself fatal to the objection, however meritorious the underlying jurisdictional point may be.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The three requirements of Section 21 are conjunctive, and the failure-of-justice requirement is not a formality that follows from the jurisdictional error — it must be independently pleaded and proved."
:::

**Bahrein Petroleum Co. Ltd. v. P.J. Pappu**, AIR 1966 SC 634 — 3 Judges.
*Facts:* Whether an objection to territorial jurisdiction not raised at the trial could be entertained later.
**Ratio decidendi:** Independently of Section 21, the principle of waiver applies: a defendant who does not raise the objection to territorial jurisdiction at the earliest stage and allows the trial to proceed cannot be permitted to raise it subsequently, in the absence of prejudice.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Waiver operates alongside Section 21, and a defendant who allows the trial to run its course on the merits has waived the point independently of the statute."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
The bench strengths and reported citations above — in particular for Hira Lal Patni, Pathumma and Bahrein Petroleum — should be verified in the official reports (AIR / SCC) and checked for subsequent consideration. Note also that *Hira Lal Patni* turns on Clause 12 of the Letters Patent and on a party who had positively taken the benefit of the forum, so its waiver reasoning is at its strongest where there has been active submission, not mere silence; and *Bahrein Petroleum* states a common-law waiver principle that runs alongside Section 21 rather than under it, so it should be pleaded as an independent answer, not as a gloss on the statute.
:::

#### Strategy and drafting

**For the defendant — the checklist that preserves the objection.**

1. **Raise it as preliminary objection No. 1 in the written statement.** Not in the middle of the pleading, not in a paragraph headed "without prejudice". First, numbered, unambiguous.
2. **Plead the prejudice in the written statement itself**, not only in appeal. Set out what the wrong forum will cost you in evidentiary terms — witnesses beyond process, records beyond reach, a different substantive law applied.
3. **File a simultaneous application under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a)** asking that jurisdiction be tried as a preliminary issue, and a parallel application under Order VII Rule 10 for return of the plaint.
4. **Press for a decision before issues are settled.** If the court defers it, record your objection to the deferral in the order sheet. A note in the order sheet is your proof of promptness three years later.
5. **If the objection is rejected, do not sit on it.** Consider revision under Section 115 where the order amounts to a jurisdictional error, and in any event carry the ground into the memorandum of appeal under Order XLI Rule 1(2).

**For the plaintiff — the checklist that defeats the objection.**

1. **Push for early settlement of issues.** Every day gained tightens the Section 21 net around the defendant.
2. **When the objection comes late, answer all three limbs separately** — not merely "it is belated". Show (i) the defect was apparent on the plaint from day one; (ii) issues were settled on [date] without objection; and (iii) no prejudice is pleaded, let alone proved.
3. **Where a fresh suit is filed to set aside your decree**, move at once under Order VII Rule 11(d) pleading Section 21A **and** Section 11 in the alternative.

**Drafting traps.**

| Trap | Consequence | Cure |
|---|---|---|
| Territorial objection buried in paragraph 24 of the written statement on the merits | Court holds it was not taken at the earliest opportunity | Preliminary objection No. 1, before the para-wise reply |
| Objection pleaded without any prejudice | Fails the third limb of S.21 | Separate particularised paragraph on failure of justice |
| Ground of appeal reading only "no territorial jurisdiction" | Incomplete ground; appellate court is *statutorily barred* from allowing it | Add a distinct ground pleading consequent failure of justice with particulars |
| Fresh suit to set aside a decree on place-of-suing grounds | Rejected under O.VII R.11(d) by force of S.21A | Frame the challenge, if genuinely available, on fraud or subject-matter incompetence |
| Treating S.21(3) as curing an untransferred execution | Execution is without authority; S.39 not complied with | Obtain transfer of the decree under S.39 before executing outside the local limits |
| Objecting to pecuniary jurisdiction of a *lower* court under S.21(2) | Misconceived — that is inherent incompetence, not a S.21 irregularity | Plead it as a nullity, raisable at any stage |

**Model preliminary objection — written statement.**

> **PRELIMINARY OBJECTION NO. 1 — WANT OF TERRITORIAL JURISDICTION**
>
> 1. That this Hon'ble Court has no territorial jurisdiction to entertain, try or dispose of the present suit. The Defendant neither resides, nor carries on business, nor personally works for gain within the local limits of the jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court, the Defendant's sole residence and place of business being at [address]. No part of the cause of action pleaded in the plaint has arisen within the said local limits: the Agreement dated [date] was executed at [place], was to be performed at [place], and the breach alleged occurred at [place], all outside the said limits.
> 2. That the averments in paragraph [x] of the plaint regarding jurisdiction are conclusory and are denied. The Plaintiff has failed to comply with Order VII Rule 1(f) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, in that the plaint discloses no facts showing that this Hon'ble Court has jurisdiction.
> 3. **That the Defendant will suffer a failure of justice if the suit is tried by this Hon'ble Court.** The Defendant's material witnesses, namely [names], reside at [place], beyond the limits prescribed by Order XVI Rule 19 of the Code, and cannot be compelled to attend before this Hon'ble Court. The books of account and original records relevant to the defence of payment are maintained at [place]. The Defendant will thereby be prevented from proving his defence of [defence], and the disposal of the suit on its merits will be prejudicially affected.
> 4. That this objection is raised at the earliest possible opportunity, being the first pleading filed by the Defendant, and well before the settlement of issues, as required by Section 21(1) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908.
> 5. That the plaint is accordingly liable to be returned under Order VII Rule 10 of the Code for presentation to the proper court, and the Defendant is filing a separate application to that effect along with an application under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a) for the trial of the question of jurisdiction as a preliminary issue.

**Model ground of appeal — Section 21 compliant.**

> **GROUND [n]:** BECAUSE the learned Trial Court had no territorial jurisdiction to try the suit, no part of the cause of action having arisen within its local limits and the Appellant neither residing nor carrying on business therein.
>
> **GROUND [n+1]:** BECAUSE the said objection was taken by the Appellant in the Court of first instance at the earliest possible opportunity, namely as Preliminary Objection No. 1 in the written statement dated [date], and before the settlement of issues on [date], as required by Section 21(1) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, and was wrongly rejected by order dated [date].
>
> **GROUND [n+2]:** BECAUSE there has been a consequent failure of justice, in that the Appellant's witnesses [names], resident at [place], were beyond the process of the learned Trial Court under Order XVI Rule 19 of the Code; the Appellant's application dated [date] for issue of a commission under Order XXVI Rule 4 was rejected by order dated [date]; and the Appellant was in consequence prevented from proving his defence of [defence], which defence, if established, would have resulted in the dismissal of the suit.

## Sections 22 to 25 — Transfer of suits, appeals and other proceedings

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute The court is right, and the case is still in the wrong place.

Sections 15 to 20 tell you where a suit *may* be filed. Section 21 tells you what happens if that was got wrong. Sections 22 to 25 answer a different question altogether: the forum is **lawful** — and it is still the wrong place for this case to be tried.

That is not an illegality. It is **oppression**, or duplication, or a risk to a fair trial. So the Code does not invalidate anything. It **moves the case**.

Four provisions, and you must pick the right one or lose months:

- **Section 22** — the **defendant's** provision, and his alone. Only where the suit could have been filed in two or more competent courts. After notice. And it dies at the settlement of issues, exactly like a Section 21 objection.
- **Section 23** — pure routing. It tells you *which* court hears the Section 22 application. No test, no discretion.
- **Section 24** — the workhorse. **Any** party, or the court on its own motion. **At any stage.** Suits, appeals, execution. And by sub-section (5) it reaches a suit sitting in a court that has no jurisdiction at all.
- **Section 25** — the **Supreme Court** only, and only **across State lines**. "Expedient for the ends of justice."

The two things to carry away: **Section 24(5) can save a time-barred suit** that a return of plaint would kill. And a **High Court has no power to transfer a case into another State** — that application belongs in the Supreme Court, and filing it in the wrong place costs you the months you spend there.
:::

::: story Four powers, and the one line that decides which one you use

A transfer application is one of the few things in civil practice where the *substance* of your case barely matters at the threshold. What matters is whether you filed it in a court that has the power to give you what you want. Get that wrong and you do not lose on the merits — you never reach them.

So the chapter is best learned as a set of boundaries.

**The first boundary is who is asking.** Section 22 says "any defendant". Not any party. The plaintiff chose the forum; the Code does not let him complain about his own choice under Section 22. If a plaintiff wants a transfer, his route is Section 24 or Section 25, and there is no Section 22 available to him at all. This is a small point that decides the maintainability of the application.

**The second boundary is when.** Section 22 imports the Section 21 timetable word for word — "at the earliest possible opportunity and in all cases where issues are settled at or before such settlement". The two sections were built to run together. Section 21 kills the *objection*; Section 22 kills the *transfer application*; and both die on the same date. Section 24, by contrast, says "**at any stage**", and means it. So the practical consequence of missing the Section 22 window is not that the defendant is finished — it is that he is pushed from a comparatively structured provision onto the open discretion of Section 24, where he must persuade rather than qualify.

**The third boundary is geography, and it is the one that traps people.** Section 24 empowers the High Court or the District Court to transfer to a court **subordinate to it**. A court in another State is not subordinate to it. So a High Court cannot move a case across a State line, however compelling the facts. That is Section 25 territory and the Supreme Court's alone. *Durgesh Sharma v. Jayshree* says so in terms, and the mistake it corrects is made constantly — usually by an applicant who reasons from convenience rather than from power.

**The fourth boundary is jurisdiction itself, and here the Code does something unexpected.** Section 22 presupposes that every candidate court is competent; it is a choice *among* lawful forums. Section 24(5) throws that presupposition away in a single line: *"A suit or proceeding may be transferred under this section from a Court which has no jurisdiction to try it."*

Sit with that line, because it is the most valuable sentence in this chapter.

The ordinary consequence of filing in a court without jurisdiction is a return of the plaint under Order VII Rule 10. The plaint goes back, and on re-presentation the suit is **instituted afresh**. If limitation has run in the meantime — and by the time a jurisdictional point is decided, it usually has — the plaintiff is thrown onto Section 14 of the Limitation Act and must plead and prove that he prosecuted the earlier proceeding with due diligence and in good faith. That is a fight, and it is a fight he may lose.

Section 24(5) offers a different road. The suit is not returned; it is **moved**. The same suit continues, in a competent court, carrying its original date of institution with it. Limitation never becomes an issue, because the suit was filed in time and has never ceased to exist. Under Section 24(2) the transferee court may even proceed from the point at which the transfer took place instead of retrying.

**Which explains a strategic inversion worth remembering.** When a jurisdictional defect surfaces late, the *defendant* usually wants the plaint returned — precisely because a return restarts the clock and may destroy the claim. The *plaintiff* should resist the return and apply under Section 24(5). The two parties are fighting about limitation while appearing to argue about procedure, and the party who understands that wins.

**A last word on the grounds.** Section 24 and Section 25 both run on discretion, and discretion attracts bad applications. Two things are consistently not grounds: the convenience of counsel, and an adverse interlocutory order. An apprehension that justice will not be done must rest on objective material, because — as *Maneka Sanjay Gandhi* puts it — transfer casts a reflection on the court from which the case is moved. Imputations against a judicial officer, made without foundation, do not merely fail; they attract costs.

### The Act, decompiled

Four provisions with four different gatekeepers. Read for **who may apply**, **which court decides**, **when**, and — in Section 24(5) — for the one sentence that dispenses with jurisdiction altogether.

> **22. Power to transfer suits which may be instituted in more than one Court.** — Where a suit may be instituted in any one of two or more Courts and is instituted in one of such Courts, [[=any defendant]], [[!after notice to the other parties]], [[?may]], [[=at the earliest possible opportunity]] and in all cases where issues are settled [[=at or before such settlement]], apply to have the suit transferred to another Court, and the Court to which such application is made, after considering the objections of the other parties (if any), [[!shall determine]] in which of the several Courts having jurisdiction the suit [[!shall proceed]].

> **23. To what Court application lies.** — **(1)** Where the several Courts having jurisdiction are subordinate to the same Appellate Court, an application under section 22 [[!shall be made]] to the [[=Appellate Court]].
> **(2)** Where such Courts are subordinate to different Appellate Courts but to the same High Court, the application [[!shall be made]] to the [[=said High Court]].
> **(3)** Where such Courts are subordinate to different High Courts, the application [[!shall be made]] to the [[=High Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction the Court in which the suit is brought is situate]].

> **24. General power of transfer and withdrawal.** — **(1)** On the application of any of the parties and after notice to the parties and after hearing such of them as desired to be heard, or [[=of its own motion without such notice]], the High Court or the District Court [[?may]] [[=at any stage]] —
> **(a)** transfer any suit, appeal or other proceeding pending before it for trial or disposal to any Court subordinate to it and competent to try or dispose of the same, or
> **(b)** withdraw any suit, appeal or other proceeding pending in any Court subordinate to it, and —
> **(i)** try or dispose of the same; or
> **(ii)** transfer the same for trial or disposal to any Court subordinate to it and competent to try or dispose of the same; or
> **(iii)** retransfer the same for trial or disposal to the Court from which it was withdrawn.
> **(2)** Where any suit or proceeding has been transferred or withdrawn under sub-section (1), the Court which thereafter tries such suit may, subject to any special directions in the case of an order of transfer, either retry it or proceed from the point at which it was transferred or withdrawn.
> **(3)** For the purposes of this section, — **(a)** Courts of Additional and Assistant Judges shall be deemed to be subordinate to the District Court; **(b)** "proceeding" includes a proceeding for the execution of a decree or order.
> **(4)** The Court trying any suit transferred or withdrawn under this section from a Court of Small Causes shall, for the purposes of such suit, be deemed to be a Court of Small Causes.
> **(5)** A suit or proceeding [[?may be transferred]] under this section [[=from a Court which has no jurisdiction to try it]].

> **25. Power of Supreme Court to transfer suits, etc.** — **(1)** On the application of a party, and after notice to the parties, and after hearing such of them as desire to be heard, the Supreme Court [[?may]], [[=at any stage]], if satisfied that an order under this section is [[=expedient for the ends of justice]], direct that any suit, appeal or other proceeding be transferred from a High Court or other Civil Court in one State to a High Court or other Civil Court in any other State.
> **(2)** Every application under this section [[!shall be made by a motion]] which [[!shall be supported by an affidavit]].
> **(3)** The Court to which such suit, appeal or other proceeding is transferred shall, subject to any special directions in the order of transfer, either retry it or proceed from the stage at which it was transferred to it.
> **(4)** In dismissing any application under this section, the Supreme Court may, if it is of opinion that the application was frivolous or vexatious, order the applicant to pay by way of compensation to any person who has opposed the application such sum, not exceeding two thousand rupees, as it considers appropriate in the circumstances of the case.
> **(5)** The law applicable to any suit, appeal or other proceeding transferred under this section [[!shall be]] the law which the Court in which the suit, appeal or other proceeding was [[=originally instituted]] ought to have applied to such suit, appeal or proceeding.

**Statutory mechanics — the four transfer powers compared.**

| | **S. 22** | **S. 24** | **S. 25** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Who may apply** | The **defendant** only | **Any party**; or the court **suo motu** | **Any party** |
| **Which forum decides** | The appellate court / High Court identified by **S. 23** | The **High Court or the District Court** | The **Supreme Court** |
| **Precondition** | The suit could have been instituted in **two or more** courts | None beyond the court's supervisory relationship | Transfer must be **between States** |
| **When** | Earliest possible opportunity; **absolute bar at settlement of issues** | "**at any stage**" | "**at any stage**" |
| **Notice** | To the other parties — **mandatory** before applying | Mandatory on a party's application; **dispensed with** where the court acts of its own motion | Mandatory |
| **Test** | Which of the competent courts the suit "shall proceed" in | Judicial discretion — convenience, expedience, ends of justice | "**expedient for the ends of justice**" |
| **Transfer from a court lacking jurisdiction?** | No — all candidate courts must have jurisdiction | **Yes** — expressly, by sub-s. (5) | Yes |
| **Costs for a frivolous application** | General costs power | General costs power | **Express — up to ₹2,000** under sub-s. (4) |

**Key structural observations.**

- **Section 22 is the defendant's provision alone.** The plaintiff chose the forum; he cannot be heard to ask for a transfer under Section 22. If a plaintiff wants a transfer, his route is Section 24 or Section 25.
- **Section 22 imports the Section 21 timetable verbatim** — "at the earliest possible opportunity and in all cases where issues are settled at or before such settlement". The two provisions were designed to work in tandem: Section 21 kills the *objection*, Section 22 kills the *transfer application*, and both die at settlement of issues.
- **Notice under Section 22 is a condition precedent**, not a formality. The words are "after notice to the other parties, may … apply". An application filed without prior notice is defective.
- **Section 23 is purely a routing rule.** It contains no substantive test; it merely tells you where to file the Section 22 application. Sub-section (3) is the important one in inter-High-Court situations: the application goes to the High Court where the **suit was brought**, not where the applicant wants it sent.
- **Section 24(5) is a striking and often-forgotten power.** A suit may be transferred *from a court which has no jurisdiction to try it*. This means the High Court or District Court can transfer a wrongly-instituted suit to the right court instead of forcing a return of the plaint under Order VII Rule 10 — preserving the original date of institution and, with it, limitation. This is frequently the most valuable single tool in the chapter.
- **Section 25(5) codifies the transferee court's choice of law.** On an inter-State transfer, the transferee court applies the law that the **original** court ought to have applied. A suit transferred from Kerala to Karnataka is still governed by the substantive law and State amendments applicable in Kerala. This prevents transfer from being used to change the applicable law.

::: proviso Section 24(5) — one sentence, and it is worth more than the rest of the chapter

Sub-section (5) of Section 24 reads, in its entirety:

> *A suit or proceeding **may be transferred** under this section **from a Court which has no jurisdiction to try it**.*

Everything else in Sections 22 to 25 assumes a lawful forum and asks whether a different lawful forum would be better. This sub-section does the opposite. It lets a superior court pick up a suit that is sitting somewhere it should never have been filed and put it down where it belongs — **without returning the plaint**.

**Why that matters, in one comparison.**

| | **Return of plaint — Order VII Rule 10** | **Transfer — Section 24(5)** |
|---|---|---|
| What happens to the suit | Plaint returned; on re-presentation the suit is **instituted afresh** | The **same suit** continues, in a different court |
| Date of institution | **Lost** — the new date governs | **Preserved** |
| Limitation | Plaintiff must plead and prove **Section 14 of the Limitation Act** to exclude the earlier period | Not in issue at all |
| Evidence already recorded | Ordinarily begins again | Transferee court may proceed from the point of transfer — **Section 24(2)** |
| Available where the transferor court lacks jurisdiction | Yes — that is its purpose | **Yes, expressly** |

**The strategic inversion.** When a jurisdictional defect surfaces late, the party who *wants* the plaint returned is usually the **defendant** — because a return restarts the clock and may extinguish the claim outright. The plaintiff's move is to resist the return and apply under Section 24(1)(b) read with sub-section (5).

Both sides are arguing about limitation while appearing to argue about procedure. Say so out loud, on the record, before the court makes an Order VII Rule 10 order that cannot be undone.

**Two limits worth knowing before you rely on it.** The transfer must still be to a court **subordinate to** the High Court or District Court making the order — sub-section (5) dispenses with the transferor court's jurisdiction, not with the transferee court's competence or with the hierarchy. And the discretion remains a discretion: a court may decline where the filing was not a bona fide error but forum-shopping dressed as mistake.
:::

::: proviso The boundary a High Court cannot cross

Section 24(1) empowers the High Court or the District Court to transfer a proceeding to, or withdraw it from, a court **subordinate to it**. Those three words are the whole of the geography.

A civil court in another State is not subordinate to this High Court. So **a High Court has no power under Section 24 to send a case across a State line**, however overwhelming the convenience, however sympathetic the applicant. *Durgesh Sharma v. Jayshree* holds exactly that, and inter-State transfer is the exclusive domain of the **Supreme Court under Section 25**.

**This is a question of power, not of merit**, and it has two consequences that a discretionary point would not have:

1. **It cannot be cured by consent.** The parties cannot agree to let the High Court do it. Nor can the objection be waived by a respondent who does not take it — the court simply lacks the power.
2. **The time is unrecoverable.** A Section 24 application for inter-State relief is not a weak application; it is an incompetent one. It will be dismissed, and every month it was pending is a month the trial ran on in the forum you were trying to leave.

**The routing rule, in the order you should apply it.** *Does the transfer cross a State boundary?* If yes — Supreme Court, Section 25, by motion supported by affidavit, and nothing else will do. If no — Section 24 to the High Court or the District Court, according to which court the transferee is subordinate to.

And note the separate routing rule for Section 22 applications, which is **Section 23** and nothing else. Section 23(3) is the one that catches people: where the competent courts are subordinate to *different* High Courts, the application goes to the High Court within whose limits **the suit was brought** — not the one you are trying to move the case to.
:::

### In plain English

**Sub-module A — Why a transfer power is necessary at all.** Sections 15 to 20 frequently give the plaintiff a genuine choice of forum, and the plaintiff will naturally choose the one that suits him. Where that choice is lawful, a Section 21 objection is misconceived — the court *does* have jurisdiction. The mischief is not illegality but **oppression**. Sections 22 to 25 supply the answer: leave the forum lawful, but move the case.

**Sub-module B — Section 22 and its narrow field.** Section 22 operates only in the precise situation where "a suit **may** be instituted in any one of two or more Courts". It presupposes that every candidate court has jurisdiction. If the court in which the suit was filed lacks jurisdiction, Section 22 is inapplicable — the remedy is a Section 21 objection with a return of plaint, or a Section 24 transfer under sub-section (5).

**Sub-module C — Section 24 as the workhorse.** In practice, the overwhelming majority of transfers happen under Section 24, and for good reason:

1. It may be invoked by **any** party, including the plaintiff.
2. It applies "**at any stage**" — no settlement-of-issues guillotine.
3. It covers suits, appeals **and** "other proceedings", which by sub-section (3)(b) expressly includes **execution** proceedings.
4. It permits **withdrawal** as well as transfer, so the High Court or District Court can pull a case up to itself.
5. By sub-section (5) it works even where the transferor court has no jurisdiction.

**Sub-module D — The grounds that actually succeed under Section 24.** There is no statutory list; the discretion is guided by the ends of justice. The recurring successful grounds are:

| Ground | Weight |
|---|---|
| **Balance of convenience of parties and witnesses** — where the bulk of evidence and the parties are located | High |
| **Reasonable apprehension of a fair trial being denied**, founded on objective material | High, but must be objectively supported, not a bare allegation |
| **Common questions of law and fact in two or more suits** pending in different courts — risk of conflicting decrees | Very high; the standard ground for consolidating related suits |
| **The convenience and safety of a woman litigant**, particularly in matrimonial proceedings | Very high in practice; a well-established consideration |
| **Physical incapacity, illness or indigence of a party** making attendance at the distant forum impracticable | High, on medical or financial material |
| **Avoidance of multiplicity and conflicting findings** | High |
| Mere inconvenience or added expense to the applicant | **Low — ordinarily insufficient on its own** |
| Counsel's convenience or the applicant's preference for a particular bar | **Not a ground** |
| An apprehension based on the judge having ruled against the applicant on an interlocutory application | **Not a ground** — an adverse order is not evidence of bias |

**Sub-module E — The limits of the "fair trial apprehension" ground.** An allegation that justice will not be done must rest on material capable of creating a reasonable apprehension in the mind of a reasonable litigant — a documented pattern of hostility, a demonstrated local influence of the opposite party, a genuine threat to safety. It is not enough to assert distrust, and imputations against a judicial officer made without foundation are treated seriously and may be visited with costs.

**Sub-module F — Section 25 and inter-State transfer.** Only the Supreme Court can move a case across State lines. The statutory test is a single phrase — "**expedient for the ends of justice**" — deliberately broad. The dominant recurring application is the matrimonial transfer petition, where the wife seeks transfer of proceedings instituted by the husband in a distant State. Sub-section (4)'s costs provision, capped at ₹2,000, is a rarely-used deterrent against frivolous petitions.

**Sub-module G — Transfer versus return of plaint: the limitation point.** This distinction is worth real money to a client:

| | **Return of plaint (O. VII R. 10)** | **Transfer (S. 24)** |
|---|---|---|
| Effect on the suit | Plaint is returned; on re-presentation the suit is **instituted afresh** | The **same suit** continues in a different court |
| Limitation | Fresh institution date; the plaintiff must invoke **S. 14 of the Limitation Act** to exclude the earlier period | Original institution date **preserved** |
| Evidence already recorded | Ordinarily begins afresh | Transferee court may **proceed from the point at which it was transferred** (S. 24(2)) |
| Available where transferor court lacks jurisdiction | Yes — that is its purpose | **Yes**, expressly, under S. 24(5) |

::: tip The strategic insight of this chapter
Where a suit has been filed in the wrong court and limitation has since expired, do **not** press for a return of the plaint under Order VII Rule 10 if you are the plaintiff — a return restarts the clock and forces you onto Section 14 of the Limitation Act. Apply instead under **Section 24(5)** to have the suit transferred to the competent court. The suit continues, the institution date survives, and limitation is not in issue at all.
:::

### Test your instinct

::::instinct The lawful forum that nobody could afford

Anita, a financier at Delhi, sues Bharat, a small trader of Coimbatore, on a guarantee. The guarantee was executed at Delhi. Section 20(c) therefore gives the Delhi court jurisdiction, and there is nothing wrong with the filing.

Bharat's position is that everything else is at Coimbatore — his business, his books, his four witnesses, the place where the guarantee was to be performed. His turnover will not bear repeated air travel and Delhi counsel.

His first instinct is to plead that the Delhi court has no jurisdiction.

**What should he do instead, and where does he file it?**

:::ruling

**A jurisdictional objection is hopeless, and the answer is a transfer — but the filing address is the part he will get wrong.**

**Why Section 21 is not available.** The guarantee was executed at Delhi. Part of the cause of action arose there and Section 20(c) is satisfied on the plaint's own averments. There is no defect to object to. Bharat is not saying the court lacked power; he is saying the case does not belong there. That is the whole subject of Sections 22 to 25, and it is a different argument with a different vocabulary — **oppression**, not illegality.

**Section 22 is open to him, and this is one of the cases it was written for.** The suit *could* have been instituted in two or more competent courts: Delhi, where the guarantee was executed, and Coimbatore, where Bharat resides and where the guarantee was to be performed. Section 22 applies only in that situation, and Bharat is a defendant, which is the only person Section 22 permits to apply.

**Three conditions, and he must satisfy all of them.** Notice to Anita **before** the application — the words are "after notice to the other parties, may … apply", and that is a condition precedent, not a covering letter. The earliest possible opportunity. And, absolutely, **at or before the settlement of issues** — Section 22 imports the Section 21 timetable word for word.

**Now the part that decides whether the application is competent: where does it go?** Not to the Coimbatore court. Not to the Madras High Court. **Section 23** routes it, and sub-section (3) governs here, because the Delhi and Coimbatore courts are subordinate to different High Courts. In that situation the application goes to the High Court **within whose local limits the suit was brought** — the **Delhi High Court**.

This is counter-intuitive and it is where applications die. You apply to the High Court of the place you are trying to **leave**, not the place you are trying to reach.

**And the fallback if he is late.** If issues have already been settled, Section 22 is gone. Bharat is not finished, but he is worse off: he must apply under **Section 24** to the Delhi High Court, which applies at any stage but gives him no entitlement — only a discretion to persuade. He has traded a structured provision for an open one.

**What Anita says in answer.** That she is entitled to a forum in which she can realistically enforce a decree; that expense and inconvenience are not a denial of justice, which is what *Dr. Subramaniam Swamy v. Ramakrishna Hegde* requires; that the balance of convenience must be weighed on **both** sides, and her own witnesses and records are at Delhi; and, if Bharat has delayed, that the Section 22 window shut at the settlement of issues.

::::instinct Two suits, one joint venture, two cities

Anita and Bharat fall out over a joint venture. Anita sues at Pune for accounts. Bharat, a fortnight later, sues at Nagpur for damages. Both suits turn on the same agreement, the same alleged breaches, and very largely the same documents and witnesses.

Neither party disputes that both courts have jurisdiction.

**What is the right application, and is there a cheaper answer?**

:::ruling

**Section 24 to the Bombay High Court — and this is the strongest ground in the chapter, because it serves the court's interest as much as the applicant's.**

**Why the ground is so strong.** Common questions of law and fact, common evidence, and a real risk of **conflicting decrees** on the same agreement. The mischief is not that either party is inconvenienced; it is that the judicial system may produce two irreconcilable answers to one question, and then have to live with both. A court asked to prevent that is being offered something it wants.

**Why Section 24 and not Section 22.** Section 22 is available only to a *defendant*, and only where the *same* suit could have been filed in two or more courts. Here there are two different suits. Section 24 fits: any party may apply, the court may act of its own motion, and there is no settlement-of-issues ceiling. Both courts are in Maharashtra and subordinate to the Bombay High Court, so the geography is satisfied.

**What is actually sought.** Not "transfer both suits somewhere neutral" — that is a needless complication. Transfer the later or smaller suit to the court trying the other, so that both may be tried together, with common evidence recorded in the leading suit and separate judgments delivered. Say so in the prayer, precisely.

**Now the cheaper answer, which is worth knowing because it disposes of most of these disputes.** If the two suits were already pending **in the same court**, no transfer would be needed at all. Apply for an **analogous trial** under Section 151, invoking the court's inherent power to regulate its own procedure — evidence recorded in the leading suit and read in the connected suit by consent. Transfer is required only because the suits are in *different* courts.

**And that gives the resisting party its best line.** Rather than arguing that the causes of action are distinct — usually a losing argument on these facts — offer consent to analogous trial with common evidence and a fixed timetable. It concedes the court's real concern, costs the objector nothing of substance, and often defeats the transfer application altogether.

::::instinct The petition that was filed in a court with no power to hear it

Bharat files a petition for restitution of conjugal rights at Jodhpur. Anita has returned to her parents at Kolkata. She has an infant child, no independent income, and nobody at Jodhpur to travel with or stay with.

Her counsel files a transfer application under Section 24 in the Rajasthan High Court, asking that the petition be sent to a court at Kolkata. The application is well drafted. The facts are sympathetic. It is heard, and eight months pass.

**What happens, and what should have been done?**

:::ruling

**It is dismissed, and not on the merits. The Rajasthan High Court never had the power to grant it.**

**The boundary.** Section 24(1) permits a High Court or District Court to transfer a proceeding to, or withdraw it from, a court **subordinate to it**. A court at Kolkata is not subordinate to the Rajasthan High Court. *Durgesh Sharma v. Jayshree* holds in terms that a High Court has no power under Section 24 to transfer a case from a court subordinate to it to a court in another State. Inter-State transfer belongs exclusively to the **Supreme Court under Section 25**.

**Why this is worse than a weak application.** It is a question of *power*, not of discretion. Anita's sympathetic facts are irrelevant, because the court cannot reach them. Bharat's failure to take the objection does not help her — competence is not conferred by silence, and the parties cannot confer it by consent either. And the eight months are gone: the Jodhpur proceedings ran on throughout, which is precisely what she was trying to avoid.

**What she should have filed.** A petition to the **Supreme Court under Section 25**, by **motion supported by an affidavit** as sub-section (2) requires, on the test of whether transfer is "expedient for the ends of justice". Her material: the infant child; the absence of independent income; the absence of any person at Jodhpur; the distance and cost; and any apprehension for her safety, if there is objective material for it. The convenience of a woman litigant in matrimonial proceedings is weighed heavily, and this is the dominant recurring application under Section 25.

**How Bharat resists it, and what actually works.** Not by matching her hardship, which he will lose. By making a **concrete counter-offer**: quantified travel and accommodation for each hearing, paid in advance rather than reimbursed; a fixed sum per appearance; and a proposal for remote appearance where the court permits it. The offer must be specific, quantified, and taken on record as an undertaking — a vague willingness to bear expenses is worth nothing.

**One caution on the video-conferencing point.** Do not advance it as a general substitute for transfer. *Krishna Veni Nagam* laid down general directions to that effect; *Santhini v. Vijaya Venketesh*, a larger bench, reconsidered them, holding that in matrimonial matters the statutory scheme and the possibility of settlement ordinarily require the physical presence of the parties, subject to exceptions in the discretion of the Family Court after settlement efforts have failed. Plead remote appearance as a fact-specific proposal, not as a rule.

**And note Section 25(5), which nobody argues and occasionally matters.** On an inter-State transfer the transferee court applies the law that the **original** court ought to have applied. Moving the case does not move the applicable law, State amendments included. Transfer is a change of place, not of legal regime.

::::instinct The last day of limitation

Anita files a money suit on the very last day of limitation. Some months later it emerges that the court she filed in lacks pecuniary competence — the claim exceeds its ceiling.

Bharat's counsel, with every appearance of helpfulness, submits that the plaint should simply be returned under Order VII Rule 10 for presentation to the proper court.

**Should Anita agree?**

:::ruling

**No. That submission is the most dangerous thing said in the case, and it is dressed as a procedural courtesy.**

**What a return of plaint actually does.** Under Order VII Rule 10 the plaint goes back and, on re-presentation, the suit is **instituted afresh**. The original date of institution is gone. Limitation expired months ago. Anita would have to invoke **Section 14 of the Limitation Act** and prove that she prosecuted the earlier proceeding **with due diligence and in good faith** in a court unable to entertain it for defect of jurisdiction or other cause of a like nature. That is a contested issue of fact, decided long after the event, and she may lose it.

Bharat is not being helpful. He is trying to convert a procedural error into a complete defence.

**What Anita does instead.** She applies under **Section 24(1)(b)** read with **sub-section (5)** to the District Court — or the High Court, according to subordination — to **withdraw** the suit from the incompetent court and **transfer** it to the competent one. Sub-section (5) is express: a suit may be transferred from a court which has no jurisdiction to try it.

The consequence is the whole point. **The same suit continues.** It was instituted in time and has never ceased to exist, so limitation is not in issue at all. Under **Section 24(2)** the transferee court may even proceed from the point at which the transfer took place rather than retrying from the beginning.

**Say the real subject out loud.** Both sides are arguing about limitation while appearing to argue about procedure. Anita's counsel should put that on the record before any Order VII Rule 10 order is made, because once the plaint has been returned the position is very hard to retrieve.

**Bharat's answer, and why it fails.** He will argue that sub-section (5) is meant for territorial incompetence, not pecuniary. The sub-section says "a Court which has no jurisdiction to try it" and draws no such distinction. He will also argue that Anita should not be rescued from her own error — a fair point on discretion, and the reason her application should be candid about the mistake and supported by material showing it was bona fide rather than forum-shopping. A court that suspects the wrong court was chosen deliberately may well decline.

**One limit.** Sub-section (5) dispenses with the *transferor* court's jurisdiction. It does not dispense with the transferee court's competence, or with the requirement that the transferee be subordinate to the court making the order. And if the competent court is in another State, Section 24 cannot reach it at all.

::::instinct The execution nobody wanted moved

Anita is executing a decree against immovable property. Bharat, the judgment-debtor, applies under Section 24 to have the execution proceeding transferred out of the executing court. His grounds: the executing court has passed four orders against him in six months, and he believes Anita has local influence.

Anita's first answer is that Section 24 does not apply to execution at all.

**Is she right? And does Bharat's application succeed?**

:::ruling

**She is wrong on maintainability and right on the merits. Both halves matter.**

**Section 24 plainly covers execution.** Sub-section (3)(b) is express: for the purposes of the section, "proceeding" **includes a proceeding for the execution of a decree or order**. The application is maintainable, and an objection that it is not will simply be rejected — wasting the only opportunity Anita has to be heard first on something that counts.

**But the ground is not a ground.** Bharat has offered two things, and neither survives contact with the case law.

*Four adverse orders.* An adverse interlocutory order is not evidence of bias. If it were, every litigant who lost an application would have a transfer petition, and the more thoroughly a case had been considered the more transferable it would become. What Bharat has described is a court deciding against him, which is not a reason to move the case but a reason to appeal the orders.

*A belief about local influence.* *Maneka Sanjay Gandhi v. Rani Jethmalani* sets an **objective** test: not whether this litigant feels apprehensive, but whether the material discloses a **reasonable apprehension in the mind of a reasonable litigant** that justice will not be done. A belief is not material. Instances, dates, documents, a demonstrated pattern — those are material. And the Court has been explicit that petitions of this kind are approached with circumspection, because a transfer casts a reflection on the court from which the case is moved.

**The cost of getting this wrong is not neutral.** Unfounded imputations against a judicial officer are treated seriously. Anita should ask for costs, and should say plainly that transfer applications in execution are a familiar delaying device — because that is the frame the court will recognise.

**What Bharat could legitimately have raised.** If his real complaint is that this court cannot execute *here* — a defect in the executing court's own **local limits** — that is **Section 21(3)**, taken in the executing court, promptly, with a consequent failure of justice, and with no settlement-of-issues ceiling because execution has no issues. And if the decree was never **transferred to that court under Section 39** at all, that is not an irregularity of place but an absence of authority to execute, and it survives under Section 47.

Those are objections about power. What he filed was an objection about mood.

::: info Consolidation without transfer
Where the connected suits are already pending **in the same court**, no transfer application is needed. Apply for **analogous trial** under **Section 151**, with evidence recorded in the leading suit and read in the connected suit by consent, and separate judgments delivered. Transfer under Section 24 is required only where the suits are in *different* courts — and offering analogous trial is frequently the most effective way of *defeating* a transfer application, because it gives the court everything it actually wanted.
:::
### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

**Durgesh Sharma v. Jayshree**, (2008) 9 SCC 648 — 2 Judges.

*Facts in one line:* whether a High Court exercising power under Section 24 may transfer a case from a court subordinate to it to a court in another State, subordinate to a different High Court.

**Ratio decidendi.** It may not. A High Court has **no power under Section 24** to transfer a suit, appeal or other proceeding from a court subordinate to it to a court in another State. Section 24 operates only within the territorial limits of the High Court concerned, because the section speaks of transfer to, and withdrawal from, a court **subordinate to it**. Inter-State transfer lies exclusively with the **Supreme Court under Section 25**.

**Why this is the ratio to lead with.** It is the only proposition in this chapter that is about **power** rather than discretion, and power decides cases at the threshold. Every other question here — convenience, apprehension, common questions, hardship — is argued on the facts and can go either way. This one cannot. It disposes of an application before the merits are opened, it cannot be cured by consent, it is not waived by a respondent who fails to take it, and the time spent on the wrong application is unrecoverable.

**The sentence to plead.**

> "*Durgesh Sharma* is decisive on forum, and it is a question of jurisdiction, not of discretion: if the transfer sought crosses a State boundary, the only competent court is the Supreme Court under Section 25. This Hon'ble Court has no power under Section 24 to grant the relief prayed for, whatever the merits."

**Its limits.** It says nothing about *when* a transfer should be granted — only about *who* may grant it. Once you are in the right court, the governing authorities are *Maneka Sanjay Gandhi* on apprehension, *Dr. Subramaniam Swamy* on the ends of justice under Section 25, and *Kulwinder Kaur* on the breadth of the Section 24 discretion.

::: danger Fatal error — filing the inter-State transfer application in the High Court
This is the single most common procedural mistake in transfer practice. An application to move a case from a court in State A to a court in State B goes to the **Supreme Court under Section 25**, by motion supported by an affidavit. A Section 24 application to the High Court for that relief is not merely likely to fail — it is **beyond the High Court's power**. Every month it is pending is a month the trial runs on in the very forum you were trying to leave.

Check the geography before you check anything else.
:::

#### Sword and shield

| | **Applicant (sword)** | **Respondent (shield)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Threshold** | Check subordination and State boundaries *before* drafting a word of the merits | Take the competence point first — it disposes of the application without reaching the facts |
| **Choice of provision** | Defendant, two competent courts, issues not yet settled → **S. 22** (routed by S. 23). Otherwise → **S. 24**. Across States → **S. 25** | If the applicant is the plaintiff, or only one court was ever competent, **S. 22 is not maintainable at all** |
| **Notice** | S. 22: give notice **before** applying — condition precedent | An S. 22 application filed without prior notice is defective as filed |
| **Timing** | S. 24 and S. 25 run "at any stage"; S. 22 dies at the settlement of issues | If the applicant is out of time under S. 22, say so — it pushes him onto a discretion he must earn |
| **Best ground** | Common questions of law and fact in connected suits; risk of conflicting decrees | Offer **analogous trial** with common evidence and a fixed timetable — it gives the court what it wanted without a transfer |
| **Convenience** | Plead where the witnesses, records and parties actually are, with particulars | Convenience is comparative; the plaintiff's lawful choice of forum is not lightly displaced |
| **Apprehension of unfair trial** | Objective material only — instances, dates, orders, documented influence | An adverse interlocutory order is not bias; unfounded imputations attract costs |
| **Matrimonial S. 25 petition** | Infant child, no independent income, no companion at the distant forum, safety, distance and cost — with material | A **quantified** offer of travel and accommodation paid in advance, recorded as an undertaking, plus a fact-specific remote-appearance proposal |
| **Wrong court, limitation gone** | **S. 24(1)(b) with S. 24(5)** — the suit continues and the institution date survives | Press for return of the plaint under **O. VII R. 10** — it restarts the clock |
| **Costs** | — | **S. 25(4)** (₹2,000 cap) for a frivolous or vexatious S. 25 petition; **S. 35A** for a false or vexatious claim or defence |

#### The limitation clock

Only one provision in this group has a deadline, and the rest of the timing risk is about limitation on the *suit*, not on the application.

- **Section 22 — the only guillotine.** Earliest possible opportunity, and in all cases where issues are settled, **at or before settlement**. Identical wording to Section 21(1), and the same diarised date kills both.
- **Sections 24 and 25 — "at any stage".** No outer limit. But delay is still weighed as a discretionary factor, and an application made after the evidence is nearly closed invites the answer that the applicant is seeking a re-trial, not a fair one.

The real clock in this chapter runs elsewhere:

1. **Return of plaint restarts the suit.** Order VII Rule 10 means fresh institution on re-presentation. If limitation has run, **Section 14 of the Limitation Act** must be pleaded and proved — due diligence and good faith, in a court unable to entertain the suit for defect of jurisdiction or other cause of a like nature. It is a contested issue, not a formality.
2. **Section 24(5) stops that clock from ever starting.** The same suit continues, carrying its original institution date, and under Section 24(2) the transferee court may proceed from the point of transfer rather than retrying.
3. **A transfer application does not stay the trial.** Unless a stay is expressly obtained, the suit proceeds while the application is pending — which is why an application filed in a court without power is so expensive.
4. **Challenging a transfer order.** Transfer orders are not listed in Order XLIII Rule 1, so there is no appeal. The route is revision under **Section 115**, or supervisory jurisdiction under **Article 227**, and it must be moved promptly.

#### Interlocking matrix

| Interlock | Provision | Practical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Timing of the S.22 application | **Section 21(1)** timetable, imported verbatim | Earliest opportunity; absolute bar at settlement of issues. |
| Settlement of issues | **Order XIV Rules 1, 5** | The guillotine date for Section 22. |
| Return of plaint — the alternative to transfer | **Order VII Rule 10, 10A** | Restarts limitation; prefer S.24(5) where the plaintiff's clock has run. |
| Exclusion of time in a court without jurisdiction | **Limitation Act, 1963, S. 14** | The plaintiff's remedy after a return of plaint; requires good faith and due diligence to be pleaded and proved. |
| Stay of a suit pending elsewhere | **Section 10** (*res sub judice*) | Where the two suits are between the same parties on the same matter, S.10 may be the better remedy than transfer. |
| Consolidation / analogous trial | **Section 151** | Where both suits are already in the same court. |
| Execution proceedings are transferable | **Section 24(3)(b)** | Expressly included in "proceeding". |
| Transfer of the decree for execution — distinct power | **Sections 38, 39; Order XXI Rules 5–10** | Do not confuse a S.24 transfer of the *execution proceeding* with a S.39 transfer of the *decree*. |
| Transferee court may continue from the same stage | **Sections 24(2), 25(3)** | Avoids re-trial; ask for a specific direction to this effect in the prayer. |
| Applicable law after inter-State transfer | **Section 25(5)** | The transferee court applies the law the original court ought to have applied. |
| Affidavit in support | **Section 25(2)**; **Order XIX Rules 1–3** | The S.25 motion **must** be supported by affidavit; O.XIX R.3 requires the affidavit to be confined to facts within the deponent's own knowledge, with sources of belief disclosed. |
| Compensatory costs for a frivolous application | **Section 25(4)** (₹2,000 cap); **Section 35A** (false or vexatious claims/defences) | Two distinct costs regimes. |
| Revision | **Section 115** | An order on transfer is generally not appealable; revision, or Article 227 supervisory jurisdiction, is the route where a jurisdictional error is alleged. |
| No appeal against a transfer order | **Order XLIII Rule 1** — transfer orders are not listed | Confirms that revision or Article 227 is the challenge route. |
| Matrimonial proceedings | **Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, S. 21** applies CPC; **Family Courts Act, 1984** | S.24 and S.25 both apply to matrimonial proceedings; most reported S.25 practice arises here. |
| Video-conferencing as an alternative to transfer | High Court Video Conferencing Rules; **Order XVIII Rule 4** | Increasingly the answer offered in resistance to a transfer petition — courts often accept it. |

::: warning Two "transfers" that must never be confused
**Section 24** transfers a *proceeding* — the suit, appeal or execution case itself — from one court to another. **Section 39** transfers a *decree* to another court for execution, because the property or person is outside the original court's limits. Applying under the wrong provision is a common and expensive error: a Section 24 application will not authorise a court to execute a decree that has never been transferred to it under Section 39, and Section 21(3) will not cure that defect.
:::

#### The authorities

**Maneka Sanjay Gandhi v. Rani Jethmalani**, (1979) 4 SCC 167 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* A transfer petition founded on the petitioner's apprehension about the atmosphere in the trial court and the safety of the accused.
**Ratio decidendi:** Assurance of a fair trial is the first imperative of the dispensation of justice, and the central criterion for the court to consider when a motion for transfer is made is not the hypersensitivity or relative convenience of a party, but the existence of a **reasonable apprehension** that justice will not be done. Mere convenience of parties or their counsel is not sufficient; petitions of this kind must be approached with circumspection, since transfer casts a reflection on the court from which the case is moved.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The test in Maneka Sanjay Gandhi is objective: not whether my client feels apprehensive, but whether the material discloses a reasonable apprehension in the mind of a reasonable litigant that justice will not be done."
:::

**Dr. Subramaniam Swamy v. Ramakrishna Hegde**, (1990) 1 SCC 4 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* A petition under Section 25 to transfer a defamation suit from one State to another.
**Ratio decidendi:** The paramount consideration under Section 25 is the requirement of justice — whether the transfer is "expedient for the ends of justice". Convenience of parties is a relevant but not decisive factor, and the balance of convenience of *both* sides must be weighed. The petitioner must show that the trial in the chosen forum will result in a denial of justice, not merely that another forum would be more convenient for him.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Under Section 25 the applicant must establish that the ends of justice require the transfer — the balance of convenience of both sides is weighed, and the plaintiff's lawful choice of forum is not lightly displaced."
:::

**Kulwinder Kaur v. Kandi Friends Education Trust**, (2008) 3 SCC 659 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* Scope of the discretion under Section 24 and the grounds on which transfer may be ordered.
**Ratio decidendi:** The Court declined to lay down an exhaustive list of grounds, holding that the power under Section 24 is discretionary and must be exercised on the facts of each case in the interests of justice. Illustrative considerations include the balance of convenience, the convenience of parties and witnesses, whether a fair and impartial trial is possible, whether questions of law of a complex nature are involved, and whether the transfer would cause hardship to the opposite party.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Section 24 confers a wide but judicial discretion; the grounds are illustrative and not exhaustive, and each application turns on whether the interests of justice require the transfer on the facts."
:::

**Krishna Veni Nagam v. Harish Nagam**, (2017) 4 SCC 150 — 2 Judges, and **Santhini v. Vijaya Venketesh**, (2018) 1 SCC 1 — 3 Judges.
*Facts and relationship:* Krishna Veni Nagam sought to lay down general directions permitting matrimonial proceedings to be conducted by video-conferencing so as to reduce the volume of transfer petitions. Santhini, a larger bench, reconsidered that approach.
**Ratio decidendi:** The larger bench in Santhini overruled the general directions in Krishna Veni Nagam so far as they made video-conferencing a substitute for transfer as a matter of course, holding that in matrimonial matters the statutory scheme and the possibility of settlement require the physical presence of parties, subject to exceptions in the discretion of the Family Court after settlement efforts have failed.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The video-conferencing answer to a transfer petition must be advanced as a fact-specific proposal within the discretion of the court, and not as a general substitute for transfer — Santhini having reconsidered the general directions in Krishna Veni Nagam."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
This is a fast-moving area, particularly in matrimonial transfer practice, where the position on video-conferencing has shifted between Krishna Veni Nagam, Santhini and subsequent developments including the general adoption of remote hearings. **Confirm the current position, the citations and the bench strengths in the official reports before relying on any of the above.**

Note also the difference in kind between these authorities and the winning ratio above. *Durgesh Sharma* settles a question of **power**, and is stable. Everything in this group goes to **discretion**, is decided on its facts, and is correspondingly sensitive to how each later bench has framed it — so cite the decision whose facts resemble yours, and do not extract a general rule from any of them.
:::

#### Strategy and drafting

**Choosing the right provision — a decision tree.**

1. **Does the court in which the suit is filed have jurisdiction?**
   - **No** → Section 21 objection + Order VII Rule 10 (as defendant); or Section 24(5) transfer (as plaintiff, to save limitation).
   - **Yes** → go to 2.
2. **Are the two candidate courts in different States?**
   - **Yes** → **Section 25**, Supreme Court, motion supported by affidavit. Nothing else will do.
   - **No** → go to 3.
3. **Are you the defendant, and have issues not yet been settled?**
   - **Yes** → **Section 22**, after notice, addressed to the court identified by **Section 23**.
   - **No** (you are the plaintiff, or issues are settled) → **Section 24**, to the High Court or District Court, available at any stage.

**Where to file a Section 22 application — the Section 23 routing table.**

| Situation | Application lies to |
|---|---|
| Candidate courts subordinate to the **same** appellate court | That **appellate court** (typically the District Court) |
| Candidate courts subordinate to **different** appellate courts but the **same** High Court | That **High Court** |
| Candidate courts subordinate to **different** High Courts | The High Court within whose local limits the court **in which the suit is brought** is situate |

**Drafting traps.**

1. **Filing a Section 22 application without prior notice to the other parties.** The section says "after notice to the other parties, may … apply". Serve notice, record service, and annex proof.
2. **Missing the settlement-of-issues deadline for Section 22** and then filing under Section 22 anyway. If issues are settled, abandon Section 22 and go to Section 24, which has no such limit — but expect the delay to weigh against you on discretion.
3. **Pleading bias without material.** Bare imputations against a judicial officer will sink the application and may attract costs. Plead facts, dates, orders — or plead convenience instead.
4. **Filing an inter-State transfer application under Section 24.** See the danger box above.
5. **Omitting the affidavit in a Section 25 petition.** Sub-section (2) makes it mandatory; and under Order XIX Rule 3 the affidavit must be confined to facts within the deponent's knowledge, with sources of information and belief expressly disclosed.
6. **Failing to ask for the "continue from the same stage" direction.** Sections 24(2) and 25(3) permit the transferee court either to retry or to proceed from the stage reached, "subject to any special directions". If your client has already led evidence, ask expressly for a direction that the transferee court proceed from the stage at which the case was transferred; otherwise you may face a full retrial.
7. **Overlooking Section 10.** Where the real complaint is that two suits between the same parties on the same matter are pending, *res sub judice* under Section 10 may achieve what you need without the discretionary hazards of a transfer application.

**Strategic timing.**

| Application | Deadline | Consequence of delay |
|---|---|---|
| Section 22 | Earliest possible opportunity; **at or before settlement of issues** | Right is extinguished; you are relegated to S.24 |
| Section 24 | "At any stage" | No bar, but delay is a strong discretionary factor against you |
| Section 25 | "At any stage" | Same |
| Objection under Section 21 | Earliest possible opportunity; at or before settlement of issues | Objection is barred in appeal and revision |

**Model application under Section 24 — prayer clause.**

> It is therefore most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to:
> **(a)** withdraw Civil Suit No. [___] of [year] titled *[cause title]* from the file of the learned [Court] at [place], under Section 24(1)(b) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908;
> **(b)** transfer the said suit for trial and disposal to the learned [Court] at [place], being a court subordinate to this Hon'ble Court and competent to try and dispose of the same;
> **(c)** direct, under Section 24(2) of the Code, that the transferee court shall proceed from the stage at which the said suit stands transferred, and shall not retry the suit, the evidence of PW-1 and PW-2 having already been recorded;
> **(d)** stay all further proceedings in the said suit before the learned [Court] at [place] during the pendency of the present application; and
> **(e)** pass such further or other order as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit and proper in the facts and circumstances of the case.

**Model transfer petition under Section 25 — grounds skeleton.**

> **GROUNDS**
> **A.** BECAUSE the Petitioner is a woman residing at [place] in the State of [State], has no independent source of income, and is the sole custodian of a minor child aged [age] years.
> **B.** BECAUSE the proceedings have been instituted by the Respondent at [place] in the State of [State], at a distance of approximately [___] kilometres from the Petitioner's residence, involving [___] hours of travel each way and an expenditure of approximately ₹[___] per hearing, which the Petitioner is wholly unable to bear.
> **C.** BECAUSE the Petitioner has no relative or acquaintance at [place] who could accompany or accommodate her, and she cannot safely travel alone with an infant child.
> **D.** BECAUSE all the material witnesses of the Petitioner, namely [names], reside at [place], and the documents relevant to her defence are situate there.
> **E.** BECAUSE it is expedient for the ends of justice within the meaning of Section 25 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 that the said proceedings be transferred to the competent court at [place].
> **F.** BECAUSE the Respondent, being [occupation] with an income of approximately ₹[___] per month, is far better placed to bear the inconvenience of travel than the Petitioner.

## Chapter consolidation — where do I file, and what if I got it wrong?

**Part A — The forum table.**

| Nature of suit | Governing provision | Forum |
|---|---|---|
| Recovery, partition, mortgage, declaration of right to, or compensation for wrong to, **immovable property** | **S. 16(a)–(e)** | Where the property is situate — **mandatory** |
| Specific performance **simpliciter**, no possession claimed | **Proviso to S. 16** | Situs **or** where the defendant resides / carries on business — plaintiff's option |
| Specific performance **with possession** | **S. 16**, proviso displaced | Situs only |
| Immovable property across **two or more** jurisdictions | **S. 17** | Any court where a portion lies, provided it is competent for the **entire** claim |
| Situs genuinely **uncertain** | **S. 18** | Any of the candidate courts, on recording a statement of uncertainty |
| Compensation for wrong to the **person** or to **movables** | **S. 19** | Where the wrong was done, **or** where the defendant resides / carries on business — plaintiff's option |
| **Everything else** — contract, money, accounts, injunction in personam | **S. 20** | Defendant's residence / business (all, or one with leave), **or** where the cause of action arose wholly or in part |
| **Grade** of court, in every case | **S. 15** | The lowest grade competent by value |

**Part B — The error-and-remedy table.**

| The problem | Who raises it | Provision | When | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Court lacks **subject-matter** jurisdiction | Anyone | **S. 9**; O. VII R. 11(d) | **Any stage**, any proceeding | Decree is a **nullity** |
| Suit filed in a court of **lower** grade than competent | Defendant | Inherent incompetence; O. VII R. 10 | Any stage | Decree is a **nullity** |
| Suit filed in a court of **higher** grade than necessary | Defendant | **S. 15**, cured by **S. 21(2)** | Earliest opportunity, before issues | Irregularity; decree stands absent failure of justice |
| Wrong **place of suing** | Defendant | **Ss. 16–20**, cured by **S. 21(1)**; O. VII R. 10 | Earliest opportunity, before issues | Irregularity; decree stands absent failure of justice |
| Wrong **local limits of the executing court** | Judgment-debtor | **S. 21(3)**; S. 47 | Earliest opportunity in the executing court | Objection barred unless failure of justice |
| Fresh suit to set aside a decree on **place of suing** | Defeated party | **Barred by S. 21A**; O. VII R. 11(d) | — | Plaint rejected |
| Forum lawful but **oppressive**, both courts competent | **Defendant only** | **S. 22** (routed by **S. 23**) | Before settlement of issues | Transfer to the other competent court |
| Forum lawful but inconvenient; or related suits in different courts; or execution to be moved | **Any party**, or court *suo motu* | **S. 24** | **Any stage** | Transfer or withdrawal, **even from a court without jurisdiction** (s-s. 5) |
| Transfer **across State boundaries** | Any party | **S. 25** — Supreme Court **only** | Any stage | Transfer if expedient for the ends of justice |

::: tip The three sentences worth memorising from this chapter
**One.** Subject-matter jurisdiction can never be conferred by consent or cured by acquiescence; place and pecuniary jurisdiction ordinarily can.
**Two.** A place-of-suing objection not taken in the court of first instance at the earliest opportunity and before the settlement of issues, and unaccompanied by a demonstrated consequent failure of justice, is dead — in appeal by force of Section 21, and in a fresh suit by force of Section 21A.
**Three.** Where the plaintiff has filed in the wrong court and limitation has since run, transfer under Section 24(5) preserves the suit; return of the plaint under Order VII Rule 10 does not.
:::
