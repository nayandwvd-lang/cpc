# Sections 15 to 25 — Place of Suing, Objections to Jurisdiction and Transfer of Suits

Sections 9 to 14 answered *whether a civil court may hear the dispute at all*. Sections 15 to 25 answer the next question: **which** civil court. This chapter is about pecuniary competence, territorial competence, the forum-selection war that precedes almost every commercial suit, and the machinery by which a suit is moved from one court to another.

The single most important idea in this chapter is a distinction the CPC draws and litigants constantly miss: **subject-matter incompetence is fatal and incurable; pecuniary and territorial incompetence are curable and, unless promptly objected to, forgiven.** Section 21 is the provision that forgives.

| Section | One-line function |
|---|---|
| **15** | Every suit shall be instituted in the court of the **lowest grade** competent to try it. |
| **16** | Suits concerning **immovable property** are to be instituted where the property is situate. |
| **17** | Immovable property within the jurisdiction of **different courts** — sue in any one of them. |
| **18** | Where it is **uncertain** in which court's local limits the property lies. |
| **19** | Suits for **compensation for wrongs** to person or movables — where the wrong was done, or where the defendant resides/carries on business. |
| **20** | The **residuary** rule — defendant's residence or place of business, or where the cause of action arose wholly or in part. |
| **21** | **Objections** to place of suing, pecuniary limits and competence of the executing court — when they may be taken, and the consequential-failure-of-justice requirement. |
| **21A** | **No substantive suit** to set aside a decree on the ground of an objection as to place of suing. |
| **22** | Power to **transfer** a suit which may be instituted in more than one court, on the defendant's application. |
| **23** | To **which court** the application under Section 22 lies. |
| **24** | **General power of transfer and withdrawal** vested in the High Court and the District Court. |
| **25** | Power of the **Supreme Court** to transfer suits, appeals and other proceedings between States. |

::: info The architecture in one breath
Sections 15 to 20 tell you where you **must** or **may** file. Section 21 tells you what happens when you file in the wrong place anyway — and the answer is usually "nothing, unless the defendant objected at the earliest possible opportunity **and** a failure of justice resulted". Sections 22 to 25 provide the corrective machinery when the forum, though lawful, is inconvenient or unfair.
:::

## Section 15 — Court in which suits to be instituted

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **15. Court in which suits to be instituted.** — Every suit shall be instituted in the Court of the lowest grade competent to try it.

**Statutory mechanics.**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "Every suit **shall** be instituted" | **Mandatory in form** | The obligation is on the plaintiff, and it is unqualified in its language. |
| "Court of the **lowest grade**" | Hierarchical, not territorial | Refers to the vertical ladder — Civil Judge (Junior Division) → Civil Judge (Senior Division) → District Judge — not to geography. |
| "**competent** to try it" | Qualifying condition | Competence is measured by pecuniary value (read with the State's Civil Courts Act) and by any special jurisdiction conferred or withheld. |

**The critical structural point.** Despite the word "shall", Section 15 is consistently treated as a **rule of procedure and administrative convenience**, not a rule of jurisdiction. Its object is to prevent the higher courts from being clogged with petty litigation and to give the litigant the benefit of a cheaper, nearer forum. It follows that:

- Instituting a suit in a court of **higher** grade than necessary is an **irregularity**, curable, and cured by Section 21(2) unless objected to at the earliest opportunity with consequent failure of justice.
- Instituting a suit in a court of **lower** grade than competent — i.e. beyond that court's pecuniary ceiling — is a **nullity**. The lower court simply lacks the power. This is not a Section 15 problem at all; it is inherent want of pecuniary jurisdiction, and the decree is void.

**How "value" is fixed.** For the purpose of testing pecuniary competence, the court looks in the first instance to the **valuation stated in the plaint**, provided that valuation is not demonstrably arbitrary or made with the object of forum-shopping. The plaintiff is dominus litis on valuation, but not licensed to lie: an artificially deflated valuation invites a Section 21(2) objection, and an artificially inflated one invites an application to return the plaint.

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — Grade versus place.** Two independent axes govern where a plaint goes:

1. **Vertical (grade / pecuniary)** — governed by Section 15 read with the State Civil Courts Act. Which rung of the ladder?
2. **Horizontal (territorial / place of suing)** — governed by Sections 16 to 20. Which district or taluka?

A plaint must satisfy both. Section 15 speaks only to the first.

**Sub-module B — Why "lowest grade" is a benefit, not a burden.** The rule exists for the litigant and the system, not as a jurisdictional trap. Filing lower means lower cost, closer court, faster disposal, and — importantly — a preserved first appeal to the District Judge rather than straight to the High Court.

**Sub-module C — Valuation for jurisdiction versus valuation for court fee.** These are conceptually distinct and, in many classes of suit, numerically different. Court fee is governed by the Court Fees Act, 1870 (or the State equivalent); jurisdictional value by the Suits Valuation Act, 1887 and the State Civil Courts Act. In a suit for declaration with consequential relief, for instance, the court-fee valuation and the jurisdictional valuation may diverge sharply. **Always plead both, separately and expressly.**

**Sub-module D — What happens on a mid-suit change in value.** If the value of the subject matter appreciates during the suit, or an amendment enhances the claim beyond the trying court's ceiling, the court must return the plaint under Order VII Rule 10 for presentation to the proper court. Jurisdiction is tested continuously, not only at institution.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — the deflated valuation.** P sues D for recovery of ₹9,80,000 on a dishonoured cheque but values the suit at ₹4,00,000 "for purposes of jurisdiction" to keep it before the Civil Judge (Junior Division), whose ceiling is ₹5,00,000, because that court's board is lighter.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P will argue that valuation is his prerogative and the ceiling was not breached on the plaint as framed.
- **Shield (defendant):** D files an application at the first hearing, before framing of issues, contending that the relief claimed is a liquidated money decree of ₹9,80,000; the valuation for jurisdiction in a money suit **must** equal the amount claimed; the court is therefore pecuniarily incompetent and the plaint must be returned under Order VII Rule 10. This is not a Section 21 irregularity — it is inherent want of pecuniary jurisdiction in a court of *lower* grade, and no amount of delay cures it.

**Illustration 2 — the inflated valuation.** P, wanting the perceived advantage of a District Judge, values a partition suit over an ancestral house at ₹80,00,000 when the share claimed is worth ₹6,00,000.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P says the market value of the property justifies it.
- **Shield (defendant):** D applies for return of the plaint to the court of lowest competent grade, and separately for direction that court fee be paid on the true value. But note: filing in the *higher* court is the classic Section 15 irregularity. Under Section 21(2), the objection succeeds only if taken in the court of first instance at the earliest possible opportunity **and** a consequent failure of justice is shown. D must therefore move promptly and articulate concrete prejudice — loss of a first appeal to the District Court, for example.

**Illustration 3 — mid-trial appreciation.** In a specific-performance suit valued at ₹4,50,000 (the agreed consideration), P amends to claim mesne profits and damages taking the total to ₹12,00,000. The Civil Judge (Junior Division) must, on allowing the amendment, return the plaint under Order VII Rule 10 with an endorsement, for presentation to the Senior Division.

### 4. Procedural Interlocking Matrix

| Interlock | Provision | Practical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Return of plaint for want of pecuniary jurisdiction | **Order VII Rule 10** | Court endorses date of presentation, date of return, name of party presenting, and reasons. Plaint is re-presented, not re-filed. |
| Appeal against return | **Order XLIII Rule 1(a)** | An order under O.VII R.10 returning a plaint is appealable. |
| Fixing the day for appearance in the proper court | **Order VII Rule 10A** | Where the return is after the defendant has appeared, the court may fix a date for appearance in the transferee court, sparing fresh summons. |
| Statement of value in the plaint | **Order VII Rule 1(i)** | The plaint **shall** contain a statement of the value of the subject matter for jurisdiction *and* for court fees. |
| Valuation of the subject matter | **Suits Valuation Act, 1887**, Ss. 8, 9, 11 | Section 11 is the crucial one — an appellate court will not reverse for over- or under-valuation unless it prejudicially affected the disposal on merits. This is Section 21's twin. |
| Court fee | **Court Fees Act, 1870**, S. 7 | Ad valorem for money and possession suits; fixed or notional for pure declarations; consequential relief triggers ad valorem. |
| Limitation | **Limitation Act, 1963, S. 14** | Time spent bona fide prosecuting in a court **without jurisdiction** is excluded. This is the plaintiff's lifeline after a return of plaint. |
| Interlocutory application | Application under O.VII R.10 / R.11(d) with S.151 | The defendant's standard vehicle for a pecuniary-competence objection. |

::: warning The Section 14 Limitation Act trap
When a plaint is **returned** under Order VII Rule 10, the suit is deemed instituted afresh on the date of re-presentation — limitation is **not** automatically saved. You must expressly plead and prove Section 14 of the Limitation Act, 1963 (bona fides, due diligence, defect of jurisdiction or other cause of a like nature). Never assume the return carries your filing date with it.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Kiran Singh v. Chaman Paswan**, AIR 1954 SC 340 — Constitution Bench (5 Judges).
*Facts:* A suit undervalued in the trial court, with the appeal consequently going to the District Court instead of the High Court; the objection was raised only at the second appellate stage.
**Ratio decidendi:** A defect of jurisdiction, *whether it is pecuniary or territorial, or in respect of the subject-matter of the action*, strikes at the very authority of the court and a decree passed without jurisdiction is a nullity. **But** Section 11 of the Suits Valuation Act (and, by parity, Section 21 CPC) enacts a deliberate departure for over- and under-valuation: such a decree is not to be reversed unless the error prejudicially affected the disposal of the case on its merits.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"An objection founded merely on over- or under-valuation cannot avail my learned friend at this stage: he must, under Kiran Singh, demonstrate that the error prejudicially affected the disposal of the suit on its merits, and he has pleaded no such prejudice."
:::

**Harshad Chiman Lal Modi v. DLF Universal Ltd.**, (2005) 5 SCC 791 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* A suit for specific performance of an agreement relating to immovable property situate outside the territorial jurisdiction of the court in which it was filed.
**Ratio decidendi:** Objections as to **territorial** and **pecuniary** jurisdiction are subject to Section 21 and can be waived; but an objection as to **subject-matter** jurisdiction goes to the root, cannot be waived, and consent cannot confer what the statute withholds. The court drew the line precisely where Section 21 draws it.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Harshad Chiman Lal Modi is authority for the proposition that competence over the subject matter can neither be conferred by consent nor cured by acquiescence — unlike place of suing, which Section 21 expressly forgives."
:::

**Commercial Aviation & Travel Co. v. Vimla Pannalal**, (1988) 3 SCC 423 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* Valuation in a suit for accounts, where the plaintiff is unable to state a precise figure.
**Ratio decidendi:** In suits falling under Section 7(iv) of the Court Fees Act, the plaintiff has the right to place his own valuation and the court will not ordinarily interfere; but where an objective standard of valuation is available, the plaintiff cannot arbitrarily choose a lower figure.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The plaintiff's discretion in valuation is real but not absolute — where an objective yardstick exists, arbitrary valuation is open to correction."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Citations, bench strength and the precise scope of each holding should be confirmed against the official reports (SCC / AIR) and checked for subsequent consideration before being placed before a court. Bench strength for the two-Judge decisions above is stated from the reports and should be re-verified.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**Drafting traps that cause rejection or return.**

1. **Omitting the twin valuation statement.** Order VII Rule 1(i) requires a statement of value for jurisdiction *and* for court fees. A plaint carrying only one is defective and invites an objection at the registry stage itself.
2. **Copy-pasting the court-fee figure into the jurisdiction paragraph.** In declaratory and injunction suits these figures routinely differ. Stating them as identical without justification is the commonest drafting error in this area.
3. **Ignoring the State Civil Courts Act.** Pecuniary ceilings are State subjects and are periodically revised by notification. Check the *current* notified ceiling for the State and the district before filing.
4. **Filing high "to be safe".** It is not safe. It is a Section 15 irregularity, it costs the client a cheaper first appeal, and it hands the defence a preliminary objection.

**Strategic timing.**

- A pecuniary objection to a court of **lower** grade (inherent incompetence) may be taken at **any** time — including in execution, including for the first time in appeal — because the decree is a nullity.
- A Section 15 objection to a court of **higher** grade must be taken **at the earliest possible opportunity in the court of first instance and before settlement of issues** (Section 21(2)), and must be accompanied by a pleaded case of consequent failure of justice.
- Where you anticipate a valuation fight, raise it as a **preliminary issue under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a)** — jurisdiction of the court is one of the two categories on which a preliminary issue is permitted.

**Model prayer checklist — application for return of plaint (pecuniary).**

> It is therefore most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to:
> **(a)** frame a preliminary issue under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a) CPC on the pecuniary competence of this Hon'ble Court to try the suit;
> **(b)** hold that the suit as valued and as framed exceeds the pecuniary limits of this Hon'ble Court;
> **(c)** direct the return of the plaint under Order VII Rule 10 CPC with the endorsement required by Rule 10(2), for presentation to the proper court;
> **(d)** fix a date under Order VII Rule 10A CPC for the appearance of the parties before the proper court; and
> **(e)** pass such further or other order as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit in the circumstances of the case.

## Sections 16, 17 & 18 — Suits relating to immovable property

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **16. Suits to be instituted where subject-matter situate.** — Subject to the pecuniary or other limitations prescribed by any law, suits —
> **(a)** for the recovery of immovable property with or without rent or profits,
> **(b)** for the partition of immovable property,
> **(c)** for foreclosure, sale or redemption in the case of a mortgage of or charge upon immovable property,
> **(d)** for the determination of any other right to or interest in immovable property,
> **(e)** for compensation for wrong to immovable property,
> **(f)** for the recovery of movable property actually under distraint or attachment,
> shall be instituted in the Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction the property is situate:
>
> **Provided that** a suit to obtain relief respecting, or compensation for wrong to, immovable property held by or on behalf of the defendant may, where the relief sought can be entirely obtained through his personal obedience, be instituted either in the Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction the property is situate, or in the Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction the defendant actually and voluntarily resides, or carries on business, or personally works for gain.
>
> ***Explanation.*** — In this section "property" means property situate in India.

> **17. Suits for immovable property situate within jurisdiction of different Courts.** — Where a suit is to obtain relief respecting, or compensation for wrong to, immovable property situate within the jurisdiction of different Courts, the suit may be instituted in any Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction any portion of the property is situate: Provided that, in respect of the value of the subject matter of the suit, the entire claim is cognizable by such Court.

> **18. Place of institution of suit where local limits of jurisdiction of Courts are uncertain.** — **(1)** Where it is alleged to be uncertain within the local limits of the jurisdiction of which of two or more Courts any immovable property is situate, any one of those Courts may, if satisfied that there is ground for the alleged uncertainty, record a statement to that effect and thereupon proceed to entertain and dispose of any suit relating to that property, and its decree in the suit shall have the same effect as if the property were situate within the local limits of its jurisdiction: Provided that the suit is one with respect to which the Court is competent as regards the nature and value of the suit to exercise jurisdiction.
> **(2)** Where a statement has not been recorded under sub-section (1), and an objection is taken before an Appellate or Revisional Court that a decree or order in a suit relating to such property was made by a Court not having jurisdiction where the property is situate, the Appellate or Revisional Court shall not allow the objection unless in its opinion there was, at the time of the institution of the suit, no reasonable ground for uncertainty as to the Court having jurisdiction with respect thereto and there has been a consequent failure of justice.

**Statutory mechanics of Section 16.**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "**Subject to** the pecuniary or other limitations" | Subordinating clause | Section 16 fixes *place*, never *grade*. Section 15 still applies on top of it. |
| "**shall** be instituted in the Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction the property is situate" | **Mandatory** | The *forum rei sitae* rule. Unlike Section 20, this is not a menu — it is a command. |
| The **proviso** | **Enabling exception** | Converts the mandatory rule into an option, but only when the condition precedent is satisfied. |
| "where the relief sought can be **entirely** obtained through his **personal obedience**" | **Condition precedent** | The word "entirely" is the whole battleground. If any part of the decree requires the court to operate directly on the land, the proviso is unavailable. |
| **Explanation** | Territorial limiter | "Property" means property situate **in India**. Section 16 therefore has nothing to say about foreign immovable property; that is governed by the common-law *Moçambique* principle as adapted, and by Section 20. |

**Statutory mechanics of Section 17.** The operative word is "**may**" — Section 17 confers an *election* on the plaintiff where a single property, or a single relief, straddles two or more jurisdictions. But the proviso imposes a hard **condition precedent**: the court chosen must be competent, in terms of value, for the **entire** claim, not merely the portion within its limits. Section 17 relaxes territory; it does not relax pecuniary competence.

**Statutory mechanics of Section 18.** Two distinct limbs:

- **Sub-section (1)** — a **prophylactic** power. The court, before proceeding, *records a statement* that there is ground for uncertainty. Having done so, its decree is deemed as valid as if the property lay squarely within its limits. Recording the statement is the condition precedent to the deeming fiction.
- **Sub-section (2)** — a **curative** provision, and a near-twin of Section 21. Where no statement was recorded, the appellate or revisional court **shall not** allow the objection unless *both* (i) there was no reasonable ground for uncertainty at institution, **and** (ii) there has been a consequent failure of justice. Both conditions are cumulative; the double negative makes the burden squarely the objector's.

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — Why immovable property is treated differently.** Land cannot be moved to the court; the court must go to the land. Only the court of the *situs* can effectively execute a decree for possession, effect a partition by metes and bounds, or order a sale of mortgaged property. The rule is therefore one of practical enforceability, not of convenience.

**Sub-module B — The six categories in Section 16(a) to (f).**

| Clause | Covers | Typical suit |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | Recovery of immovable property, with or without rent/profits | Ejectment; suit for possession with mesne profits |
| (b) | Partition | Partition of coparcenary or co-owned property |
| (c) | Mortgage remedies | Foreclosure, sale, redemption |
| (d) | Determination of any other right to or interest in immovable property | Declaration of title; easement; permanent injunction restraining interference with possession |
| (e) | Compensation for wrong to immovable property | Damages for trespass, for nuisance, for waste |
| (f) | Recovery of movable property under distraint or attachment | Claim to goods seized under a distress warrant |

Clause **(d)** is the residuary net and it is wide: any suit whose object is to determine a right *to or in* land falls here.

**Sub-module C — The proviso: the "personal obedience" test.** This is the most commercially significant idea in the chapter. Ask a single question: *if the defendant simply obeys the decree, is the plaintiff fully satisfied — without the court having to act upon the land itself?*

- **Yes → the proviso applies.** The plaintiff may sue where the defendant resides or carries on business. Classic case: a suit for **specific performance** of an agreement to sell land, where the only decree needed is one directing the defendant to execute a sale deed. The defendant's own act completes the relief.
- **No → the proviso does not apply.** Classic case: specific performance **plus** a decree for delivery of possession. Possession requires the court to act on the land through its bailiff. The word "entirely" is defeated, and the suit must go to the situs court.

**Sub-module D — Section 17 in practice: the straddling property.** A single agricultural holding lies partly in District A and partly in District B; or a mortgage secures three flats in three districts. The plaintiff may institute in any one court where *some portion* lies. The purpose is to prevent the absurdity of splitting one cause of action into three suits — which would itself run headlong into Order II Rule 2. Note the proviso's discipline: if the entire claim is worth ₹90,00,000 and the chosen court's ceiling is ₹50,00,000, Section 17 does not help; you must go to a court competent for the whole.

**Sub-module E — Section 18 and the genuinely uncertain boundary.** Municipal boundaries shift; village revenue records conflict; a building sits astride a line. Section 18(1) allows a court to say, in terms, "there is real doubt where this property lies; I record that doubt and proceed." That recorded statement is armour: the decree cannot afterwards be attacked on situs grounds. If the court neglects to record it, sub-section (2) still protects the decree — but only through the harder, cumulative test.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — specific performance, bare execution of a sale deed.** P (Mumbai) contracts to buy D's plot in Nashik. D resides and carries on business in Mumbai. P is already in possession under a licence and needs only the conveyance.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P files in Mumbai, expressly pleading that the entire relief — execution and registration of the sale deed — can be obtained through D's personal obedience, and invoking the proviso to Section 16. He does **not** claim possession.
- **Shield (defendant):** D will scour the prayer clause for any relief requiring the court to act on the Nashik land — a prayer for possession, for demolition, for mutation, for injunction against third parties in occupation. If he finds one, he contends the word "entirely" is defeated and applies under Order VII Rule 10 for return of the plaint to Nashik.

::: warning Drafting Trap — the possession prayer that destroys the forum
Counsel routinely add "and be pleased to direct the defendant to deliver vacant and peaceful possession of the suit property" as a reflex. In a proviso-to-Section-16 suit, that single clause moves the case out of your chosen court. If the client is already in possession, plead that fact in the body **and** omit the possession prayer. If the client needs possession, accept the situs forum and file there.
:::

**Illustration 2 — partition where the estate straddles districts.** A joint family estate comprises a house in District A and agricultural land in District B. The plaintiff coparcener seeks partition of the whole.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P invokes Section 17, files in District A, and pleads (i) that a portion of the property is situate within that court's limits, and (ii) that the aggregate value of his share is within that court's pecuniary competence. Filing one suit for the whole estate is not merely permitted — it is compelled by Order II Rule 2, since a partition of joint family property is one cause of action.
- **Shield (defendant):** D attacks the proviso to Section 17 — the *entire* claim, not the District A portion, must be within the court's pecuniary ceiling. He puts the total valuation of the estate in issue and seeks a Commissioner's valuation.

**Illustration 3 — the injunction against interference.** P sues for a permanent injunction restraining D from interfering with P's possession of a plot in Gurugram; D lives in Delhi.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P may be tempted to file in Delhi under the proviso, arguing that D's forbearance is "personal obedience".
- **Shield (defendant):** D answers that the suit is one "for the determination of any other right to or interest in immovable property" under Section 16(d) — the very substance of the suit is P's possessory title to Gurugram land, and the proviso is confined to relief obtainable *entirely* through personal obedience in respect of property "held by or on behalf of the defendant". Where the property is in the plaintiff's own possession and the defendant is a stranger to its title, the proviso's own language does not fit. The safer forum is Gurugram.

**Illustration 4 — the uncertain boundary.** A disputed strip of land sits on the line between two tahsils; the revenue map and the municipal map disagree. P files in Tahsil Court A.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P files an application under Section 18(1) at the threshold, annexing both maps, and asks the court to record a statement of uncertainty before proceeding. That single paragraph immunises the eventual decree.
- **Shield (defendant):** If no statement was recorded and D lost at trial, D raises the objection in first appeal. He must then satisfy the appellate court, cumulatively, that there was **no reasonable ground** for uncertainty *and* that a **failure of justice** resulted. In practice, the existence of two conflicting official maps destroys the first limb outright.

### 4. Procedural Interlocking Matrix

| Interlock | Provision | Practical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Return of plaint for want of territorial jurisdiction | **Order VII Rule 10 & 10A** | The standard consequence of a successful Section 16 objection. |
| Rejection where suit appears barred | **Order VII Rule 11(d)** | Rarely the right vehicle for territorial objections — return, not rejection, is the correct relief. Pleading R.11(d) where R.10 applies is a common error. |
| Preliminary issue on jurisdiction | **Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a)** | Jurisdiction of the court is one of only two grounds on which the court may try an issue as a preliminary issue. |
| Commission to examine or measure property | **Order XXVI Rules 9, 13, 14** | Local investigation to fix boundaries (relevant to S.18) and to make a partition (S.16(b)). |
| Preliminary and final decree in partition | **Order XX Rule 18** | Section 16(b) suits produce a two-stage decree; the situs court alone can effect division by metes and bounds. |
| Mortgage suits | **Order XXXIV Rules 2–8** | Preliminary decree for foreclosure/sale/redemption; execution necessarily operates on the situs property. |
| Specific performance | **Specific Relief Act, 1963, Ss. 10, 16, 20** | Determines whether possession must be claimed along with conveyance — which in turn determines whether the proviso to S.16 survives. |
| Attachment before judgment | **Order XXXVIII Rule 5** | Attachment of immovable property is effected by the court within whose limits it lies (see also O.XXI R.54). |
| Registration of the decree | **Registration Act, 1908, S. 17(2)(vi)** | A decree comprising immovable property not the subject-matter of the suit requires registration — relevant when a proviso-forum decree touches land elsewhere. |

**Limitation periods commonly engaged.**

| Relief | Article, Limitation Act 1963 | Period | Runs from |
|---|---|---|---|
| Possession based on title | **Art. 65** | 12 years | When possession of the defendant becomes adverse to the plaintiff |
| Possession on dispossession (settled possession) | **Art. 64** | 12 years | Date of dispossession |
| Possession under Specific Relief Act S.6 | S.6(2) SRA | **6 months** | Date of dispossession |
| Specific performance | **Art. 54** | 3 years | Date fixed for performance, or when the plaintiff has notice that performance is refused |
| Redemption of mortgage | **Art. 61(a)** | 30 years | When the right to redeem accrues |
| Foreclosure | **Art. 63** | 30 years | When the money secured becomes due |

::: danger Fatal error — Section 6 Specific Relief Act
A suit under Section 6 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963 for restoration of possession must be filed within **six months** of dispossession, no appeal or review lies against the order, and no such suit lies against the Government. Advocates who reach for Article 64's twelve years and discover Section 6 was the real remedy have lost an irreplaceable summary route.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Harshad Chiman Lal Modi v. DLF Universal Ltd.**, (2005) 5 SCC 791 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* Suit for specific performance of an agreement for a plot at Gurgaon, instituted at Delhi where the defendant carried on business; the decree also involved delivery of possession.
**Ratio decidendi:** Section 16 mandates institution at the situs. The proviso is a narrow exception operating only where the relief sought can be obtained **entirely** through the defendant's personal obedience. Where the plaintiff seeks possession of the immovable property, the relief cannot be obtained by personal obedience alone, the proviso is inapplicable, and the situs court alone has jurisdiction. Territorial jurisdiction over the subject matter in such a case is not curable by consent.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"On Harshad Chiman Lal Modi, the moment a prayer for delivery of possession is added, the proviso to Section 16 is displaced and only the court within whose local limits the property is situate can try the suit."
:::

**Adcon Electronics Pvt. Ltd. v. Daulat**, (2001) 7 SCC 698 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* A suit for specific performance simpliciter, without a prayer for possession, instituted outside the situs.
**Ratio decidendi:** A suit for specific performance **simpliciter** is not a suit "for land" or for a right to or interest in immovable property in the strict sense; the relief is founded on the personal obligation of the defendant to execute the conveyance, and it falls within the proviso to Section 16. The distinction between specific performance simpliciter and specific performance with possession is decisive.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Adcon Electronics establishes that specific performance simpliciter, unaccompanied by a prayer for possession, is a personal action falling within the proviso to Section 16 — my client's choice of forum is therefore lawful."
:::

::: info The Adcon–Harshad pair
These two decisions are the two halves of a single rule and should always be cited together. Adcon tells you when the proviso is available; Harshad tells you what destroys it. The dividing line is the possession prayer.
:::

**Babu Lal v. Hazari Lal Kishori Lal**, (1982) 1 SCC 525 — 3 Judges.
*Facts:* Whether a decree for specific performance carries with it the right to possession without a separate prayer.
**Ratio decidendi:** A decree for specific performance does not automatically entitle the decree-holder to possession; but where the relief of possession is ancillary and incidental to the relief of specific performance, it may be granted in execution without a separate suit. (The interaction of this proposition with the forum question under Section 16 must be handled with care — the ancillary nature of possession in execution does not retrospectively cure a forum defect at institution.)
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The availability of possession in execution as an ancillary relief does not convert a situs-bound suit into a personal one at the stage of institution."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Bench strength and the precise ratio of Babu Lal, and the subsequent treatment of the Adcon line of authority, should be verified in the official reports before reliance. This area has attracted considerable later High Court elaboration.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**Drafting traps.**

1. **The reflex possession prayer.** Discussed above — the single most expensive drafting habit in this chapter.
2. **Pleading the proviso in the body but not in the cause-of-action paragraph.** Order VII Rule 1(e) requires the facts constituting the cause of action and *when it arose*; Rule 1(f) requires the facts showing that the court has jurisdiction. Your jurisdiction paragraph must state, in terms: the defendant resides / carries on business within these limits, and the entire relief claimed is obtainable through his personal obedience.
3. **Using Order VII Rule 11 for a territorial objection.** Rule 11 rejects; Rule 10 returns. Rejection is far more drastic and is not the remedy for want of territorial jurisdiction. Ask for return, in the alternative to rejection, and never the other way round.
4. **Forgetting the Section 17 proviso.** Choosing the convenient court where a portion of the property lies, without checking that the chosen court is competent for the *whole* claim's value.
5. **Failing to move under Section 18(1) at the threshold** where the boundary is genuinely disputed. It costs one paragraph at institution and saves the decree at appeal.

**Strategic timing.**

- Territorial objections are **Section 21 objections** — they must be taken in the court of first instance, at the earliest possible opportunity, and before settlement of issues. Raise them in the written statement itself as preliminary objection No. 1, and simultaneously move under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a).
- Do not wait for evidence. The right to object is spent once issues are settled, whatever the merits.
- Conversely, as plaintiff, **push for early settlement of issues**. Every day the defendant delays his territorial objection strengthens your Section 21(1) answer.

**Model jurisdiction paragraph — proviso to Section 16.**

> That the Defendant actually and voluntarily resides at, and carries on business from, [address], which falls within the local limits of the ordinary original civil jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court. The relief claimed in this suit is the specific performance of the Agreement to Sell dated [date] by the execution and registration of a conveyance in favour of the Plaintiff. The Plaintiff is, and has since [date] been, in lawful possession of the suit property, and no relief by way of delivery of possession is claimed or required. The entire relief sought in this suit is therefore capable of being obtained through the personal obedience of the Defendant, and this Hon'ble Court has jurisdiction to entertain, try and dispose of this suit by virtue of the proviso to Section 16 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908.

**Model prayer checklist — application under Section 18(1).**

> **(a)** be pleased to record a statement under Section 18(1) CPC that there is ground for the alleged uncertainty as to the local limits within which the suit property is situate;
> **(b)** be pleased to proceed to entertain and dispose of the present suit notwithstanding such uncertainty;
> **(c)** in the alternative, be pleased to issue a commission under Order XXVI Rule 9 CPC for local investigation and demarcation of the suit property.

## Section 19 — Suits for compensation for wrongs to person or movables

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **19. Suits for compensation for wrongs to person or movables.** — Where a suit is for compensation for wrong done to the person or to movable property, if the wrong was done within the local limits of the jurisdiction of one Court and the defendant resides, or carries on business, or personally works for gain, within the local limits of the jurisdiction of another Court, the suit may be instituted at the option of the plaintiff in either of the said Courts.
>
> ***Illustrations***
> **(a)** A, residing in Delhi, beats B in Calcutta. B may sue A either in Calcutta or in Delhi.
> **(b)** A, residing in Delhi, publishes in Calcutta statements defamatory of B. B may sue A either in Calcutta or in Delhi.

**Statutory mechanics.**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "compensation for **wrong** done" | Defining phrase | Confined to **torts**. Section 19 does not apply to breach of contract, however wrongful; that is Section 20 territory. |
| "to the **person** or to **movable property**" | Subject-matter limiter | Assault, defamation, malicious prosecution, false imprisonment; conversion, detinue, damage to goods. Wrongs to *immovable* property go to Section 16(e). |
| "the suit **may** be instituted **at the option of the plaintiff**" | **Enabling / permissive** | A true election. Neither forum is superior; the defendant cannot compel a transfer merely because the other forum is more convenient (though S.22/S.24 remain available). |
| "in **either** of the said Courts" | Binary, not open-ended | Only two candidate fora: the place of the wrong, and the place of the defendant's residence/business/personal work for gain. |

**Condition precedent.** Section 19 is engaged only where the two places are **different**. If the wrong was done where the defendant resides, there is no election to make and the section is otiose.

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — Why torts get an election.** A tortfeasor should not be able to insulate himself by committing the wrong far from home; equally, a plaintiff should not be forced to chase a defendant across the country. The Code therefore gives the victim the choice.

**Sub-module B — "Wrong done to the person".** Includes physical wrongs (assault, battery, false imprisonment) and wrongs to reputation and dignity (defamation, malicious prosecution). It does **not** extend to purely economic torts unconnected with person or movables — those are assessed under Section 20.

**Sub-module C — Where is a defamation "done"?** In the tort of libel, publication is the gist of the action. The wrong is done wherever the defamatory matter is **published** — that is, communicated to a third person. A single article circulated in five States is a wrong done in five places, giving the plaintiff five candidate fora under Section 19, plus the defendant's own. This is why defamation forum-shopping is endemic and why Sections 22 to 25 matter so much in this class of case.

**Sub-module D — Section 19 versus Section 20.** Section 19 is a *specific* provision for torts; Section 20 is the *residuary*. Where Section 19 applies, it is the governing provision, but note that its two options substantially overlap with Section 20's — "where the cause of action arises" (S.20(c)) will usually be the place of the wrong. The practical significance of Section 19 lies in its clarity, and in its express confirmation that the plaintiff, not the defendant, holds the election.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — the multi-State publication.** A newspaper published from Delhi, circulating in Delhi, Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra and Karnataka, carries a defamatory article about P, who lives in Bengaluru. The publisher's registered office and place of business is Delhi.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P sues in Bengaluru, pleading that the newspaper is circulated and read within those local limits and that publication — and therefore the wrong — occurred there. He pleads specific facts of circulation: named subscribers, distribution figures, the local edition.
- **Shield (defendant):** D cannot deny P's election outright, but attacks the *factual foundation*: he denies circulation within the local limits, denies that any identified third person within those limits read the article, and pleads that the mere accessibility of an online edition does not constitute publication within the forum. Failing that, D moves under Section 22 (if the suit could have been filed in more than one court) or applies to the High Court under Section 24, or to the Supreme Court under Section 25 if the competing proceedings are in different States.

**Illustration 2 — conversion of goods.** D, resident and trading at Kanpur, wrongfully seizes and sells P's consignment of machinery at Surat.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P elects Surat — the place of the wrong — where his witnesses (the transporter, the godown keeper, the local purchaser) are available and where the goods can be traced.
- **Shield (defendant):** D pleads that the seizure was under a contractual lien, thereby recharacterising the claim as one in contract rather than tort, which would displace Section 19 and throw the forum question back to Section 20. This recharacterisation argument is the standard defensive move against a Section 19 election.

::: warning Drafting Trap — pleading the wrong as a "wrong"
If your plaint pleads a breach of contract and then adds "and the said conduct is also wrongful and tortious", you have not converted a contract claim into a tort claim. The court looks at the substance of the pleading and the source of the duty breached. A forum founded on Section 19 collapses the moment the court holds the real claim to be contractual.
:::

**Illustration 3 — malicious prosecution.** D, residing at Jaipur, sets the criminal law in motion against P before a magistrate at Ajmer; P is acquitted and sues for malicious prosecution.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** The wrong is done where the prosecution was instituted and continued — Ajmer. P may sue at Ajmer or at Jaipur, at his option.
- **Shield (defendant):** D contends that the essential ingredient of *termination in the plaintiff's favour* occurred at Ajmer and that the complaint was lodged at Jaipur, splitting the *situs* of the wrong; and separately moves under Section 24 for transfer on the ground of convenience of witnesses, all of whom are at Jaipur.

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Kusum Ingots & Alloys Ltd. v. Union of India**, (2004) 6 SCC 254 — 3 Judges.
*Facts:* Whether the situs of a legislative or executive act confers jurisdiction, in the context of the "cause of action" doctrine under Article 226(2).
**Ratio decidendi:** The court laid down that a *part* of the cause of action arising within a forum confers jurisdiction, but the doctrine of *forum conveniens* permits the court to decline to exercise it. Though decided in the writ context, the reasoning on the constituent facts of a cause of action is regularly applied to Sections 19 and 20.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Even where a fraction of the cause of action arises within these limits, this Hon'ble Court retains the discretion, on forum conveniens principles, to decline to exercise jurisdiction."
:::

**Dhodha House v. S.K. Maingi**, (2006) 9 SCC 41 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* Forum for passing-off and infringement actions; whether availability of goods in a territory founds jurisdiction.
**Ratio decidendi:** The mere possibility that goods might be sold, or an advertisement might be seen, within a jurisdiction does not by itself constitute a part of the cause of action arising there; there must be a factual foundation pleaded and proved.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A bare assertion of circulation or availability within the forum, unsupported by pleaded particulars, cannot found territorial jurisdiction."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Kusum Ingots is a writ-jurisdiction decision applied by analogy; Dhodha House arises in the intellectual-property context and has been considered in later decisions. Verify the reports, the bench strength, and subsequent treatment before placing either before a court on a pure Section 19 point.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**Drafting traps.**

1. **Asserting circulation without particulars.** "The said defamatory publication was widely circulated within the jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court" is a conclusion, not a fact. Plead named readers, distribution figures, the local edition, the date.
2. **Mixing contract and tort in one undifferentiated narrative.** If the forum depends on Section 19, keep the tortious cause of action separately and distinctly pleaded, with its own paragraph identifying the duty, the breach, and the damage.
3. **Missing the one-year limitation.** See the danger box above.
4. **Claiming unliquidated damages without a valuation basis.** In tort suits the plaintiff must still value the claim for jurisdiction and pay court fee; an arbitrary round figure invites objection.

**Strategic timing.**

- As **plaintiff**, elect early and irrevocably. Filing in two fora invites Section 10 (*res sub judice*) and a Section 22 or Section 25 transfer application against you.
- As **defendant**, your Section 19 forum objection must be taken at the earliest opportunity under Section 21; but where the forum is lawful and merely inconvenient, the objection is misconceived — move instead under Section 22 (before settlement of issues) or Section 24.

**Model prayer checklist — plaint for defamation.**

> **(a)** decree a sum of ₹[amount] together with interest at [rate]% per annum from the date of publication till realisation, by way of compensation for the defamatory publication dated [date];
> **(b)** a decree of permanent injunction restraining the Defendants, their servants and agents, from further publishing, republishing, circulating or disseminating the said defamatory imputations or any imputation of like effect;
> **(c)** a mandatory injunction directing the Defendants to publish an unconditional apology and retraction with the same prominence, in the same publication, within [number] days;
> **(d)** a direction for delivery up and destruction of all unsold copies containing the impugned publication;
> **(e)** costs of and incidental to the suit; and
> **(f)** such further or other relief as the nature and circumstances of the case may require.

## Section 20 — Other suits to be instituted where defendants reside or cause of action arises

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **20. Other suits to be instituted where defendants reside or cause of action arises.** — Subject to the limitations aforesaid, every suit shall be instituted in a Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction —
> **(a)** the defendant, or each of the defendants where there are more than one, at the time of the commencement of the suit, actually and voluntarily resides, or carries on business, or personally works for gain; or
> **(b)** any of the defendants, where there are more than one, at the time of the commencement of the suit, actually and voluntarily resides, or carries on business, or personally works for gain, provided that in such case either the leave of the Court is given, or the defendants who do not reside, or carry on business, or personally work for gain, as aforesaid, acquiesce in such institution; or
> **(c)** the cause of action, wholly or in part, arises.
>
> ***Explanation.*** — A corporation shall be deemed to carry on business at its sole or principal office in India or, in respect of any cause of action arising at any place where it has also a subordinate office, at such place.

**Statutory mechanics.**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "**Subject to the limitations aforesaid**" | Subordinating clause | Section 20 is the **residuary**. It yields to Sections 15 to 19. If the suit is one for immovable property, Section 16 governs and Section 20 is irrelevant. |
| "every suit **shall** be instituted in a Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction — (a) … **or** (b) … **or** (c)" | Mandatory frame, **disjunctive** content | The obligation is mandatory, but the three limbs are alternatives. The plaintiff elects among them. |
| "**actually and voluntarily** resides" | Double qualifier | Excludes both fictional residence (a mere postal address) and involuntary residence (imprisonment, hospitalisation, forced posting). |
| "at the **time of the commencement of the suit**" | Temporal fixing | Jurisdiction is tested on the date of institution. A defendant who moves house the following week cannot unseat the forum. |
| Clause (b) proviso — "**either the leave of the Court is given, or** the [other] defendants … **acquiesce**" | **Condition precedent** | Where you sue several defendants and only one is within the limits, you must obtain leave *before or at institution*, or plead acquiescence. This is routinely forgotten. |
| "the cause of action, **wholly or in part**, arises" | Deliberately wide | A *fraction* suffices. This is the busiest limb in commercial litigation. |
| **Explanation** (corporations) | Deeming provision | Principal office always works. A subordinate office works **only** if the cause of action arose at that place. |

**The Explanation is narrower than it looks.** Read carefully, it gives two rules, not one:

1. A corporation is deemed to carry on business at its **sole or principal office in India** — always, for any cause of action.
2. A corporation is deemed to carry on business at a **subordinate office** — but only "in respect of any cause of action arising at any place where it has also a subordinate office".

So the mere existence of a branch within the forum does not confer jurisdiction. The cause of action must have arisen at that branch.

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — "Actually and voluntarily resides".** Residence means something more settled than presence and something less permanent than domicile. The test is a real, physical, habitual abode, chosen freely. A person may have more than one residence; he may not have a merely notional one. A defendant who maintains a locked flat he never occupies does not "actually" reside there; a defendant confined in prison within the limits does not reside there "voluntarily".

**Sub-module B — "Carries on business".** Three ingredients are usually required: (i) a real, substantial and systematic course of business activity, not an isolated transaction; (ii) carried on at a fixed place within the limits; and (iii) carried on by the defendant himself or through an agent whose acts bind him. An agent who merely canvasses orders subject to acceptance elsewhere does not make his principal carry on business at the place of canvassing.

**Sub-module C — "Personally works for gain".** Directed at the employed and the self-employed who have no "business" as such — a salaried manager, a practising professional, a labourer. The word **personally** excludes work done through others.

**Sub-module D — Clause (b) and the leave requirement.** This is the most-litigated procedural trap in Section 20. Where there are several defendants and only one of them satisfies the residence/business test within the forum, the plaint is maintainable **only if**:

- the court's **leave** has been obtained (ordinarily applied for in the plaint itself or by a separate application at presentation), **or**
- the non-resident defendants **acquiesce** — that is, submit without objection.

The consequence of omitting leave is not automatically fatal, because Section 21 forgives place-of-suing defects; but a defendant who objects promptly, before settlement of issues, and shows prejudice, will succeed. The cure is trivial and the omission is inexcusable: **ask for leave in the plaint**.

**Sub-module E — "Cause of action, wholly or in part".** The cause of action is the bundle of *material facts* which the plaintiff must plead and prove to obtain the decree — not the evidence by which he proves them, and not facts that are merely incidental. Contrast:

| Fact | Part of the cause of action? |
|---|---|
| Place where the contract was negotiated and concluded | **Yes** |
| Place where the contract was to be performed | **Yes** |
| Place where the goods were delivered or rejected | **Yes** |
| Place where the payment was to be made | **Yes** |
| Place where the breach occurred | **Yes** |
| Place where the plaintiff resides and "suffered loss" | **Ordinarily no** — mere residence of the plaintiff is not a constituent fact |
| Place from which a notice of demand was posted by the plaintiff | **Ordinarily no** |
| Place where the plaintiff's bank is situated | **Ordinarily no**, unless payment was contractually to be made there |

**Sub-module F — Ouster clauses and exclusive jurisdiction agreements.** Where two or more courts have jurisdiction under Section 20, the parties may by contract agree that only one of them shall be resorted to. Such a clause is **not** hit by Section 28 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, because it does not absolutely restrain legal proceedings — it merely selects among competent fora. But the converse is fundamental: **parties cannot by agreement confer jurisdiction on a court that has none.** A clause naming a court where neither defendant resides and no part of the cause of action arose is void and inoperative.

The construction rule matters: where the clause uses words of exclusivity — "only", "alone", "exclusively", "and no other court" — the ouster is clear. Where it merely says "the courts at Mumbai shall have jurisdiction", the *expressio unius* maxim is generally applied to read in exclusivity, but the point remains arguable and turns on the whole contract.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — the supply contract with a branch office.** P, a Pune manufacturer, sues D Ltd., whose registered office is at Chennai and which has a branch at Pune. The purchase order was placed by the Pune branch, goods were delivered at Pune, and payment was to be made at Pune. P sues at Pune.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P pleads two independent limbs. First, under the Explanation, D Ltd. is deemed to carry on business at Pune because it has a subordinate office there *and* the cause of action arose at that place. Second, and independently, under clause (c), the entire cause of action — order, delivery, breach of payment obligation — arose within these limits.
- **Shield (defendant):** D pleads the exclusive jurisdiction clause in its standard terms naming Chennai; argues that the purchase order was accepted at Chennai and that acceptance, not offer, completes the contract; and denies that the Pune office had authority to conclude, as opposed to forward, the order.

**Illustration 2 — the ouster clause naming a stranger court.** A loan agreement between a Kolkata lender and a Kolkata borrower, performed entirely in Kolkata, provides that "the Courts at New Delhi shall have exclusive jurisdiction". The lender sues at New Delhi.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** Relies on the clause and the parties' consent.
- **Shield (defendant):** The clause is inoperative. Consent cannot confer territorial jurisdiction where the Code confers none: no defendant resides or carries on business at Delhi and no part of the cause of action arose there. The Delhi court must return the plaint under Order VII Rule 10. **This is the one situation where a jurisdiction clause simply fails.**

**Illustration 3 — multiple defendants, one within the forum.** P sues three guarantors: D1 at Lucknow, D2 at Indore, D3 at Nagpur. The cause of action arose at Indore. P files at Lucknow.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P must invoke clause (b) — one defendant (D1) resides within the limits — and must apply for **leave** under the proviso, pleading why it is convenient and just to sue all three at Lucknow.
- **Shield (defendant):** D2 and D3 point to the absence of leave, deny acquiescence, and object at the first hearing. They add that the cause of action arose wholly at Indore, so clause (c) is unavailable to the plaintiff. Without leave, and with a prompt objection plus demonstrated prejudice (three sets of witnesses displaced to a distant forum), the Section 21 shield fails the plaintiff.

::: warning Drafting Trap — the missing leave application
When suing multiple defendants where only one is within the local limits, insert an express paragraph: *"Only Defendant No. 1 resides within the local limits of the jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court. The Plaintiff therefore seeks the leave of this Hon'ble Court under clause (b) of Section 20 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 to institute the present suit against all the Defendants in this Hon'ble Court."* Then carry it into the prayer. It costs three lines and forecloses an entire line of defence.
:::

**Illustration 4 — the plaintiff's own residence.** P, resident at Chandigarh, sues D of Hyderabad on a contract negotiated, concluded and performed at Hyderabad. P files at Chandigarh, pleading that he received the repudiation letter at Chandigarh and suffered loss there.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** Argues that receipt of the repudiation is the moment of breach and it was received within the forum.
- **Shield (defendant):** The receipt of a communication by the plaintiff at his own residence is not, without more, a constituent fact of the cause of action; nor is the situs of the plaintiff's loss. This is the classic attempt to manufacture a home forum, and it ordinarily fails.

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**A.B.C. Laminart Pvt. Ltd. v. A.P. Agencies, Salem**, (1989) 2 SCC 163 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* A contract for supply of metallic yarn contained a clause that "any dispute arising out of this sale shall be subject to Kaira jurisdiction"; suit was filed at Salem.
**Ratio decidendi:** Where more than one court has jurisdiction under the Code, parties may by agreement confine the dispute to one of them, and such an agreement is not hit by Section 28 of the Contract Act. But the ouster must be clear: where the clause does not use words such as "alone", "only", "exclusively", the maxim *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* may be applied, though the intention must be gathered from the whole clause. Crucially, the court also held that a cause of action for breach may arise at the place where the goods were to be delivered and payment made.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Under A.B.C. Laminart, an ouster clause operates only where the chosen court is itself one of the courts having jurisdiction under the Code; and absent words of exclusivity, the ouster of the other competent forum is not to be readily inferred."
:::

**Swastik Gases Pvt. Ltd. v. Indian Oil Corporation Ltd.**, (2013) 9 SCC 32 — 3 Judges.
*Facts:* An agreement provided that "the agreement shall be subject to jurisdiction of the courts at Kolkata"; suit filed at Jaipur.
**Ratio decidendi:** The absence of words like "alone", "only" or "exclusive" is **not decisive**. Where a jurisdiction clause names a particular court and that court has jurisdiction under the Code, the intendment is that the parties intended to exclude all other fora; otherwise the clause would be rendered meaningless. *Expressio unius est exclusio alterius* applies.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Swastik Gases settles that a jurisdiction clause naming one competent court excludes the others even without the word 'exclusively' — the clause must be given meaning, not rendered otiose."
:::

**Patel Roadways Ltd. v. Prasad Trading Co.**, (1991) 4 SCC 270 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* A corporation with its principal office in one State and a subordinate office in another, where the cause of action arose.
**Ratio decidendi:** On the true construction of the Explanation to Section 20, where a corporation has a subordinate office at the place where the cause of action arises, it **cannot** be heard to say that it is suable only at its principal office. The purpose of the Explanation is to protect the plaintiff from being compelled to travel to the corporation's head office; it is not a shield for the corporation.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Patel Roadways is decisive: where the cause of action arose at the branch, the corporation is suable at the branch, and the location of its registered office is beside the point."
:::

**Indian Performing Rights Society Ltd. v. Sanjay Dalia**, (2015) 10 SCC 161 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* The plaintiff sought to sue at its principal place of business under the special jurisdiction provisions of the Copyright and Trade Marks Acts, though the cause of action arose where it had a branch.
**Ratio decidendi:** The additional forum conferred on a plaintiff by special statutes is not a licence for forum-shopping. Where the plaintiff has a branch office at the place where the cause of action arose, it must sue there and cannot drag the defendant to the place of its head office. The provisions must be read to avoid absurdity and abuse.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Sanjay Dalia forecloses forum-shopping by a plaintiff who has a branch at the situs of the cause of action, whatever the location of its head office."
:::

**Kusum Ingots & Alloys Ltd. v. Union of India**, (2004) 6 SCC 254 — 3 Judges.
**Ratio decidendi (as applied here):** Only those facts which are *material* and which the plaintiff must prove to succeed form part of the cause of action; and even where a part of the cause of action arises within the forum, the court retains a discretion on *forum conveniens* grounds.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A fact which is merely incidental, and which the plaintiff need not prove to obtain the decree, does not form part of the cause of action and cannot found jurisdiction."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
The relationship between A.B.C. Laminart and Swastik Gases on the necessity of exclusionary words has been the subject of continued elaboration, as has the reach of Sanjay Dalia. Confirm the citations, bench strength and current status of each decision in the official reports before reliance.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**Drafting traps that cause rejection or return.**

1. **The conclusory jurisdiction paragraph.** "This Hon'ble Court has jurisdiction to try the present suit" satisfies nothing. Order VII Rule 1(f) demands *facts*. Plead the address of the defendant within the limits, or the specific constituent facts of the cause of action that arose within them, with dates.
2. **Omitting leave under clause (b).** Discussed above. Three lines in the plaint, three years of litigation saved.
3. **Relying on a subordinate office alone.** The Explanation requires the cause of action to have arisen at that office. A branch that had nothing to do with the transaction confers nothing.
4. **Pleading a jurisdiction clause naming a court that has no jurisdiction under the Code.** Void and unhelpful; check first that the named court independently qualifies under Section 20.
5. **Ignoring Section 80 CPC.** Where the Government or a public officer acting in official capacity is a defendant, the two-month notice is a condition precedent; the plaint must either plead compliance and annex the notice, or plead and obtain leave under Section 80(2).
6. **Ignoring Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act.** See the danger box above.
7. **Pleading residence as at some past date.** The test is residence "at the time of the commencement of the suit". Plead present-tense facts.

**Strategic timing.**

| Move | When it must be made | Provision |
|---|---|---|
| Plaintiff's leave application under clause (b) | At presentation of the plaint | S. 20(b) proviso |
| Defendant's territorial objection | In the written statement, and in any event **at the earliest possible opportunity and before settlement of issues** | S. 21(1) |
| Application to try jurisdiction as a preliminary issue | Immediately after the written statement, before evidence | O. XIV R. 2(2)(a) |
| Application for return of plaint | With, or immediately after, the written statement | O. VII R. 10 |
| Application under S. 8 of the Arbitration Act | Not later than the date of submitting the first statement on the substance of the dispute | Arbitration Act, S. 8 |
| Transfer application by defendant where two or more courts are competent | After notice to the defendant and **before settlement of issues** | S. 22 |

::: tip The single most useful habit in Section 20 practice
Draft the jurisdiction paragraph **last**, after the prayer clause is final. The forum depends on the reliefs claimed, and the reliefs are often revised late. A jurisdiction paragraph written before the prayer was settled is a jurisdiction paragraph that no longer matches the suit.
:::

**Model jurisdiction paragraph — cause of action limb.**

> That the Agreement dated [date] was negotiated and executed at [place], which is within the local limits of the ordinary original civil jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court; the goods thereunder were delivered by the Plaintiff to the Defendant at [place], within the said limits; the consideration was, in terms of clause [x] of the said Agreement, payable by the Defendant to the Plaintiff at [place], within the said limits; and the Defendant's failure and refusal to make payment, being the breach complained of, occurred within the said limits. The cause of action has therefore arisen, wholly and in any event in part, within the local limits of the jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court, and this Hon'ble Court has jurisdiction to entertain, try and dispose of the present suit under Section 20(c) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908.

**Model prayer clause — leave under Section 20(b).**

> **(a)** be pleased to grant leave to the Plaintiff under clause (b) of Section 20 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 to institute the present suit against all the Defendants in this Hon'ble Court, Defendant No. 1 alone residing and carrying on business within the local limits of the jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court;

## Sections 21 & 21A — Objections to jurisdiction

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **21. Objections to jurisdiction.** — **(1)** No objection as to the place of suing shall be allowed by any Appellate or Revisional Court unless such objection was taken in the Court of first instance at the earliest possible opportunity and in all cases where issues are settled at or before such settlement, and unless there has been a consequent failure of justice.
> **(2)** No objection as to the competence of a Court with reference to the pecuniary limits of its jurisdiction shall be allowed by any Appellate or Revisional Court unless such objection was taken in the Court of first instance at the earliest possible opportunity, and in all cases where issues are settled, at or before such settlement, and unless there has been a consequent failure of justice.
> **(3)** No objection as to the competence of the executing Court with reference to the local limits of its jurisdiction shall be allowed by any Appellate or Revisional Court unless such objection was taken in the executing Court at the earliest possible opportunity, and unless there has been a consequent failure of justice.

> **21A. Bar on suit to set aside decree on objection as to place of suing.** — No suit shall lie challenging the validity of a decree passed in a former suit between the same parties, or between the parties under whom they or any of them claim, litigating under the same title, on any ground based on an objection as to the place of suing.
>
> ***Explanation.*** — The expression "former suit" means a suit which has been decided prior to the decision in the suit in which the validity of the decree is questioned, whether or not the previously decided suit was instituted prior to the suit in which the validity of such decree is questioned.

**Statutory mechanics of Section 21.**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "**No** objection … **shall be allowed**" | **Prohibitory**, double negative | The appellate court is *forbidden* to entertain the objection unless the conditions are met. The burden lies entirely on the objector. |
| "by any **Appellate or Revisional** Court" | Forum limiter | Section 21 does not stop the trial court from entertaining a prompt objection. It bars the *belated* objection at the appellate stage. |
| "as to the **place of suing**" (sub-s. 1) | Subject-matter of the bar | Territorial jurisdiction only — Sections 16 to 20. |
| "**pecuniary** limits" (sub-s. 2) | Subject-matter of the bar | Pecuniary competence — Section 15. |
| "**local limits** of the **executing** Court" (sub-s. 3) | Subject-matter of the bar | Territorial competence at the execution stage — Sections 38, 39. |
| "at the **earliest possible opportunity**" | **Condition precedent (1)** | A pure question of promptness, judged on the record. |
| "and **in all cases where issues are settled, at or before such settlement**" | **Condition precedent (2) — an absolute ceiling** | Even an otherwise "prompt" objection is too late if issues have been settled. Note sub-section (3) has no issue-settlement clause — execution proceedings have no issues. |
| "**and** unless there has been a **consequent failure of justice**" | **Condition precedent (3)** | Conjunctive. Promptness alone is not enough. The objector must show prejudice *caused by* the wrong forum. |

**Three cumulative conditions.** The word "and" appears twice. The objector must establish, cumulatively: **(i)** the objection was taken in the court of first instance; **(ii)** at the earliest possible opportunity and, where issues were settled, at or before settlement; **and (iii)** a consequent failure of justice. Failure on any one limb defeats the objection.

**What Section 21 conspicuously does *not* cover.** Jurisdiction over the **subject matter**. Section 21 lists three things — place of suing, pecuniary limits, and the executing court's local limits. Subject-matter competence is absent by deliberate omission. A decree passed by a court having no jurisdiction over the subject matter is a **nullity**, may be questioned at any stage, in any proceeding, including in execution and in collateral proceedings, and is not saved by consent, waiver or acquiescence.

**Statutory mechanics of Section 21A.** Inserted by the Amendment Act of 1976. It is a **substantive bar on a separate suit**, complementing the procedural bar in Section 21. The three conditions are: (i) a decree in a *former suit*; (ii) between the same parties or their privies; (iii) litigating under the same title. The Explanation makes the section date-agnostic — what matters is which suit was *decided* first, not which was *instituted* first. This mirrors Explanation I to Section 11.

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — The policy.** A litigant should not be permitted to take his chance on the merits, lose, and then spring a technical forum objection to wipe out years of trial. Section 21 converts territorial and pecuniary jurisdiction from *conditions of validity* into *conditions of regularity* — irregularities which must be objected to promptly or are treated as waived.

**Sub-module B — The three-tier hierarchy of jurisdictional defects.** This is the organising idea of the whole chapter:

| Tier | Type of defect | Effect of the decree | Curable? | When objectionable |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Subject-matter** — the court has no power over this class of dispute at all | **Nullity**; void; can be ignored | **Never** | At any stage, in any proceeding, including execution and collateral attack |
| **2** | **Pecuniary** — suit filed in a court of *lower* grade than competent | **Nullity** as to that court's power, but Section 21(2) and Section 11 of the Suits Valuation Act intervene on over/under-valuation | Substantially, yes | Must satisfy S.21(2) if raised in appeal |
| **3** | **Territorial (place of suing)** and **pecuniary (higher grade)** | **Valid and binding** unless S.21 conditions satisfied | **Yes** | Court of first instance, at the earliest opportunity, before settlement of issues |

**Sub-module C — "At the earliest possible opportunity".** Not a fixed date; a standard measured against the record. In practice:

- If the defect is apparent on the face of the plaint, the earliest opportunity is the **first appearance** or the **written statement**.
- If the defect emerges only from facts pleaded in the written statement or discovered later, the earliest opportunity is **immediately upon discovery**, and the party must move at once.
- A defendant who files a written statement on the merits without a preliminary objection, and raises the point only at final arguments, has failed this limb absolutely.

**Sub-module D — The absolute ceiling: "at or before such settlement".** This is the harder of the two temporal limbs and it is *not* co-extensive with "earliest possible opportunity". Even if a defendant can honestly say he raised it as soon as he could, if issues have already been settled the door is shut. The rationale: once issues are framed, the parties and the court have committed to a trial plan; forum objections thereafter waste judicial time.

**Sub-module E — "Consequent failure of justice" — the limb that defeats most objections.** This is a *causation* requirement, and it is the one most often overlooked. The objector must show not merely that the forum was wrong, but that being in the wrong forum **caused** a miscarriage of justice. Examples of what is and is not enough:

| Pleaded prejudice | Sufficient? |
|---|---|
| "The suit ought to have been filed at Nagpur, not Nashik" | **No** — that is the defect, not the prejudice |
| "I was put to expense in travelling to a distant forum" | **Ordinarily no** — inconvenience is not a failure of justice; the remedy was a transfer application |
| "My material witnesses could not be produced because they were beyond the process of that court and I was thereby prevented from proving my defence" | **Yes, if proved** |
| "The wrong forum applied a different State amendment / rent legislation which does not apply to the property, to my detriment" | **Yes** — a real, substantive detriment |
| "The suit was tried by a court of higher grade, and I therefore lost my first appeal to the District Judge and had to appeal to the High Court at far greater cost" | **Arguable and frequently pressed** — plead it specifically |

**Sub-module F — Section 21(3) and execution.** Sub-section (3) protects execution proceedings taken in a court whose *local limits* did not, strictly, cover the matter. Note two differences from sub-sections (1) and (2): there is no "settlement of issues" ceiling (execution has no issues), and the objection must be taken *in the executing court*, not the trial court. But **beware**: Section 21(3) covers only the *local limits* of the executing court. It does not save an execution taken by a court to which the decree was never transferred under Section 39, nor an execution of a decree that is itself a nullity — those objections survive under Section 47.

**Sub-module G — Section 21A and the closing of the last door.** Before 1976, a defeated party might file a fresh suit to have the earlier decree declared a nullity for want of territorial jurisdiction. Section 21A closes that route. Read together:

- **Section 21(1)** — you cannot raise it in appeal or revision (unless the three conditions are met).
- **Section 21A** — you cannot raise it in a fresh suit at all.
- **Section 11** — you cannot re-agitate the merits.

The combined effect is that a place-of-suing objection not taken before settlement of issues is, for all practical purposes, dead.

**Sub-module H — What Section 21A does *not* bar.** It bars a suit founded on "an objection as to the **place of suing**". It does not bar a suit to set aside a decree on the ground of **fraud** (Section 44 of the Evidence Act; the principle in *S.P. Chengalvaraya Naidu*), nor a challenge founded on want of **subject-matter** jurisdiction, nor a challenge that the decree was passed against a dead person or without service. Section 21A is narrow, and the pleading in a set-aside suit must be framed to fall outside it.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — the ambush at final arguments.** P sues D at Ludhiana on a contract performed entirely at Amritsar; D resides at Amritsar. D files a written statement contesting the merits and pleading limitation, but says nothing about jurisdiction. Issues are settled. Evidence is led over three years. At final arguments, D's counsel rises and submits that the court has no territorial jurisdiction.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** P answers with Section 21(1) in three moves. First, the objection was not taken at the earliest possible opportunity — the defect was apparent on the face of the plaint and D's own written statement disclosed his Amritsar residence. Second, and conclusively, issues were settled without any such objection, and Section 21(1) imposes an absolute ceiling at settlement. Third, D has pleaded no failure of justice whatever; he has contested the merits fully, led his evidence, and cross-examined at length.
- **Shield (defendant):** D's only escape is to recharacterise the defect as one of **subject-matter** — for instance, by contending that the dispute lies exclusively within the domain of a tribunal (a Rent Controller, a Debts Recovery Tribunal, a Consumer Commission), so that Section 9 and not Section 20 is engaged. That objection is outside Section 21 altogether and can be taken at any stage.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Section 21 is not a rule of waiver in the loose sense — it is a statutory command to the appellate court. My learned friend does not merely need a good point; he needs to have taken it before issues were settled and to have suffered a failure of justice in consequence. He has done neither."
:::

**Illustration 2 — the objection taken properly, and why it still fails.** D files a written statement with preliminary objection No. 1 on territorial jurisdiction, and simultaneously applies under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a) for a preliminary issue. The trial court wrongly rejects it and proceeds. D loses on the merits and appeals.

- **Sword (defendant, now appellant):** D has cleared the first two limbs cleanly. He must now build the third — failure of justice. He pleads that two crucial witnesses, resident at the correct forum and outside the trial court's process under Order XVI Rule 19 (the "one hundred kilometres" limit), could not be compelled to attend, that he applied for a commission under Order XXVI Rule 4 and it was refused, and that his defence of payment therefore failed for want of the very evidence the wrong forum denied him.
- **Shield (plaintiff, now respondent):** P attacks causation. He shows that D never applied for a commission, never sought summons for the witnesses, and led no material to show they were unwilling. Absent a demonstrated causal link between the forum and the evidentiary failure, the third limb collapses and the decree stands.

::: warning Drafting Trap — pleading the defect without pleading the prejudice
The overwhelming majority of Section 21 objections die on the third limb, not the first two. A memorandum of appeal that pleads "the learned trial court had no territorial jurisdiction" and stops there has pleaded only half a ground. **Always add a separate, particularised ground setting out precisely what the appellant was prevented from doing, and how the outcome would have differed.**
:::

**Illustration 3 — the collateral attack in execution.** A money decree passed at Court A is put into execution. The judgment-debtor objects under Section 47 that Court A had no territorial jurisdiction and the decree is a nullity.

- **Sword (decree-holder):** The decree-holder answers that an executing court **cannot go behind the decree**. The only nullity that an executing court may notice is one arising from inherent lack of *subject-matter* or *pecuniary* competence apparent on the face of the record. A territorial defect is precisely what Section 21(1) and Section 21A were enacted to foreclose, and the judgment-debtor never objected at trial.
- **Shield (judgment-debtor):** His only viable path is to establish that Court A lacked competence over the subject matter, or that the decree was obtained by fraud, or that it was passed against a dead person — none of which is a "place of suing" objection and none of which Section 21A bars.

**Illustration 4 — the fresh suit blocked by Section 21A.** D, having lost a partition suit at Court A, files a fresh suit at Court B for a declaration that the decree of Court A is void because the property lay outside Court A's local limits.

- **Sword (plaintiff in the second suit / D):** Contends that Section 16 is mandatory and a decree in breach is void.
- **Shield (defendant in the second suit / P):** Section 21A is a complete answer on its own terms — same parties, same title, former suit decided, ground based on an objection as to place of suing. The plaint is liable to be rejected under **Order VII Rule 11(d)** as barred by law. P should also plead Section 11 in the alternative.

::: danger Fatal error — the fresh suit that cannot survive Order VII Rule 11(d)
A suit hit by Section 21A is barred *by law* on the face of the plaint. It will not merely fail; it will be rejected at the threshold with costs, and the limitation clock on any legitimate alternative remedy will have run in the meantime. Before drafting any suit to set aside a decree, ask whether the ground is one "based on an objection as to the place of suing". If it is, the suit is not available and another remedy must be found.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Kiran Singh v. Chaman Paswan**, AIR 1954 SC 340 — Constitution Bench (5 Judges).
*Facts:* Under-valuation of a suit, with the consequence that the first appeal went to the District Court instead of the High Court; the objection was pressed at the second appellate stage.
**Ratio decidendi:** The foundational statement of the whole doctrine. A decree passed by a court without jurisdiction is a nullity and its invalidity may be set up whenever and wherever it is sought to be enforced or relied upon, even at the stage of execution and in collateral proceedings. **But** the legislature has, by Section 21 CPC and Section 11 of the Suits Valuation Act, made a deliberate departure in the case of over- and under-valuation: such objections are not to be entertained by an appellate court unless there has been a consequent failure of justice or prejudice on the merits.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Kiran Singh is the source of the rule that a nullity may be challenged anywhere and at any time — but it is equally the source of the exception that valuation and place-of-suing defects are outside that rule and require proof of prejudice."
:::

**Hira Lal Patni v. Sri Kali Nath**, AIR 1962 SC 199 — 3 Judges.
*Facts:* A party who had submitted to the jurisdiction of the Bombay High Court and taken the benefit of proceedings there later challenged that court's jurisdiction on the ground that leave under Clause 12 of the Letters Patent had not been obtained.
**Ratio decidendi:** The objection as to *local* jurisdiction of a court stands on a different footing from an objection to competence with reference to the subject matter. Competence over the subject matter goes to the root and cannot be waived; but an objection as to territorial jurisdiction, or the absence of leave to sue, **can be waived**, and where a party has consented to the jurisdiction and taken the chance of a decision in his favour, he cannot afterwards turn round and challenge it.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"On Hira Lal Patni, a party who submits to the jurisdiction and takes his chance on the merits waives any objection as to place of suing, and is precluded from resurrecting it once the result is known."
:::

**Harshad Chiman Lal Modi v. DLF Universal Ltd.**, (2005) 5 SCC 791 — 2 Judges.
**Ratio decidendi (on this point):** The court expressly restated the dividing line: objections as to territorial and pecuniary jurisdiction fall within Section 21 and are curable by waiver or acquiescence; an objection as to jurisdiction over the subject matter is a different matter altogether, cannot be waived, and consent cannot confer such jurisdiction.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The line drawn in Harshad Chiman Lal Modi is the line drawn by Section 21 itself: place and money are forgiven; subject matter never is."
:::

**Pathumma v. Kuntalan Kutty**, (1981) 3 SCC 589 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* A belated objection to territorial jurisdiction raised in appeal.
**Ratio decidendi:** All three conditions of Section 21 are cumulative and must be satisfied; the absence of a demonstrated consequent failure of justice is by itself fatal to the objection, however meritorious the underlying jurisdictional point may be.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The three requirements of Section 21 are conjunctive, and the failure-of-justice requirement is not a formality that follows from the jurisdictional error — it must be independently pleaded and proved."
:::

**Bahrein Petroleum Co. Ltd. v. P.J. Pappu**, AIR 1966 SC 634 — 3 Judges.
*Facts:* Whether an objection to territorial jurisdiction not raised at the trial could be entertained later.
**Ratio decidendi:** Independently of Section 21, the principle of waiver applies: a defendant who does not raise the objection to territorial jurisdiction at the earliest stage and allows the trial to proceed cannot be permitted to raise it subsequently, in the absence of prejudice.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Waiver operates alongside Section 21, and a defendant who allows the trial to run its course on the merits has waived the point independently of the statute."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
The bench strengths and reported citations above — in particular for Hira Lal Patni, Pathumma and Bahrein Petroleum — should be verified in the official reports (AIR / SCC) and checked for subsequent consideration. Kiran Singh remains the leading Constitution Bench authority, but its application to particular classes of defect has been refined in later decisions.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**For the defendant — the checklist that preserves the objection.**

1. **Raise it as preliminary objection No. 1 in the written statement.** Not in the middle of the pleading, not in a paragraph headed "without prejudice". First, numbered, unambiguous.
2. **Plead the prejudice in the written statement itself**, not only in appeal. Set out what the wrong forum will cost you in evidentiary terms — witnesses beyond process, records beyond reach, a different substantive law applied.
3. **File a simultaneous application under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a)** asking that jurisdiction be tried as a preliminary issue, and a parallel application under Order VII Rule 10 for return of the plaint.
4. **Press for a decision before issues are settled.** If the court defers it, record your objection to the deferral in the order sheet. A note in the order sheet is your proof of promptness three years later.
5. **If the objection is rejected, do not sit on it.** Consider revision under Section 115 where the order amounts to a jurisdictional error, and in any event carry the ground into the memorandum of appeal under Order XLI Rule 1(2).

**For the plaintiff — the checklist that defeats the objection.**

1. **Push for early settlement of issues.** Every day gained tightens the Section 21 net around the defendant.
2. **When the objection comes late, answer all three limbs separately** — not merely "it is belated". Show (i) the defect was apparent on the plaint from day one; (ii) issues were settled on [date] without objection; and (iii) no prejudice is pleaded, let alone proved.
3. **Where a fresh suit is filed to set aside your decree**, move at once under Order VII Rule 11(d) pleading Section 21A **and** Section 11 in the alternative.

**Drafting traps.**

| Trap | Consequence | Cure |
|---|---|---|
| Territorial objection buried in paragraph 24 of the written statement on the merits | Court holds it was not taken at the earliest opportunity | Preliminary objection No. 1, before the para-wise reply |
| Objection pleaded without any prejudice | Fails the third limb of S.21 | Separate particularised paragraph on failure of justice |
| Ground of appeal reading only "no territorial jurisdiction" | Incomplete ground; appellate court is *statutorily barred* from allowing it | Add a distinct ground pleading consequent failure of justice with particulars |
| Fresh suit to set aside a decree on place-of-suing grounds | Rejected under O.VII R.11(d) by force of S.21A | Frame the challenge, if genuinely available, on fraud or subject-matter incompetence |
| Treating S.21(3) as curing an untransferred execution | Execution is without authority; S.39 not complied with | Obtain transfer of the decree under S.39 before executing outside the local limits |
| Objecting to pecuniary jurisdiction of a *lower* court under S.21(2) | Misconceived — that is inherent incompetence, not a S.21 irregularity | Plead it as a nullity, raisable at any stage |

**Model preliminary objection — written statement.**

> **PRELIMINARY OBJECTION NO. 1 — WANT OF TERRITORIAL JURISDICTION**
>
> 1. That this Hon'ble Court has no territorial jurisdiction to entertain, try or dispose of the present suit. The Defendant neither resides, nor carries on business, nor personally works for gain within the local limits of the jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court, the Defendant's sole residence and place of business being at [address]. No part of the cause of action pleaded in the plaint has arisen within the said local limits: the Agreement dated [date] was executed at [place], was to be performed at [place], and the breach alleged occurred at [place], all outside the said limits.
> 2. That the averments in paragraph [x] of the plaint regarding jurisdiction are conclusory and are denied. The Plaintiff has failed to comply with Order VII Rule 1(f) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, in that the plaint discloses no facts showing that this Hon'ble Court has jurisdiction.
> 3. **That the Defendant will suffer a failure of justice if the suit is tried by this Hon'ble Court.** The Defendant's material witnesses, namely [names], reside at [place], beyond the limits prescribed by Order XVI Rule 19 of the Code, and cannot be compelled to attend before this Hon'ble Court. The books of account and original records relevant to the defence of payment are maintained at [place]. The Defendant will thereby be prevented from proving his defence of [defence], and the disposal of the suit on its merits will be prejudicially affected.
> 4. That this objection is raised at the earliest possible opportunity, being the first pleading filed by the Defendant, and well before the settlement of issues, as required by Section 21(1) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908.
> 5. That the plaint is accordingly liable to be returned under Order VII Rule 10 of the Code for presentation to the proper court, and the Defendant is filing a separate application to that effect along with an application under Order XIV Rule 2(2)(a) for the trial of the question of jurisdiction as a preliminary issue.

**Model ground of appeal — Section 21 compliant.**

> **GROUND [n]:** BECAUSE the learned Trial Court had no territorial jurisdiction to try the suit, no part of the cause of action having arisen within its local limits and the Appellant neither residing nor carrying on business therein.
>
> **GROUND [n+1]:** BECAUSE the said objection was taken by the Appellant in the Court of first instance at the earliest possible opportunity, namely as Preliminary Objection No. 1 in the written statement dated [date], and before the settlement of issues on [date], as required by Section 21(1) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, and was wrongly rejected by order dated [date].
>
> **GROUND [n+2]:** BECAUSE there has been a consequent failure of justice, in that the Appellant's witnesses [names], resident at [place], were beyond the process of the learned Trial Court under Order XVI Rule 19 of the Code; the Appellant's application dated [date] for issue of a commission under Order XXVI Rule 4 was rejected by order dated [date]; and the Appellant was in consequence prevented from proving his defence of [defence], which defence, if established, would have resulted in the dismissal of the suit.

## Sections 22 to 25 — Transfer of suits, appeals and other proceedings

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **22. Power to transfer suits which may be instituted in more than one Court.** — Where a suit may be instituted in any one of two or more Courts and is instituted in one of such Courts, any defendant, after notice to the other parties, may, at the earliest possible opportunity and in all cases where issues are settled at or before such settlement, apply to have the suit transferred to another Court, and the Court to which such application is made, after considering the objections of the other parties (if any), shall determine in which of the several Courts having jurisdiction the suit shall proceed.

> **23. To what Court application lies.** — **(1)** Where the several Courts having jurisdiction are subordinate to the same Appellate Court, an application under section 22 shall be made to the Appellate Court.
> **(2)** Where such Courts are subordinate to different Appellate Courts but to the same High Court, the application shall be made to the said High Court.
> **(3)** Where such Courts are subordinate to different High Courts, the application shall be made to the High Court within the local limits of whose jurisdiction the Court in which the suit is brought is situate.

> **24. General power of transfer and withdrawal.** — **(1)** On the application of any of the parties and after notice to the parties and after hearing such of them as desired to be heard, or of its own motion without such notice, the High Court or the District Court may at any stage —
> **(a)** transfer any suit, appeal or other proceeding pending before it for trial or disposal to any Court subordinate to it and competent to try or dispose of the same, or
> **(b)** withdraw any suit, appeal or other proceeding pending in any Court subordinate to it, and —
> **(i)** try or dispose of the same; or
> **(ii)** transfer the same for trial or disposal to any Court subordinate to it and competent to try or dispose of the same; or
> **(iii)** retransfer the same for trial or disposal to the Court from which it was withdrawn.
> **(2)** Where any suit or proceeding has been transferred or withdrawn under sub-section (1), the Court which thereafter tries such suit may, subject to any special directions in the case of an order of transfer, either retry it or proceed from the point at which it was transferred or withdrawn.
> **(3)** For the purposes of this section, — **(a)** Courts of Additional and Assistant Judges shall be deemed to be subordinate to the District Court; **(b)** "proceeding" includes a proceeding for the execution of a decree or order.
> **(4)** The Court trying any suit transferred or withdrawn under this section from a Court of Small Causes shall, for the purposes of such suit, be deemed to be a Court of Small Causes.
> **(5)** A suit or proceeding may be transferred under this section from a Court which has no jurisdiction to try it.

> **25. Power of Supreme Court to transfer suits, etc.** — **(1)** On the application of a party, and after notice to the parties, and after hearing such of them as desire to be heard, the Supreme Court may, at any stage, if satisfied that an order under this section is expedient for the ends of justice, direct that any suit, appeal or other proceeding be transferred from a High Court or other Civil Court in one State to a High Court or other Civil Court in any other State.
> **(2)** Every application under this section shall be made by a motion which shall be supported by an affidavit.
> **(3)** The Court to which such suit, appeal or other proceeding is transferred shall, subject to any special directions in the order of transfer, either retry it or proceed from the stage at which it was transferred to it.
> **(4)** In dismissing any application under this section, the Supreme Court may, if it is of opinion that the application was frivolous or vexatious, order the applicant to pay by way of compensation to any person who has opposed the application such sum, not exceeding two thousand rupees, as it considers appropriate in the circumstances of the case.
> **(5)** The law applicable to any suit, appeal or other proceeding transferred under this section shall be the law which the Court in which the suit, appeal or other proceeding was originally instituted ought to have applied to such suit, appeal or proceeding.

**Statutory mechanics — the four transfer powers compared.**

| | **S. 22** | **S. 24** | **S. 25** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Who may apply** | The **defendant** only | **Any party**; or the court **suo motu** | **Any party** |
| **Which forum decides** | The appellate court / High Court identified by **S. 23** | The **High Court or the District Court** | The **Supreme Court** |
| **Precondition** | The suit could have been instituted in **two or more** courts | None beyond the court's supervisory relationship | Transfer must be **between States** |
| **When** | Earliest possible opportunity; **absolute bar at settlement of issues** | "**at any stage**" | "**at any stage**" |
| **Notice** | To the other parties — **mandatory** before applying | Mandatory on a party's application; **dispensed with** where the court acts of its own motion | Mandatory |
| **Test** | Which of the competent courts the suit "shall proceed" in | Judicial discretion — convenience, expedience, ends of justice | "**expedient for the ends of justice**" |
| **Transfer from a court lacking jurisdiction?** | No — all candidate courts must have jurisdiction | **Yes** — expressly, by sub-s. (5) | Yes |
| **Costs for a frivolous application** | General costs power | General costs power | **Express — up to ₹2,000** under sub-s. (4) |

**Key structural observations.**

- **Section 22 is the defendant's provision alone.** The plaintiff chose the forum; he cannot be heard to ask for a transfer under Section 22. If a plaintiff wants a transfer, his route is Section 24 or Section 25.
- **Section 22 imports the Section 21 timetable verbatim** — "at the earliest possible opportunity and in all cases where issues are settled at or before such settlement". The two provisions were designed to work in tandem: Section 21 kills the *objection*, Section 22 kills the *transfer application*, and both die at settlement of issues.
- **Notice under Section 22 is a condition precedent**, not a formality. The words are "after notice to the other parties, may … apply". An application filed without prior notice is defective.
- **Section 23 is purely a routing rule.** It contains no substantive test; it merely tells you where to file the Section 22 application. Sub-section (3) is the important one in inter-High-Court situations: the application goes to the High Court where the **suit was brought**, not where the applicant wants it sent.
- **Section 24(5) is a striking and often-forgotten power.** A suit may be transferred *from a court which has no jurisdiction to try it*. This means the High Court or District Court can transfer a wrongly-instituted suit to the right court instead of forcing a return of the plaint under Order VII Rule 10 — preserving the original date of institution and, with it, limitation. This is frequently the most valuable single tool in the chapter.
- **Section 25(5) codifies the transferee court's choice of law.** On an inter-State transfer, the transferee court applies the law that the **original** court ought to have applied. A suit transferred from Kerala to Karnataka is still governed by the substantive law and State amendments applicable in Kerala. This prevents transfer from being used to change the applicable law.

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — Why a transfer power is necessary at all.** Sections 15 to 20 frequently give the plaintiff a genuine choice of forum, and the plaintiff will naturally choose the one that suits him. Where that choice is lawful, a Section 21 objection is misconceived — the court *does* have jurisdiction. The mischief is not illegality but **oppression**. Sections 22 to 25 supply the answer: leave the forum lawful, but move the case.

**Sub-module B — Section 22 and its narrow field.** Section 22 operates only in the precise situation where "a suit **may** be instituted in any one of two or more Courts". It presupposes that every candidate court has jurisdiction. If the court in which the suit was filed lacks jurisdiction, Section 22 is inapplicable — the remedy is a Section 21 objection with a return of plaint, or a Section 24 transfer under sub-section (5).

**Sub-module C — Section 24 as the workhorse.** In practice, the overwhelming majority of transfers happen under Section 24, and for good reason:

1. It may be invoked by **any** party, including the plaintiff.
2. It applies "**at any stage**" — no settlement-of-issues guillotine.
3. It covers suits, appeals **and** "other proceedings", which by sub-section (3)(b) expressly includes **execution** proceedings.
4. It permits **withdrawal** as well as transfer, so the High Court or District Court can pull a case up to itself.
5. By sub-section (5) it works even where the transferor court has no jurisdiction.

**Sub-module D — The grounds that actually succeed under Section 24.** There is no statutory list; the discretion is guided by the ends of justice. The recurring successful grounds are:

| Ground | Weight |
|---|---|
| **Balance of convenience of parties and witnesses** — where the bulk of evidence and the parties are located | High |
| **Reasonable apprehension of a fair trial being denied**, founded on objective material | High, but must be objectively supported, not a bare allegation |
| **Common questions of law and fact in two or more suits** pending in different courts — risk of conflicting decrees | Very high; the standard ground for consolidating related suits |
| **The convenience and safety of a woman litigant**, particularly in matrimonial proceedings | Very high in practice; a well-established consideration |
| **Physical incapacity, illness or indigence of a party** making attendance at the distant forum impracticable | High, on medical or financial material |
| **Avoidance of multiplicity and conflicting findings** | High |
| Mere inconvenience or added expense to the applicant | **Low — ordinarily insufficient on its own** |
| Counsel's convenience or the applicant's preference for a particular bar | **Not a ground** |
| An apprehension based on the judge having ruled against the applicant on an interlocutory application | **Not a ground** — an adverse order is not evidence of bias |

**Sub-module E — The limits of the "fair trial apprehension" ground.** An allegation that justice will not be done must rest on material capable of creating a reasonable apprehension in the mind of a reasonable litigant — a documented pattern of hostility, a demonstrated local influence of the opposite party, a genuine threat to safety. It is not enough to assert distrust, and imputations against a judicial officer made without foundation are treated seriously and may be visited with costs.

**Sub-module F — Section 25 and inter-State transfer.** Only the Supreme Court can move a case across State lines. The statutory test is a single phrase — "**expedient for the ends of justice**" — deliberately broad. The dominant recurring application is the matrimonial transfer petition, where the wife seeks transfer of proceedings instituted by the husband in a distant State. Sub-section (4)'s costs provision, capped at ₹2,000, is a rarely-used deterrent against frivolous petitions.

**Sub-module G — Transfer versus return of plaint: the limitation point.** This distinction is worth real money to a client:

| | **Return of plaint (O. VII R. 10)** | **Transfer (S. 24)** |
|---|---|---|
| Effect on the suit | Plaint is returned; on re-presentation the suit is **instituted afresh** | The **same suit** continues in a different court |
| Limitation | Fresh institution date; the plaintiff must invoke **S. 14 of the Limitation Act** to exclude the earlier period | Original institution date **preserved** |
| Evidence already recorded | Ordinarily begins afresh | Transferee court may **proceed from the point at which it was transferred** (S. 24(2)) |
| Available where transferor court lacks jurisdiction | Yes — that is its purpose | **Yes**, expressly, under S. 24(5) |

::: tip The strategic insight of this chapter
Where a suit has been filed in the wrong court and limitation has since expired, do **not** press for a return of the plaint under Order VII Rule 10 if you are the plaintiff — a return restarts the clock and forces you onto Section 14 of the Limitation Act. Apply instead under **Section 24(5)** to have the suit transferred to the competent court. The suit continues, the institution date survives, and limitation is not in issue at all.
:::

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — the lawful but oppressive forum.** P, a Delhi financier, sues D, a small trader of Coimbatore, on a guarantee. The guarantee was executed at Delhi, so Section 20(c) plainly gives the Delhi court jurisdiction. All of D's witnesses, records and business are at Coimbatore, and D's turnover cannot bear repeated travel.

- **Sword (defendant):** A Section 21 objection is hopeless — the Delhi court has jurisdiction. D's correct move is a **Section 22** application (both Delhi and Coimbatore being competent, since the guarantee was to be performed at Coimbatore), filed after notice to P, before settlement of issues, addressed under Section 23(3) to the **Delhi High Court** because the two courts are subordinate to different High Courts and the suit was brought at Delhi. In the alternative and without the timing constraint, D applies under **Section 24** to the Delhi High Court.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** P resists on the ground that he too is entitled to the protection of a forum where he can enforce a decree; that D's inconvenience is mere expense and not a denial of justice; and, if D has delayed, that the Section 22 window has closed with the settlement of issues — leaving D to the harder discretionary standard of Section 24.

**Illustration 2 — two suits, one transaction, two courts.** Arising out of a single joint venture, P sues D at Pune for accounts and D sues P at Nagpur for damages. Both suits turn on the same agreement and the same alleged breaches.

- **Sword (either party):** An application under **Section 24** to the Bombay High Court for transfer of one suit to the court trying the other, so that both may be tried together. The ground is the strongest available: common questions of law and fact, common evidence, and the real risk of conflicting decrees.
- **Shield (the resisting party):** Argues that the causes of action are distinct, that the evidence does not overlap, and — often more effectively — offers instead that the two suits be tried **analogously** by consent, with common evidence recorded in the leading suit and separate judgments delivered, avoiding transfer altogether.

::: info Consolidation without transfer
Where both suits are already pending **in the same court**, no transfer is needed at all: apply for analogous trial under **Section 151** read with the court's inherent power to regulate its own procedure, with evidence recorded in the leading suit and read in the connected suit by consent. Transfer is only required when the suits are in *different* courts.
:::

**Illustration 3 — the matrimonial transfer petition.** H files a petition for restitution of conjugal rights at Jodhpur. W, who has returned to her parents at Kolkata, has an infant child, no independent income, and no relative at Jodhpur.

- **Sword (wife):** A **Section 25** petition to the Supreme Court, supported by an affidavit under sub-section (2), pleading: the infant child; the absence of independent income; the absence of any person at Jodhpur to accompany her; the distance and cost; and, where applicable, an apprehension for her safety. The consistent practice of the Court is to weigh the convenience of the wife heavily in matrimonial transfers.
- **Shield (husband):** Offers to bear all travel and accommodation expenses for each hearing and to pay a fixed sum per appearance; offers to have the proceedings conducted through video-conferencing; and points out that he too has limited means. Courts frequently accept a well-structured expenses-and-video-conferencing offer as an alternative to transfer, so the offer should be concrete, quantified and recorded.

**Illustration 4 — Section 24(5) rescuing a time-barred suit.** P files a money suit on the last day of limitation in a court which, it later transpires, lacks pecuniary competence.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** Do **not** consent to a return of the plaint. Apply under **Section 24(1)(b)** read with **sub-section (5)** to the District Court to withdraw the suit from the incompetent court and transfer it to the competent one. The suit — instituted in time — continues. Had the plaint been returned instead, re-presentation would have been beyond limitation and P would have been driven to prove diligence and good faith under Section 14 of the Limitation Act.
- **Shield (defendant):** Presses for a return of the plaint under Order VII Rule 10 precisely because it destroys the limitation position, and resists the Section 24 route by arguing that sub-section (5) is meant for territorial, not pecuniary, incompetence — an argument that the plain words of the sub-section do not support.

**Illustration 5 — transfer of an execution proceeding.** A decree is being executed against immovable property, and the judgment-debtor alleges that the executing court is being influenced locally by the decree-holder.

- **Sword (judgment-debtor):** "Proceeding" in Section 24 expressly includes a proceeding for the execution of a decree (sub-section 3(b)). An application under Section 24 to the District Court or High Court is maintainable. The apprehension must be supported by objective material — instances, dates, orders.
- **Shield (decree-holder):** Points to the settled position that an adverse interlocutory order is not evidence of bias, that vague imputations against a judicial officer are impermissible, and that transfer applications in execution are a common delaying device. Seeks costs.

### 4. Procedural Interlocking Matrix

| Interlock | Provision | Practical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Timing of the S.22 application | **Section 21(1)** timetable, imported verbatim | Earliest opportunity; absolute bar at settlement of issues. |
| Settlement of issues | **Order XIV Rules 1, 5** | The guillotine date for Section 22. |
| Return of plaint — the alternative to transfer | **Order VII Rule 10, 10A** | Restarts limitation; prefer S.24(5) where the plaintiff's clock has run. |
| Exclusion of time in a court without jurisdiction | **Limitation Act, 1963, S. 14** | The plaintiff's remedy after a return of plaint; requires good faith and due diligence to be pleaded and proved. |
| Stay of a suit pending elsewhere | **Section 10** (*res sub judice*) | Where the two suits are between the same parties on the same matter, S.10 may be the better remedy than transfer. |
| Consolidation / analogous trial | **Section 151** | Where both suits are already in the same court. |
| Execution proceedings are transferable | **Section 24(3)(b)** | Expressly included in "proceeding". |
| Transfer of the decree for execution — distinct power | **Sections 38, 39; Order XXI Rules 5–10** | Do not confuse a S.24 transfer of the *execution proceeding* with a S.39 transfer of the *decree*. |
| Transferee court may continue from the same stage | **Sections 24(2), 25(3)** | Avoids re-trial; ask for a specific direction to this effect in the prayer. |
| Applicable law after inter-State transfer | **Section 25(5)** | The transferee court applies the law the original court ought to have applied. |
| Affidavit in support | **Section 25(2)**; **Order XIX Rules 1–3** | The S.25 motion **must** be supported by affidavit; O.XIX R.3 requires the affidavit to be confined to facts within the deponent's own knowledge, with sources of belief disclosed. |
| Compensatory costs for a frivolous application | **Section 25(4)** (₹2,000 cap); **Section 35A** (false or vexatious claims/defences) | Two distinct costs regimes. |
| Revision | **Section 115** | An order on transfer is generally not appealable; revision, or Article 227 supervisory jurisdiction, is the route where a jurisdictional error is alleged. |
| No appeal against a transfer order | **Order XLIII Rule 1** — transfer orders are not listed | Confirms that revision or Article 227 is the challenge route. |
| Matrimonial proceedings | **Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, S. 21** applies CPC; **Family Courts Act, 1984** | S.24 and S.25 both apply to matrimonial proceedings; most reported S.25 practice arises here. |
| Video-conferencing as an alternative to transfer | High Court Video Conferencing Rules; **Order XVIII Rule 4** | Increasingly the answer offered in resistance to a transfer petition — courts often accept it. |

::: warning Two "transfers" that must never be confused
**Section 24** transfers a *proceeding* — the suit, appeal or execution case itself — from one court to another. **Section 39** transfers a *decree* to another court for execution, because the property or person is outside the original court's limits. Applying under the wrong provision is a common and expensive error: a Section 24 application will not authorise a court to execute a decree that has never been transferred to it under Section 39, and Section 21(3) will not cure that defect.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Maneka Sanjay Gandhi v. Rani Jethmalani**, (1979) 4 SCC 167 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* A transfer petition founded on the petitioner's apprehension about the atmosphere in the trial court and the safety of the accused.
**Ratio decidendi:** Assurance of a fair trial is the first imperative of the dispensation of justice, and the central criterion for the court to consider when a motion for transfer is made is not the hypersensitivity or relative convenience of a party, but the existence of a **reasonable apprehension** that justice will not be done. Mere convenience of parties or their counsel is not sufficient; petitions of this kind must be approached with circumspection, since transfer casts a reflection on the court from which the case is moved.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The test in Maneka Sanjay Gandhi is objective: not whether my client feels apprehensive, but whether the material discloses a reasonable apprehension in the mind of a reasonable litigant that justice will not be done."
:::

**Dr. Subramaniam Swamy v. Ramakrishna Hegde**, (1990) 1 SCC 4 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* A petition under Section 25 to transfer a defamation suit from one State to another.
**Ratio decidendi:** The paramount consideration under Section 25 is the requirement of justice — whether the transfer is "expedient for the ends of justice". Convenience of parties is a relevant but not decisive factor, and the balance of convenience of *both* sides must be weighed. The petitioner must show that the trial in the chosen forum will result in a denial of justice, not merely that another forum would be more convenient for him.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Under Section 25 the applicant must establish that the ends of justice require the transfer — the balance of convenience of both sides is weighed, and the plaintiff's lawful choice of forum is not lightly displaced."
:::

**Durgesh Sharma v. Jayshree**, (2008) 9 SCC 648 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* Whether a High Court, exercising power under Section 24, may transfer a case from a court subordinate to it to a court in another State subordinate to a different High Court.
**Ratio decidendi:** A High Court has **no power under Section 24 to transfer a suit, appeal or other proceeding from a court subordinate to it to a court in another State**. Inter-State transfer is exclusively within the domain of the Supreme Court under Section 25. Section 24 operates only within the territorial limits of the High Court concerned.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Durgesh Sharma is decisive on forum: if the transfer sought crosses a State boundary, the only competent court is the Supreme Court under Section 25 — a High Court simply has no such power under Section 24."
:::

::: danger Fatal error — filing the inter-State transfer application in the High Court
This is the single most common procedural mistake in transfer practice. Applications to move a case from a court in State A to a court in State B must go to the **Supreme Court under Section 25**. A Section 24 application to the High Court for that relief is not merely likely to fail — it is beyond the High Court's power, and the months spent on it are irrecoverable while the trial proceeds in the original forum.
:::

**Kulwinder Kaur v. Kandi Friends Education Trust**, (2008) 3 SCC 659 — 2 Judges.
*Facts:* Scope of the discretion under Section 24 and the grounds on which transfer may be ordered.
**Ratio decidendi:** The Court declined to lay down an exhaustive list of grounds, holding that the power under Section 24 is discretionary and must be exercised on the facts of each case in the interests of justice. Illustrative considerations include the balance of convenience, the convenience of parties and witnesses, whether a fair and impartial trial is possible, whether questions of law of a complex nature are involved, and whether the transfer would cause hardship to the opposite party.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Section 24 confers a wide but judicial discretion; the grounds are illustrative and not exhaustive, and each application turns on whether the interests of justice require the transfer on the facts."
:::

**Krishna Veni Nagam v. Harish Nagam**, (2017) 4 SCC 150 — 2 Judges, and **Santhini v. Vijaya Venketesh**, (2018) 1 SCC 1 — 3 Judges.
*Facts and relationship:* Krishna Veni Nagam sought to lay down general directions permitting matrimonial proceedings to be conducted by video-conferencing so as to reduce the volume of transfer petitions. Santhini, a larger bench, reconsidered that approach.
**Ratio decidendi:** The larger bench in Santhini overruled the general directions in Krishna Veni Nagam so far as they made video-conferencing a substitute for transfer as a matter of course, holding that in matrimonial matters the statutory scheme and the possibility of settlement require the physical presence of parties, subject to exceptions in the discretion of the Family Court after settlement efforts have failed.
::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The video-conferencing answer to a transfer petition must be advanced as a fact-specific proposal within the discretion of the court, and not as a general substitute for transfer — Santhini having reconsidered the general directions in Krishna Veni Nagam."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
This is a fast-moving area, particularly in matrimonial transfer practice, where the position on video-conferencing has shifted between Krishna Veni Nagam, Santhini and subsequent developments including the general adoption of remote hearings. **Confirm the current position, the citations and the bench strengths in the official reports before relying on any of the above.** Durgesh Sharma on the S.24 / S.25 boundary is the most settled proposition in this group.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**Choosing the right provision — a decision tree.**

1. **Does the court in which the suit is filed have jurisdiction?**
   - **No** → Section 21 objection + Order VII Rule 10 (as defendant); or Section 24(5) transfer (as plaintiff, to save limitation).
   - **Yes** → go to 2.
2. **Are the two candidate courts in different States?**
   - **Yes** → **Section 25**, Supreme Court, motion supported by affidavit. Nothing else will do.
   - **No** → go to 3.
3. **Are you the defendant, and have issues not yet been settled?**
   - **Yes** → **Section 22**, after notice, addressed to the court identified by **Section 23**.
   - **No** (you are the plaintiff, or issues are settled) → **Section 24**, to the High Court or District Court, available at any stage.

**Where to file a Section 22 application — the Section 23 routing table.**

| Situation | Application lies to |
|---|---|
| Candidate courts subordinate to the **same** appellate court | That **appellate court** (typically the District Court) |
| Candidate courts subordinate to **different** appellate courts but the **same** High Court | That **High Court** |
| Candidate courts subordinate to **different** High Courts | The High Court within whose local limits the court **in which the suit is brought** is situate |

**Drafting traps.**

1. **Filing a Section 22 application without prior notice to the other parties.** The section says "after notice to the other parties, may … apply". Serve notice, record service, and annex proof.
2. **Missing the settlement-of-issues deadline for Section 22** and then filing under Section 22 anyway. If issues are settled, abandon Section 22 and go to Section 24, which has no such limit — but expect the delay to weigh against you on discretion.
3. **Pleading bias without material.** Bare imputations against a judicial officer will sink the application and may attract costs. Plead facts, dates, orders — or plead convenience instead.
4. **Filing an inter-State transfer application under Section 24.** See the danger box above.
5. **Omitting the affidavit in a Section 25 petition.** Sub-section (2) makes it mandatory; and under Order XIX Rule 3 the affidavit must be confined to facts within the deponent's knowledge, with sources of information and belief expressly disclosed.
6. **Failing to ask for the "continue from the same stage" direction.** Sections 24(2) and 25(3) permit the transferee court either to retry or to proceed from the stage reached, "subject to any special directions". If your client has already led evidence, ask expressly for a direction that the transferee court proceed from the stage at which the case was transferred; otherwise you may face a full retrial.
7. **Overlooking Section 10.** Where the real complaint is that two suits between the same parties on the same matter are pending, *res sub judice* under Section 10 may achieve what you need without the discretionary hazards of a transfer application.

**Strategic timing.**

| Application | Deadline | Consequence of delay |
|---|---|---|
| Section 22 | Earliest possible opportunity; **at or before settlement of issues** | Right is extinguished; you are relegated to S.24 |
| Section 24 | "At any stage" | No bar, but delay is a strong discretionary factor against you |
| Section 25 | "At any stage" | Same |
| Objection under Section 21 | Earliest possible opportunity; at or before settlement of issues | Objection is barred in appeal and revision |

**Model application under Section 24 — prayer clause.**

> It is therefore most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to:
> **(a)** withdraw Civil Suit No. [___] of [year] titled *[cause title]* from the file of the learned [Court] at [place], under Section 24(1)(b) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908;
> **(b)** transfer the said suit for trial and disposal to the learned [Court] at [place], being a court subordinate to this Hon'ble Court and competent to try and dispose of the same;
> **(c)** direct, under Section 24(2) of the Code, that the transferee court shall proceed from the stage at which the said suit stands transferred, and shall not retry the suit, the evidence of PW-1 and PW-2 having already been recorded;
> **(d)** stay all further proceedings in the said suit before the learned [Court] at [place] during the pendency of the present application; and
> **(e)** pass such further or other order as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit and proper in the facts and circumstances of the case.

## Chapter consolidation — where do I file, and what if I got it wrong?

**Part A — The forum table.**

| Nature of suit | Governing provision | Forum |
|---|---|---|
| Recovery, partition, mortgage, declaration of right to, or compensation for wrong to, **immovable property** | **S. 16(a)–(e)** | Where the property is situate — **mandatory** |
| Specific performance **simpliciter**, no possession claimed | **Proviso to S. 16** | Situs **or** where the defendant resides / carries on business — plaintiff's option |
| Specific performance **with possession** | **S. 16**, proviso displaced | Situs only |
| Immovable property across **two or more** jurisdictions | **S. 17** | Any court where a portion lies, provided it is competent for the **entire** claim |
| Situs genuinely **uncertain** | **S. 18** | Any of the candidate courts, on recording a statement of uncertainty |
| Compensation for wrong to the **person** or to **movables** | **S. 19** | Where the wrong was done, **or** where the defendant resides / carries on business — plaintiff's option |
| **Everything else** — contract, money, accounts, injunction in personam | **S. 20** | Defendant's residence / business (all, or one with leave), **or** where the cause of action arose wholly or in part |
| **Grade** of court, in every case | **S. 15** | The lowest grade competent by value |

**Part B — The error-and-remedy table.**

| The problem | Who raises it | Provision | When | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Court lacks **subject-matter** jurisdiction | Anyone | **S. 9**; O. VII R. 11(d) | **Any stage**, any proceeding | Decree is a **nullity** |
| Suit filed in a court of **lower** grade than competent | Defendant | Inherent incompetence; O. VII R. 10 | Any stage | Decree is a **nullity** |
| Suit filed in a court of **higher** grade than necessary | Defendant | **S. 15**, cured by **S. 21(2)** | Earliest opportunity, before issues | Irregularity; decree stands absent failure of justice |
| Wrong **place of suing** | Defendant | **Ss. 16–20**, cured by **S. 21(1)**; O. VII R. 10 | Earliest opportunity, before issues | Irregularity; decree stands absent failure of justice |
| Wrong **local limits of the executing court** | Judgment-debtor | **S. 21(3)**; S. 47 | Earliest opportunity in the executing court | Objection barred unless failure of justice |
| Fresh suit to set aside a decree on **place of suing** | Defeated party | **Barred by S. 21A**; O. VII R. 11(d) | — | Plaint rejected |
| Forum lawful but **oppressive**, both courts competent | **Defendant only** | **S. 22** (routed by **S. 23**) | Before settlement of issues | Transfer to the other competent court |
| Forum lawful but inconvenient; or related suits in different courts; or execution to be moved | **Any party**, or court *suo motu* | **S. 24** | **Any stage** | Transfer or withdrawal, **even from a court without jurisdiction** (s-s. 5) |
| Transfer **across State boundaries** | Any party | **S. 25** — Supreme Court **only** | Any stage | Transfer if expedient for the ends of justice |

::: tip The three sentences worth memorising from this chapter
**One.** Subject-matter jurisdiction can never be conferred by consent or cured by acquiescence; place and pecuniary jurisdiction ordinarily can.
**Two.** A place-of-suing objection not taken in the court of first instance at the earliest opportunity and before the settlement of issues, and unaccompanied by a demonstrated consequent failure of justice, is dead — in appeal by force of Section 21, and in a fresh suit by force of Section 21A.
**Three.** Where the plaintiff has filed in the wrong court and limitation has since run, transfer under Section 24(5) preserves the suit; return of the plaint under Order VII Rule 10 does not.
:::
