# Sections 96 to 115 — Appeals, Review and Revision

There is no natural right of appeal. It is a creature of statute, and every appellate remedy in the Code exists only because a section creates it and only to the extent that section allows. That single proposition explains most of what follows: why an appeal lies from a decree but only from a *listed* order; why the second appeal is confined to a substantial question of law; why review is available on narrow grounds before the same court; and why revision is not a second bite at the facts.

The remedies form a hierarchy of narrowing scrutiny. A first appeal rehears the case on facts and law. A second appeal touches only substantial questions of law. Revision looks solely at jurisdictional error. Review does not look at correctness at all, but at whether an error is apparent on the face of the record. Choosing wrongly among them costs the client the remedy, because limitation runs while the mistake is discovered.

| Provision | One-line function |
| --- | --- |
| s. 96 | First appeal from an original decree — on facts and law |
| s. 96(3) | **No appeal from a consent decree** |
| s. 97 | Failure to appeal a preliminary decree bars later challenge |
| s. 99 | No reversal for misjoinder or irregularity not affecting the merits or jurisdiction |
| s. 100 | Second appeal — only on a **substantial question of law** |
| s. 100A | No further appeal from a decision of a single Judge in appeal |
| s. 103 | Power of the High Court to determine issues of fact in second appeal |
| ss. 104–106 | Appeals from **orders** — confined to the listed categories |
| s. 105 | Interlocutory orders may be attacked in the appeal from the decree; **s. 105(2)** bars a remand order not appealed |
| s. 107 | Powers of the appellate court — determine, remand, frame issues, take evidence |
| s. 114 | Review |
| s. 115 | Revision — jurisdictional error only |

::: info The architecture in one breath
Section 96 gives a full rehearing from a decree. Section 100 narrows the next tier to a substantial question of law, and section 100A stops the chain after a single Judge decides an appeal. Orders are different in kind: section 104 with **Order XLIII Rule 1** provides an exhaustive list, and anything not listed is not independently appealable — but section 105 preserves it for attack in the appeal from the final decree. Section 114 with **Order XLVII** allows the same court to correct an error apparent on the face of the record. Section 115 allows the High Court to correct a subordinate court that acted without jurisdiction, exceeded it, or acted with material irregularity — and nothing more.
:::

::: warning The four questions that decide your remedy
1. Is the decision a **decree** or an **order**? Decrees are appealable under s. 96; orders only if listed in O. XLIII R. 1.
2. If it is a decree, is this the **first** or **second** appeal? A second appeal needs a substantial question of law formulated under s. 100(4).
3. If no appeal lies, is the error one of **jurisdiction** (s. 115 / Article 227) or one **apparent on the face of the record** (s. 114)?
4. What is the **limitation**? Ninety days to the High Court, thirty days to the District Court (Articles 116–117, Limitation Act, 1963); thirty days for review (Article 124). Verify against the current Schedule.
:::

## Sections 96, 97 and 99 with Order XLI — the first appeal

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **96. Appeal from original decree.** — (1) Save where otherwise expressly provided in the body of this Code or by any other law for the time being in force, an appeal shall lie from every decree passed by any Court exercising original jurisdiction to the Court authorised to hear appeals from the decisions of such Court.
>
> (2) An appeal may lie from an original decree passed ex parte.
>
> (3) **No appeal shall lie from a decree passed by the Court with the consent of parties.**
>
> (4) No appeal shall lie, except on a question of law, from a decree in any suit of the nature cognisable by Courts of Small Causes, when the amount or value of the subject-matter of the original suit does not exceed ten thousand rupees.

> **97. Appeal from final decree where no appeal from preliminary decree.** — Where any party aggrieved by a preliminary decree passed after the commencement of this Code does not appeal from such decree, he shall be precluded from disputing its correctness in any appeal which may be preferred from the final decree.

> **99. No decree to be reversed or modified for error or irregularity not affecting merits or jurisdiction.** — No decree shall be reversed or substantially varied, nor shall any case be remanded, in appeal on account of any misjoinder or non-joinder of parties or causes of action or any error, defect or irregularity in any proceeding in the suit, not affecting the merits of the case or the jurisdiction of the Court:
>
> Provided that nothing in this section shall apply to non-joinder of a necessary party.

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| s. 96(1) — "**an appeal shall lie from every decree**" | **Right, subject to express exception** | The first appeal is a matter of right, not of leave |
| s. 96(1) — "Save where otherwise expressly provided" | **Limiting** | Other statutes may bar or channel the appeal |
| s. 96(2) — "**may** lie … ex parte" | **Enabling** | An ex parte decree is appealable *and* may be set aside under O. IX R. 13 — two concurrent remedies |
| s. 96(3) — "**No appeal shall lie** … with the consent of parties" | **Absolute bar** | Consent decrees are challenged, if at all, by suit or recall on the ground of no consent |
| s. 97 — "**shall be precluded**" | **Mandatory preclusion** | A preliminary decree unappealed becomes final between the parties |
| s. 99 — "**No decree shall be reversed** … not affecting the merits or the jurisdiction" | **Curative** | Technical defects do not upset decrees |
| s. 99 proviso — non-joinder of a **necessary** party | **Carve-out** | The one defect that survives the curative rule |

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — the first appeal is a rehearing, not a review.** This is the point most often underestimated. On a first appeal the appellate court has the same jurisdiction as the trial court over both fact and law: it may reappreciate the entire evidence, reverse findings of fact, and substitute its own conclusions. Correspondingly, **Order XLI Rule 31** obliges the first appellate court to write a judgment stating the points for determination, the decision on each, and the reasons — a judgment that merely records agreement with the trial court is liable to be set aside as no judgment at all.

**Sub-module B — the consent decree bar, and its real boundary.** Section 96(3) prohibits an appeal from a decree passed with the consent of parties, because there is no *lis* left to agitate — the parties have compromised. But the bar assumes consent actually existed. Where a party contends there was **no lawful agreement or compromise at all**, or that consent was obtained by fraud or was given without authority, that dispute is decided by the court that recorded the compromise, under the proviso to **Order XXIII Rule 3** — not by appeal. The distinction is fine and decisive: you do not appeal a consent decree; you apply to the recording court to have it recalled on the ground that there was no consent.

**Sub-module C — section 97 and the preliminary decree trap.** In partition, mortgage and accounts suits the court passes a preliminary decree declaring rights and a final decree working them out. If a party is aggrieved by the preliminary decree — the shares declared, the accounts directed — he must appeal **then**. Section 97 forecloses the point at the final-decree stage. Every year, litigants who thought they would "raise it at the end" find the shares fixed against them beyond challenge.

**Sub-module D — the ex parte decree's two doors.** A defendant against whom an ex parte decree is passed may (i) appeal under s. 96(2), or (ii) apply under **Order IX Rule 13** to set it aside for want of service or sufficient cause. The routes differ in what they examine: the appeal tests the decree's correctness on the material; the Rule 13 application tests whether the defendant was properly served or prevented by sufficient cause. Both may be pursued, but Explanation to Rule 13 provides that where an appeal has been disposed of on the merits, the Rule 13 application will not lie.

**Sub-module E — cross-objections under Order XLI Rule 22.** A respondent who is partly aggrieved need not always file his own appeal. He may file **cross-objections** within one month of service of notice of the appeal, and they are heard as if they were an appeal. Crucially, cross-objections survive the withdrawal or dismissal of the main appeal. A respondent who neither appeals nor cross-objects is taken to have accepted the decree and cannot ask the appellate court to improve his position.

**Sub-module F — additional evidence under Order XLI Rule 27 is exceptional.** The appellate court is not a forum for filling gaps left at trial. Rule 27 permits additional evidence only where the trial court refused evidence that ought to have been admitted, or the party shows that despite due diligence the evidence was not within his knowledge or could not be produced, or the appellate court itself requires it to pronounce judgment. The last ground is for the *court's* need, not the party's convenience.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — the perfunctory appellate judgment.** A first appellate court disposes of a title appeal in three paragraphs, saying it agrees with the trial court's reasoning. *As sword in second appeal:* the appellant contends non-compliance with **O. XLI R. 31** — no points for determination, no independent reasons — and seeks remand. This is one of the most frequently successful grounds in second appeal.

**Illustration 2 — the compromise the client says he never made.** A decree is recorded on a compromise petition bearing what the defendant says is a forged signature. *Wrong move:* filing a first appeal, which s. 96(3) bars. *Right move:* an application before the **court that recorded the compromise**, under the proviso to O. XXIII R. 3, to decide whether a lawful compromise was in fact arrived at.

**Illustration 3 — the partition suit.** A preliminary decree declares the plaintiff entitled to one-sixth. He believes he is entitled to one-third but does not appeal, intending to raise it after the Commissioner reports. *Result:* s. 97 precludes him. The one-sixth share is final.

**Illustration 4 — the respondent who wanted more.** The plaintiff sues for ₹20 lakhs and is decreed ₹12 lakhs. The defendant appeals. The plaintiff, content to defend, files nothing. *Result:* he cannot ask the appellate court to raise the decree to ₹20 lakhs; that required cross-objections under O. XLI R. 22 within one month of notice.

**Illustration 5 — the curable defect.** A necessary party's name is misspelled and a formal proof was taken out of sequence. *As shield on appeal:* s. 99 answers it — no reversal for irregularity not affecting the merits or jurisdiction. *But* if a co-owner whose presence was essential was never joined at all, the proviso applies and s. 99 gives no protection.

::: danger Filing an appeal does not stay execution
This is the single most expensive misconception in appellate practice. Under **Order XLI Rule 5**, an appeal operates as no stay; execution proceeds unless the appellate court — or, under R. 5(2), the court that passed the decree — orders otherwise. A client told "we have appealed" may lose possession or see his property sold while the appeal is admitted but unstayed. Apply for stay **with** the memorandum, not after.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Santosh Hazari v. Purushottam Tiwari, (2001) 3 SCC 179** — Bench of three.
*Facts:* A first appellate court reversed the trial court without recording its own findings on the issues.
**Ratio decidendi:** The first appeal is a valuable right and the parties have a right to a full hearing on questions of fact as well as law; the first appellate court must record its own findings on all issues, and a judgment that does not comply with the requirements of Order XLI Rule 31 is unsustainable.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The first appellate court has recorded no point for determination and no independent finding; on *Santosh Hazari* that is not a judgment in the eye of law, and the matter must go back."
:::

**Madhukar v. Sangram, (2001) 4 SCC 756** — Bench of two.
*Facts:* Concerned the extent of the first appellate court's duty when it affirms a trial court decree.
**Ratio decidendi:** Sitting as a court of first appeal, it is the duty of the court to deal with all the issues and the evidence led by the parties before recording its findings; a cursory affirmation does not discharge that duty.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"An appellate court affirming a decree owes the same duty to engage with the evidence as one reversing it — *Madhukar v. Sangram*."
:::

**Union of India v. Ibrahim Uddin, (2012) 8 SCC 148** — Bench of two.
*Facts:* Additional evidence was sought at the appellate stage without satisfying the conditions of Order XLI Rule 27.
**Ratio decidendi:** Additional evidence cannot be admitted merely because it would assist the party seeking it; the discretion is confined to the grounds in Rule 27, and where the ground invoked is the court's own requirement, it must be the court's need to pronounce judgment, not the party's failure at trial.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Rule 27 is not a remedy for a case badly led at trial. On *Ibrahim Uddin*, the applicant must bring himself within one of the three grounds, and he has not."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Bench strength, paragraph numbers and subsequent history must be checked against the current reports. Limitation articles and court-fee provisions vary by State and are periodically amended — confirm both before filing.
:::

**The first-appeal filing checklist:**

| # | Item |

| 1 | Certified copy of the **judgment and decree** — time for obtaining them is excludable under s. 12, Limitation Act |
| 2 | Limitation computed and, if needed, a **s. 5 application** with an affidavit explaining every day of delay |

::: danger Five ways a first appeal is lost before it is heard
1. **Appealing a consent decree** — barred by s. 96(3); the remedy lies before the recording court under O. XXIII R. 3.
2. **Not appealing the preliminary decree** — foreclosed by s. 97.
3. **No stay application** — the decree is executed while the appeal is pending.
4. **No cross-objections** — the respondent cannot improve his decree.
5. **Treating Rule 27 as a second chance at evidence** — the application fails and signals the weakness of the case.
:::

## Sections 100, 100A, 101 and 103 with Order XLII — the second appeal

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **100. Second appeal.** — (1) Save as otherwise expressly provided in the body of this Code or by any other law for the time being in force, an appeal shall lie to the High Court from every decree passed in appeal by any Court subordinate to the High Court, **if the High Court is satisfied that the case involves a substantial question of law**.
>
> (2) An appeal may lie under this section from an appellate decree passed ex parte.
>
> (3) In an appeal under this section, the memorandum of appeal shall precisely state the substantial question of law involved in the appeal.
>
> (4) Where the High Court is satisfied that a substantial question of law is involved in any case, **it shall formulate that question**.
>
> (5) The appeal shall be heard on the question so formulated and the respondent shall, at the hearing of the appeal, be allowed to argue that the case does not involve such question:
>
> Provided that nothing in this sub-section shall be deemed to take away or abridge the power of the Court to hear, for reasons to be recorded, the appeal on any other substantial question of law, not formulated by it, if it is satisfied that the case involves such question.

> **100A. No further appeal in certain cases.** — Notwithstanding anything contained in any Letters Patent for any High Court or in any instrument having the force of law or in any other law for the time being in force, where any appeal from an original or appellate decree or order is heard and decided by a **single Judge of a High Court, no further appeal shall lie** from the judgment and decree of such single Judge.

> **101. Second appeal on no other grounds.** — No second appeal shall lie except on the ground mentioned in section 100.

> **103. Power of High Court to determine issue of fact.** — In any second appeal, the High Court may, if the evidence on the record is sufficient, determine any issue necessary for the disposal of the appeal —
>
> (a) which has not been determined by the lower appellate Court or both by the Court of first instance and the lower appellate Court, or
>
> (b) which has been wrongly determined by such Court or Courts by reason of a decision on such question of law as is referred to in section 100.

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| s. 100(1) — "**if the High Court is satisfied**" | **Condition precedent to jurisdiction** | Satisfaction is not a formality; without a substantial question of law the High Court has no jurisdiction to enter the appeal at all |
| s. 100(3) — "**shall precisely state**" | **Mandatory pleading requirement** | A memorandum that recites grounds of fact instead of a question of law is defective on its face |
| s. 100(4) — "**it shall formulate that question**" | **Mandatory judicial act** | Formulation is not optional. A judgment deciding a second appeal without a formulated question is liable to be set aside on that ground alone |
| s. 100(5) proviso — "**for reasons to be recorded**" | **Conditional enlargement** | The Court may go beyond the formulated question, but only with recorded reasons and after hearing the respondent on it |
| s. 100A — "**Notwithstanding anything contained in any Letters Patent**" | **Non-obstante clause** | Overrides the Letters Patent appeal. The single Judge in appeal is the end of the road within the High Court |
| s. 101 — "**No second appeal shall lie except**" | **Exhaustive bar** | Closes the argument that some residual equitable jurisdiction survives s. 100 |
| s. 103 — "**may, if the evidence on the record is sufficient**" | **Discretionary, conditional** | A power to decide facts, not to re-open them. It is confined to the existing record |

::: danger The single most common fatal defect
A second appeal that argues the evidence was wrongly appreciated is not a second appeal. It is a first appeal filed in the wrong forum, and it will be dismissed at the admission stage. The lower appellate court is the **final court of fact**. Unless its finding is perverse, based on no evidence, or reached by ignoring admissible evidence or admitting inadmissible evidence, the finding is beyond challenge.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

#### 2.1 What makes a question of law "substantial"

Not every legal question qualifies. The test that has survived is threefold. The question must be (i) debatable — not already concluded by binding authority; (ii) material — its answer must affect the outcome of the case, not merely the reasoning; and (iii) either of general public importance or one that directly and substantially affects the rights of the parties.

A question already settled by the Supreme Court, or by a binding decision of the same High Court, is not substantial however forcefully it is argued. A question that would leave the decree standing whatever the answer is not substantial because it is not material. And a question that turns on the construction of a particular document between these parties may still be substantial, because the second limb — direct and substantial effect on the parties' rights — is disjunctive from general public importance.

#### 2.2 The distinction between a question of fact, a question of law, and a mixed question

- **Question of fact:** What did the deed say? Was possession delivered? Was the notice served? Findings on these are for the trial court and the first appellate court.
- **Question of law:** What is the legal effect of what the deed said? Does the pleaded fact constitute a valid tender in law? Does the admitted possession amount to adverse possession?
- **Mixed question:** Whether a given set of proved facts satisfies a statutory standard — for example, whether proved conduct amounts to "wilful default". The primary facts are for the fact-finding courts; the application of the legal standard to them is a question of law and can be a substantial one.

The practical drafting move is therefore to concede the primary facts and attack the legal characterisation. The moment the memorandum disputes what happened, the appeal is outside s. 100.

#### 2.3 When a finding of fact can be attacked — perversity

A finding of fact is open to challenge in second appeal in a narrow set of situations, and each must be pleaded as a question of law:

1. The finding is based on **no evidence** at all.
2. The finding rests on **inadmissible evidence**, and without it the finding cannot stand.
3. **Admissible and material evidence was ignored** — not merely disbelieved, but not considered.
4. The finding is **perverse**, meaning no reasonable person acting judicially could have reached it on the material.
5. The finding was reached by **misconstruing a document** whose construction is a question of law.

The distinction that matters: a wrong appreciation of evidence is not perversity. Only a finding unsupportable on any view of the record qualifies.

#### 2.4 Section 100(4) — formulation is jurisdictional

The High Court must formulate the question at the admission stage. This is not a clerical step. It defines the boundary of the hearing, it tells the respondent what case must be met, and it fixes the scope of the judgment. A judgment that decides a second appeal without formulating any question, or that decides on a question different from the one formulated without recording reasons under the proviso to s. 100(5), is vulnerable in appeal to the Supreme Court.

#### 2.5 Section 100A — the end of the Letters Patent appeal

Before the 2002 amendment, a decision of a single Judge of a High Court in appeal could be carried to a Division Bench under the Letters Patent. Section 100A, with its non-obstante clause, closed that route. Where a single Judge hears and decides an appeal — whether from an original decree, an appellate decree, or an order — no further intra-court appeal lies. The only route beyond is the Supreme Court.

Note carefully what s. 100A does **not** cover: it applies where the single Judge sat *in appeal*. A single Judge deciding an original-side suit, or exercising writ jurisdiction under Article 226, is not within s. 100A, and the intra-court appeal in those situations survives on its own terms.

#### 2.6 Section 103 — deciding facts in a second appeal

Section 103 is often misread as a licence to re-appreciate evidence. It is the opposite: it exists so that a second appeal succeeding on a point of law does not have to be remanded when the record is already sufficient to decide the consequential factual issue. It operates only where an issue was **not determined** below, or was **wrongly determined because of the very error of law** on which the second appeal succeeds — and only if the evidence on record suffices. It is a power of disposal, not of review.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

::: tip Illustration 1 — the question that is not substantial
A tenant's second appeal contends that the first appellate court wrongly believed the landlord's witness on bona fide requirement. Framed as "whether the finding on bona fide need is sustainable", the question fails all three limbs — it is not debatable in law, it asks the High Court to weigh testimony, and the answer turns on credibility. The appeal is dismissed at admission.
:::

::: tip Illustration 2 — the same case, correctly framed
The same tenant reframes: the first appellate court held that a landlord's requirement for a married son is *per se* the landlord's own requirement, without any finding of dependency. The question becomes "whether requirement of an adult independent son can be the landlord's bona fide requirement within the statute in the absence of a finding of dependency". That is a question of legal characterisation on admitted facts, it is material, and it affects the parties' rights directly. It is admitted.
:::

::: tip Illustration 3 — sword: the ignored document
In a suit on title, a registered sale deed was exhibited and marked but neither the trial court nor the first appellate court referred to it in deciding title. The appellant pleads the question as "whether a finding on title reached without considering a registered document exhibited on record is sustainable in law". This is the ignored-material-evidence ground — a question of law, not an invitation to reweigh.
:::

::: tip Illustration 4 — shield: resisting formulation
For the respondent, the strategy under s. 100(5) is to argue at the hearing that the formulated question does not in truth arise on the pleadings or the findings — for example, that the point was never raised before the first appellate court and there is no finding to support it. Section 100(5) expressly preserves this argument even after formulation.
:::

::: tip Illustration 5 — s. 103 in operation
A second appeal succeeds on the point that the first appellate court applied the wrong article of limitation. The issue of when the cause of action accrued was decided below only through that wrong lens, but the evidence — a dated notice and an admitted reply — is complete on the record. Rather than remand, the High Court decides the accrual issue itself under s. 103(b) and disposes of the suit.
:::

Where the trial court and the first appellate court have concurred on a finding of fact, the threshold for interference is at its highest. A generalised complaint that the concurrent view is wrong will not be entertained.

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

#### Chunilal V. Mehta & Sons Ltd. v. Century Spinning & Manufacturing Co. Ltd., AIR 1962 SC 1314

**Bench strength:** Five Judges (Constitution Bench).

*Facts:* A dispute over the construction of a managing-agency agreement raised the question whether its interpretation was a substantial question of law for the purposes of an appeal certificate.

**Ratio decidendi:** A question of law is substantial if it is of general public importance, **or** if it directly and substantially affects the rights of the parties and is not finally settled by the Supreme Court, the Privy Council or the Federal Court, or is not free from difficulty and calls for discussion of alternative views. A question is not substantial where it is settled, or where the principle is settled and only its application to the facts remains.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The question raised is substantial within *Chunilal V. Mehta*, being neither concluded by binding authority nor free from difficulty, and directly affecting the rights of the parties."
:::

#### Santosh Hazari v. Purushottam Tiwari (Deceased) by LRs, (2001) 3 SCC 179

**Bench strength:** Three Judges.

*Facts:* A second appeal in a title suit where the first appellate court had reversed the trial court without an independent reappraisal of the evidence.

**Ratio decidendi:** The phrase "substantial question of law" imports something in contradistinction to a technical, or a question of little or no substance. To be substantial, the question must be debatable, not previously settled, and must have a material bearing on the decision of the case. The judgment also fixes the correlative duty of the first appellate court to record its own reasons on the points for determination — a failure that itself can generate a substantial question of law.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The first appellate court has not discharged its obligation under Order XLI Rule 31 to record independent reasons on the points for determination, and that omission is itself a substantial question of law within *Santosh Hazari*."
:::

#### Hero Vinoth (Minor) v. Seshammal, (2006) 5 SCC 545

**Bench strength:** Two Judges.

*Facts:* A second appeal in which the High Court interfered with concurrent findings of fact on the validity of a sale.

**Ratio decidendi:** The general rule is that the High Court will not interfere with concurrent findings of fact. The exceptions are confined: where the courts below ignored material evidence or acted on no evidence, where they drew wrong inferences from proved facts by applying the law erroneously, or where the finding is based on inadmissible evidence or arrived at by considering matters irrelevant in law. Interference on any other basis exceeds s. 100.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The concurrent finding is assailable within the *Hero Vinoth* exceptions, being founded on no evidence / reached by ignoring [specify the exhibit], and therefore raises a question of law and not of appreciation."
:::

#### Municipal Committee, Hoshiarpur v. Punjab State Electricity Board, (2010) 13 SCC 216

**Bench strength:** Two Judges.

*Facts:* A second appeal decided by the High Court without formulating any substantial question of law.

**Ratio decidendi:** Formulation of the substantial question of law under s. 100(4) is mandatory. The jurisdiction of the High Court in second appeal is confined to the question so formulated, and a judgment rendered without formulation, or on a question not formulated and without recording reasons under the proviso to s. 100(5), is unsustainable.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The impugned judgment decides the second appeal without formulating any substantial question of law and is therefore without jurisdiction under section 100(4)."
:::

#### Nazir Mohamed v. J. Kamala, (2020) 19 SCC 57

**Bench strength:** Two Judges.

*Facts:* A second appeal where the High Court reversed findings on the strength of a re-appreciation of the evidence.

**Ratio decidendi:** A second appeal does not lie on the ground of erroneous findings of fact based on the appreciation of the relevant evidence. The existence of a substantial question of law is *sine qua non* for the exercise of jurisdiction under s. 100, and the High Court cannot substitute its own view of the evidence for that of the first appellate court, which is the final court of fact.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The existence of a substantial question of law is the *sine qua non* for the exercise of jurisdiction under section 100; in its absence the High Court has no jurisdiction to disturb the finding of the final court of fact."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Citations, bench strength and the current text of ss. 100–103 must be checked against the current bare Act, any State amendments to s. 100, and the latest reported judgments before being relied on in court.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**Drafting the substantial question — a working method**

1. Read the first appellate judgment and isolate every **finding of primary fact**. Accept them all for the purposes of the memorandum.
2. Identify the **legal standard** the court applied to those facts — the statutory phrase, the doctrine, the rule of construction.
3. Ask whether that standard was correctly identified and correctly applied. The gap between the two is where the question lives.
4. Frame it as a **question**, beginning "Whether…", capable of a yes-or-no answer, and material — if answered your way, the decree must fall.
5. Check it is not concluded by binding authority. If it is, and the authority is against you, the appeal is not maintainable on that question.
6. State it **precisely** in the memorandum as s. 100(3) requires, before the grounds.

**Checklist before filing a second appeal**

| Step | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Certified copy of the first appellate decree and judgment |
| 2 | Limitation computed from the correct date, with time for obtaining copies excluded |
| 3 | Substantial question of law precisely stated in the memorandum — s. 100(3) |
| 4 | Grounds confined to law; no ground inviting re-appreciation of evidence |
| 5 | Where a finding of fact is attacked, the specific vice pleaded — no evidence / inadmissible evidence / ignored evidence / perversity — with the exhibit identified |
| 6 | Separate application for stay of execution; filing alone does not stay |
| 7 | Application under s. 5 of the Limitation Act, if delayed, supported by an affidavit disclosing sufficient cause day by day |

::: danger Five ways a second appeal fails at admission
1. **The question is really a fact.** "Whether the courts below correctly appreciated the evidence" is not a question of law.
2. **The question is settled.** Binding authority already answers it; it is therefore not substantial.
3. **The question is immaterial.** Answering it either way leaves the decree standing.
4. **No question stated.** The memorandum runs to grounds without stating the question at all, contrary to s. 100(3).
5. **The point was never taken below.** There is no finding to attack, and the High Court will not entertain a case pleaded for the first time in second appeal.
:::

## Sections 104 to 107 with Order XLIII — appeals from orders and the powers of the appellate court

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **104. Orders from which appeal lies.** — (1) An appeal shall lie from the following orders, and save as otherwise expressly provided in the body of this Code or by any law for the time being in force, **from no other orders** —
>
> (ff) an order under section 35A;
>
> (ffa) an order under section 91 or section 92 refusing leave to institute a suit of the nature referred to in section 91 or section 92, as the case may be;
>
> (g) an order under section 95;
>
> (h) an order under any of the provisions of this Code imposing a fine or directing the arrest or detention in the civil prison of any person except where such arrest or detention is in execution of a decree;
>
> (i) any order made under rules from which an appeal is expressly allowed by rules:
>
> Provided that no appeal shall lie against any order specified in clause (ff) save on the ground that no order, or an order for the payment of a less amount, ought to have been made.
>
> (2) **No appeal shall lie from any order passed in appeal under this section.**

> **105. Other orders.** — (1) Save as otherwise expressly provided, no appeal shall lie from any order made by a Court in the exercise of its original or appellate jurisdiction; but, where a decree is appealed from, **any error, defect or irregularity in any order, affecting the decision of the case, may be set forth as a ground of objection in the memorandum of appeal**.
>
> (2) Notwithstanding anything contained in sub-section (1), where any party aggrieved by an order of remand made after the commencement of this Code from which an appeal lies does not appeal therefrom, **he shall thereafter be precluded from disputing its correctness**.

> **106. What Courts to hear appeals.** — Where an appeal from any order is allowed it shall lie to the Court to which an appeal would lie from the decree in the suit in which such order was made, or where such order is made by a Court (not being a High Court) in the exercise of appellate jurisdiction, then to the High Court.

> **107. Powers of Appellate Court.** — (1) Subject to such conditions and limitations as may be prescribed, an Appellate Court shall have power —
>
> (a) to determine a case finally;
>
> (b) to remand a case;
>
> (c) to frame issues and refer them for trial;
>
> (d) to take additional evidence or to require such evidence to be taken.
>
> (2) Subject as aforesaid, the Appellate Court shall have the same powers and shall perform as nearly as may be the same duties as are conferred and imposed by this Code on Courts of original jurisdiction in respect of suits instituted therein.

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| s. 104(1) — "**from no other orders**" | **Exhaustive bar** | The list in s. 104 read with O. XLIII R. 1 is closed. An order outside it carries no independent appeal, however serious the error |
| s. 104(1)(i) — "**from which an appeal is expressly allowed by rules**" | **Incorporating provision** | This is the doorway to Order XLIII Rule 1, which supplies the real working list |
| s. 104(2) — "**No appeal shall lie from any order passed in appeal**" | **Absolute bar** | One appeal from an order, and no more. There is no second appeal from an order |
| s. 105(1) — "**may be set forth as a ground of objection**" | **Preservation, not revival** | A non-appealable interlocutory order is not lost; it is carried forward and attacked in the appeal from the decree |
| s. 105(1) — "**affecting the decision of the case**" | **Materiality threshold** | The interlocutory error must have influenced the result. A harmless irregularity does not survive s. 99 |
| s. 105(2) — "**shall thereafter be precluded**" | **Mandatory preclusion** | The one exception to s. 105(1). An appealable remand order not appealed becomes unassailable |
| s. 107(1) — "**shall have power**" | **Enabling** | The four powers; their exercise is governed by the conditions in Order XLI |
| s. 107(2) — "**the same powers and… the same duties**" | **Assimilation** | The appellate court is a court of first instance for the purposes of its own procedure |

::: danger Section 105(2) is a trap that closes silently
An order of remand under Order XLI Rule 23 or 23A **is** appealable — Order XLIII Rule 1(u). If you accept the remand and go back to trial without appealing, you are barred forever from saying the remand was wrong, even when the case eventually reaches the appellate court on the final decree. The decision to accept a remand must therefore be taken deliberately, within limitation, and recorded in the file.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

#### 2.1 The two-tier structure of orders

The Code treats decrees and orders differently on purpose. A decree conclusively determines the rights of the parties on some or all matters in controversy; an order is everything else. If every order were appealable, no suit would reach trial. So the Code allows an appeal only from those orders which either finally dispose of a right (rejection of a plaint), or inflict an immediate and irreversible consequence (an injunction, an attachment, a receiver, an arrest).

Everything else is preserved rather than appealed. Section 105(1) is the compensating mechanism: the error is not lost, it is postponed.

#### 2.2 Order XLIII Rule 1 — the working list

Section 104(1)(i) points to the rules, and Order XLIII Rule 1 supplies the list that practitioners actually use. The categories that recur most often:

| Clause | Order and Rule | Order appealed from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| (a) | O. VII R. 10 | Returning a plaint for presentation to the proper court |
| (c) | O. IX R. 9 | Refusing to set aside a dismissal for default |
| (d) | O. IX R. 13 | Refusing to set aside an ex parte decree |
| (f) | O. XI R. 21 | Dismissal of suit or striking of defence for non-compliance with discovery |
| (i) | O. XXI R. 34 | Order on an objection to the draft of a document |
| (j) | O. XXI R. 72 or R. 92 | Setting aside or refusing to set aside a sale |
| (k) | O. XXII R. 9 | Refusing to set aside an abatement or dismissal |
| (q) | O. XXV R. 1 or R. 2 | Security for costs |
| (r) | O. XXXIX RR. 1, 2, 2A, 4 or 10 | **Temporary injunctions — grant, refusal, vacation, disobedience** |
| (s) | O. XXXVIII R. 2, 3 or 6 | Attachment before judgment |
| (t) | O. XL R. 1 or R. 4 | Appointment or removal of a receiver |
| (u) | O. XLI R. 23 or R. 23A | **Remand** |
| (w) | O. XLVII R. 4 | Granting an application for review |

Note the asymmetry in the last entry: an order **granting** review is appealable; an order **refusing** review is not. The remedy against a refusal is to appeal the original decree, if an appeal lies, or to move under Article 227.

::: warning What is conspicuously absent from the list
There is **no appeal** from an order rejecting a plaint's amendment application under Order VI Rule 17, from an order framing or refusing to frame issues, from an order on adjournment, from an order refusing review, or — most importantly — from an order **rejecting** an application under Order VII Rule 11. (An order *allowing* O. VII R. 11 rejects the plaint, which is a decree under s. 2(2), and carries a first appeal under s. 96.) For all of these, the routes are s. 105(1) at the end of the suit, or Article 227 immediately.
:::

#### 2.3 Section 105(1) — carrying the error forward

The mechanism is simple but under-used. When the trial court passes an interlocutory order that is wrong and not appealable — refusing an amendment, refusing to summon a witness, wrongly framing an issue — you do two things. First, record your objection at the time so the record shows it. Second, when the decree is appealed, set the error out as a specific ground in the memorandum of appeal under s. 105(1).

The threshold is that the error must have **affected the decision of the case**. Section 99 works in tandem: an irregularity that did not affect the merits or the jurisdiction will not upset the decree. So the ground must plead not merely that the order was wrong, but how it changed the outcome.

#### 2.4 Section 105(2) — the remand exception

Section 105(2) reverses the ordinary rule for one class of order. A remand order under Order XLI Rule 23 or 23A is appealable. If it is not appealed, the party is precluded from disputing its correctness later. The rationale is that a remand restarts the trial, and it would be intolerable to allow a party to litigate through a fresh trial and then say the trial should never have happened.

The preclusion applies only where an **appeal lay** from the remand order. A remand under the inherent power, or an order that is in substance not a Rule 23 remand, does not attract s. 105(2).

#### 2.5 Section 106 — which court hears the appeal from an order

The rule follows the decree. The appeal from an order goes to the court that would hear an appeal from the decree in that suit. Where the order was made by a court exercising appellate jurisdiction and that court is not a High Court, the appeal goes to the High Court. Combined with s. 104(2), this gives exactly one appeal from an order, in the forum determined by the suit's own valuation.

#### 2.6 Section 107 — the four powers

- **107(1)(a) Determine the case finally** — the ordinary course. Read with Order XLI Rule 24, where the evidence on record is sufficient the appellate court should decide rather than remand.
- **107(1)(b) Remand** — governed by Order XLI Rule 23 (where the suit was disposed of on a preliminary point and the decree is reversed) and Rule 23A (where the suit was disposed of otherwise than on a preliminary point but a retrial is considered necessary). Remand is exceptional; routine remands are discouraged because they restart limitation-free delay.
- **107(1)(c) Frame issues and refer for trial** — Order XLI Rule 25, where the lower court omitted to frame or try an issue essential to the right decision. The finding comes back with the evidence; the appellate court then decides.
- **107(1)(d) Take additional evidence** — Order XLI Rule 27, on the three grounds only: the lower court refused evidence that ought to have been admitted; the evidence was not within the party's knowledge or could not be produced despite due diligence; or the appellate court itself requires it to pronounce judgment. Rule 28 governs the mode, Rule 29 requires the points to be recorded.

Section 107(2) then assimilates the appellate court to a court of original jurisdiction for procedural purposes — which is why the appellate court can, for example, permit an amendment under Order VI Rule 17 or add a party under Order I Rule 10.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

::: tip Illustration 1 — sword: the injunction refused
The trial court refuses a temporary injunction under Order XXXIX Rules 1 and 2, and demolition is imminent. This is squarely within Order XLIII Rule 1(r). File the appeal at once, with an application for an interim order pending the appeal. Waiting for the decree is meaningless — the subject-matter will be gone.
:::

::: tip Illustration 2 — shield: no appeal from the refusal to reject a plaint
A defendant's application under Order VII Rule 11(d) that the suit is barred by limitation is dismissed. There is no appeal. The options are to move the High Court under Article 227 if the order is jurisdictionally perverse, or to plead the limitation bar as an issue, take it to trial, and — if the decree goes against the defendant — carry the error forward under s. 105(1). The second route is usually correct; Article 227 is not a substitute for the trial.
:::

::: tip Illustration 3 — the amendment refused
The plaintiff's application under Order VI Rule 17 to plead an alternative case of part performance is refused after the trial has begun. No appeal lies. Counsel records the objection on the order sheet, leads whatever evidence is admissible on the pleadings as they stand, and — on appeal from the decree — pleads under s. 105(1) that the refusal was contrary to the proviso to Rule 17 and affected the decision, since the alternative case would have answered the very ground on which the suit was dismissed.
:::

::: tip Illustration 4 — the remand accepted, the right lost
The first appellate court reverses a dismissal on the preliminary point of limitation and remands the suit for trial under Order XLI Rule 23. The defendant, confident of winning at trial, does not appeal. He loses at trial, loses the first appeal, and in second appeal contends that the remand was wrong because the suit was plainly time-barred. Section 105(2) shuts the argument out entirely.
:::

::: tip Illustration 5 — s. 107(1)(c) in a partition suit
In a partition appeal, the trial court framed no issue on the ouster pleaded by one co-sharer, although evidence was led. Rather than remanding the whole suit, the appellate court frames the issue under s. 107(1)(c) with Order XLI Rule 25, refers it to the trial court for a finding on the existing and any further evidence, and on receipt of the finding decides the appeal itself.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

#### Shah Babulal Khimji v. Jayaben D. Kania, (1981) 4 SCC 8

**Bench strength:** Three Judges.

*Facts:* Whether an interlocutory order of a single Judge on the original side of the Bombay High Court refusing a receiver and an injunction was a "judgment" for the purposes of a Letters Patent appeal.

**Ratio decidendi:** An interlocutory order is a "judgment" if it possesses the characteristics and trappings of finality — that is, if it affects vital and valuable rights of the parties and works serious injustice, rather than merely regulating the procedure of the suit. Orders on injunctions, receivers, attachment before judgment and the like fall on the appealable side; routine procedural directions do not.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The impugned order determines a valuable right and possesses the trappings of finality within *Shah Babulal Khimji*, and is therefore appealable notwithstanding its interlocutory form."
:::

#### Sushil Kumar Mehta v. Gobind Ram Bohra (Dead) through LRs, (1990) 1 SCC 193

**Bench strength:** Two Judges.

*Facts:* A decree passed by a civil court in a matter within the exclusive jurisdiction of a Rent Controller was sought to be executed; the objection of nullity was raised at execution.

**Ratio decidendi:** A decree passed by a court without inherent jurisdiction over the subject-matter is a nullity, and its invalidity may be set up at any stage, including in execution and in collateral proceedings. Consent, waiver or the failure to appeal cannot confer jurisdiction where the statute withholds it.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The decree is a nullity for want of inherent jurisdiction over the subject-matter, and within *Sushil Kumar Mehta* that objection is not cured by the failure to appeal."
:::

#### Surya Dev Rai v. Ram Chander Rai, (2003) 6 SCC 675

**Bench strength:** Two Judges.

*Facts:* Whether a High Court could exercise certiorari and supervisory jurisdiction against orders of civil courts after the curtailment of s. 115 by the 1999 amendment.

**Ratio decidendi:** The supervisory jurisdiction under Article 227 remains available against orders of civil courts and is not taken away by the amendment of s. 115, but it is to be exercised sparingly — only to keep subordinate courts within the bounds of their authority, not to correct mere errors of fact or law. *(The certiorari limb of this decision was later overruled; see* Radhey Shyam *below.)*

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Where no appeal lies under section 104 read with Order XLIII Rule 1, the supervisory jurisdiction under Article 227 survives, to be invoked only for a jurisdictional error and not as a substitute for an appeal."
:::

#### Jagdish Singh v. Natthu Singh, (1992) 1 SCC 647

**Bench strength:** Two Judges.

*Facts:* An appellate court's exercise of power to mould relief and take account of subsequent events in a suit for specific performance.

**Ratio decidendi:** The appellate court, exercising powers under s. 107 read with Order XLI, stands in the shoes of the trial court and may mould the relief to do complete justice between the parties, taking into account events subsequent to the institution of the suit where necessary to give effect to the rights as they stand at the date of the decree.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Under section 107(2) the appellate court has the same powers as the court of first instance and may mould the relief to take account of subsequent events."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
The full text of Order XLIII Rule 1 has been amended and clauses renumbered; check the current Rule and any High Court amendments before relying on a clause letter. Confirm citations and bench strength independently.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**Decision tree — an adverse order has just been passed**

1. Is it a **decree** within s. 2(2)? If yes, s. 96 first appeal. (Rejection of a plaint under O. VII R. 11 is a decree; so is a determination under s. 47.)
2. If it is an order, find it in **Order XLIII Rule 1** by Order and Rule. If it is there, appeal under s. 104(1)(i) — and note the limitation runs from the date of the order, not the decree.
3. If it is not there, is the consequence **irreversible** before the decree? If yes, consider **Article 227**, pleading a jurisdictional error and not merely an erroneous view.
4. If the consequence is not irreversible, **preserve it**: record the objection on the order sheet, and plead it under **s. 105(1)** in the appeal from the decree, showing how it affected the decision.
5. If it is a **remand order**, treat it as urgent. Appeal it or lose it under **s. 105(2)**.

**Model ground under section 105(1)**

> "That the learned Trial Court erred in rejecting the appellant's application dated ____ under Order VI Rule 17 CPC. The said order, though not independently appealable, is set forth as a ground of objection under section 105(1) CPC, the error having affected the decision of the case in that the amendment sought to plead ____ , and the suit has been dismissed on the very ground which the proposed amendment would have answered."

::: danger Five errors in handling orders
1. **Appealing what is not appealable.** The appeal is dismissed as not maintainable and, worse, limitation for the correct remedy runs meanwhile.
2. **Not appealing a remand.** Section 105(2) closes the point permanently.
3. **Treating Article 227 as an appeal.** A petition that argues the merits will be dismissed; plead jurisdictional excess or material irregularity.
4. **Failing to record the objection.** Section 105(1) is far harder to run when the record shows no contemporaneous protest.
5. **Missing the shorter limitation.** Appeals from orders run from the date of the order. Practitioners who wait for a certified copy of the decree find the appeal time-barred.
:::

## Section 114 with Order XLVII — review

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **114. Review.** — Subject as aforesaid, any person considering himself aggrieved —
>
> (a) by a decree or order from which an appeal is allowed by this Code, but from which no appeal has been preferred,
>
> (b) by a decree or order from which no appeal is allowed by this Code, or
>
> (c) by a decision on a reference from a Court of Small Causes,
>
> may apply for a review of judgment to the Court which passed the decree or made the order, and the Court may make such order thereon as it thinks fit.

> **Order XLVII Rule 1(1).** — Any person considering himself aggrieved… may apply for a review of judgment to the Court which passed the decree or made the order **on the discovery of new and important matter or evidence which, after the exercise of due diligence, was not within his knowledge or could not be produced by him at the time when the decree was passed or order made, or on account of some mistake or error apparent on the face of the record, or for any other sufficient reason**, and may make such order thereon as it thinks fit.
>
> **Explanation.** — The fact that the decision on a question of law on which the judgment of the Court is based has been reversed or modified by the subsequent decision of a superior Court in any other case, shall not be a ground for the review of such judgment.

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| s. 114 — "**to the Court which passed the decree**" | **Forum-fixing, mandatory** | Review lies to the same court, ideally the same judge (O. XLVII R. 5). It is not a challenge to a higher forum |
| s. 114(a) — "**from which no appeal has been preferred**" | **Election** | Where an appeal lies and has been filed, review is not available on the same grounds; the party must choose |
| s. 114 — "**may make such order thereon as it thinks fit**" | **Discretionary** | Even where a ground is made out, review is not a matter of right |
| O. XLVII R. 1 — "**after the exercise of due diligence**" | **Condition precedent** | Newly discovered evidence must be shown to have been undiscoverable earlier, on affidavit |
| O. XLVII R. 1 — "**error apparent on the face of the record**" | **Narrow gateway** | The error must be self-evident, not one established by long argument |
| O. XLVII R. 1 — "**any other sufficient reason**" | **Ejusdem generis** | Read as analogous to the two specified grounds, not as a residual discretion at large |
| Explanation to R. 1 | **Express exclusion** | A later contrary decision of a superior court in *another* case is not a ground for review |

::: danger Review is not an appeal in disguise
The single proposition that decides most review applications: an erroneous decision may be corrected by a superior court in appeal; it cannot be corrected by the same court on review. If the error requires argument to establish, it is not apparent on the face of the record.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

#### 2.1 The three grounds, separated

**(a) Discovery of new and important matter or evidence.** Three cumulative requirements: the matter must be *new* (not on the record), *important* (capable of altering the result), and it must be shown that despite *due diligence* it was not within the applicant's knowledge or could not be produced earlier. The third requirement is where most applications fail — evidence that was available but not led is not newly discovered.

**(b) Mistake or error apparent on the face of the record.** The error must be visible on a mere inspection: an arithmetical mistake in computing mesne profits; a decree that grants relief never claimed; a judgment that proceeds on a statutory provision that stood repealed; a finding that contradicts an admitted fact recorded in the same judgment. An error that requires the court to re-examine the evidence, or to hear a fresh argument on the construction of a statute, is not apparent — it may well be an error, but its correction lies in appeal.

**(c) Any other sufficient reason.** Read *ejusdem generis* with the first two — a reason sufficient on grounds at least analogous to those specified. Recognised instances include a judgment passed without notice to a party, or a decree drawn up contrary to the judgment pronounced.

#### 2.2 What review cannot do

- It cannot **rehear the merits**. A different view of the same evidence is not a ground.
- It cannot be used to raise a **new argument** that could have been raised at the hearing.
- It cannot be founded on a **later decision of a superior court in another case** — the Explanation to Rule 1 forecloses this expressly. (A decision of a superior court *in the same case*, however, is a different matter.)
- It cannot **substitute for an appeal** where an appeal lies and limitation has expired. Delay in appealing is not a ground for review.

#### 2.3 The relationship between review and appeal

Section 114(a) contemplates review where an appeal is allowed but has not been preferred; s. 114(b) where no appeal is allowed. Order XLVII Rule 1(2) adds the corollary: where an appeal has been preferred, review of the same decree by the lower court on grounds available in the appeal is not maintainable. If the appeal is disposed of, the appellate decree supersedes the original, and the review must go to the appellate court.

The practical consequence is that the two remedies must be sequenced deliberately. Filing a review does not extend the time for appeal, though the time spent prosecuting a review can be relevant to a s. 5 application — a point to be argued, not assumed.

#### 2.4 Procedure under Order XLVII

- **Rule 2** (as it stands after amendment) — the application is made to the court that passed the decree.
- **Rule 3** — the application follows the form of an appeal, so far as applicable.
- **Rule 4** — the court may reject the application at once if there is no sufficient ground; if it sees a sufficient ground it grants the application, but must issue notice to the opposite party before doing so where the application is on the ground of newly discovered evidence.
- **Rule 5** — where the judge who passed the decree is still attached to the court, the application should ordinarily go before him, unless he is precluded by absence or other cause.
- **Rule 7** — an order **rejecting** the application is **not appealable**; an order **granting** it is appealable under Order XLIII Rule 1(w), on the limited grounds in Rule 7(1)(a)–(c).
- **Rule 8** — where the application is granted, the court proceeds to rehear the case.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

::: tip Illustration 1 — a genuine error apparent
A money decree computes interest at 12% per annum from the date of the suit but the decretal figure has been worked out at 12% from the date of the loan, three years earlier. The mismatch between the operative direction and the arithmetic is visible on the face of the decree. Review lies, and is the correct remedy — an appeal on the point would be disproportionate and slower.
:::

::: tip Illustration 2 — not an error apparent
A defendant contends in review that the court applied Article 58 of the Limitation Act when Article 59 was the correct article. Establishing this requires a construction of the plaint and an argument on which article governs. However strong the point, it is not apparent on the face of the record. The remedy is appeal.
:::

::: tip Illustration 3 — sword: relief not claimed
The decree directs delivery of possession, but the plaint sought only a permanent injunction and no relief of possession was ever prayed for or paid court fee on. The decree grants what was not claimed — an error visible from a comparison of the plaint and the decree. Review is available, and is faster than an appeal.
:::

::: tip Illustration 4 — shield: resisting review
Where the opposite party files a review that in substance re-argues the evidence, the respondent's answer is short: identify each ground, show that it requires re-appreciation, and cite the rule that an erroneous decision is correctable in appeal alone. Add, where applicable, that the applicant has already filed an appeal, attracting Order XLVII Rule 1(2).
:::

::: tip Illustration 5 — the Explanation in action
Six months after a decree construing a statutory provision, the Supreme Court in an unrelated matter takes the contrary view. The losing party applies for review. The Explanation to Order XLVII Rule 1 bars it in terms. The only route is an appeal, if one is still open, or a s. 5 application supported by the change in law as sufficient cause — an argument on condonation, not on review.
:::

| Grounds | **O. XLVII R. 1(1)** | Plead the specific ground; do not plead all three in the alternative without particulars |
| Newly discovered evidence | **O. XLVII R. 1(1)** first limb | Affidavit must set out the diligence exercised and why the material could not be produced earlier |
| Appeal already filed | **O. XLVII R. 1(2)** | Review by the lower court on the same grounds is not maintainable |
| Later contrary ruling in another case | **Explanation to R. 1** | Not a ground |
| Which judge hears it | **O. XLVII R. 5** | Ordinarily the judge who passed the decree |
| Rejection of review | **O. XLVII R. 7(1)** | **Not appealable.** Remedy is Article 227, or the appeal from the original decree if still open |
| Grant of review | **O. XLIII R. 1(w)** with **O. XLVII R. 7** | Appealable, but only on the limited grounds specified |
| Limitation | **Article 124, Limitation Act, 1963** | Thirty days from the date of the decree or order. Verify against the current Schedule |
| Correcting a clerical slip instead | **s. 152** | Arithmetical or clerical errors and accidental slips are corrected under s. 152, with no limitation — often the better route |
| Amendment of the decree | **s. 153** | General power to amend defects in proceedings |

::: warning Section 152 before section 114
Where the grievance is a clerical or arithmetical mistake, or an accidental slip in drawing up the decree, use **section 152**, not review. There is no period of limitation for a s. 152 application, the court can act *suo motu*, and it avoids the discretionary hurdles of Order XLVII. Reserve review for substantive errors apparent on the record.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

#### Satyanarayan Laxminarayan Hegde v. Mallikarjun Bhavanappa Tirumale, AIR 1960 SC 137

**Bench strength:** Five Judges (Constitution Bench).

*Facts:* Whether an alleged error in a tribunal's decision could be corrected by certiorari as an error apparent on the face of the record.

**Ratio decidendi:** An error apparent on the face of the record must be one that is self-evident and does not require an elaborate argument to establish. Where two opinions are reasonably possible on the point, the error, if any, is not apparent. This test governs the corresponding expression in Order XLVII Rule 1.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"An error which has to be established by a long-drawn process of reasoning on points on which there may conceivably be two opinions is not an error apparent on the face of the record — *Satyanarayan Hegde*."
:::

#### Parsion Devi v. Sumitri Devi, (1997) 8 SCC 715

**Bench strength:** Two Judges.

*Facts:* A trial court reviewed and reversed its own earlier order in effect on a fresh appreciation of the merits.

**Ratio decidendi:** A review petition has a limited purpose and cannot be allowed to be an appeal in disguise. Under Order XLVII Rule 1 the power can be exercised only for correction of a patent error, and not to correct an erroneous decision, which is the function of a superior court in appeal.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The review sought is an appeal in disguise; under *Parsion Devi* the power under Order XLVII Rule 1 extends only to a patent error and not to an erroneous decision."
:::

#### Board of Control for Cricket in India v. Netaji Cricket Club, (2005) 4 SCC 741

**Bench strength:** Two Judges.

*Facts:* Whether a court could review a consent order recorded on a misapprehension of the true position placed before it.

**Ratio decidendi:** "Sufficient reason" in Order XLVII Rule 1 is wide enough to include a case where the order was passed on a mistaken assumption of fact or of the parties' position, or where the court's own order requires correction to give effect to what was actually intended. The power is not confined to the two specified grounds, though it must be exercised within their analogy.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The expression 'any other sufficient reason' in Order XLVII Rule 1 covers an order passed on a mistaken assumption of fact — *BCCI v. Netaji Cricket Club*."
:::

#### Kamlesh Verma v. Mayawati, (2013) 8 SCC 320

**Bench strength:** Two Judges.

*Facts:* A review of a judgment quashing criminal proceedings, sought on grounds amounting to a re-argument.

**Ratio decidendi:** The judgment consolidates the law into a working catalogue — review is maintainable on discovery of new and important matter, on a mistake or error apparent on the face of the record, or for any other sufficient reason analogous to these; and it is **not** maintainable as a repetition of old and overruled arguments, as a minor mistake of inconsequential import, as a re-hearing on the merits, or where the error must be detected by a process of reasoning.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Applying the catalogue in *Kamlesh Verma*, the present application falls within the class of cases in which review is not maintainable, being a rehearing on the merits."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Order XLVII has been amended, and Rule 7 in particular differs across High Court amendments on the appealability of orders on review. Confirm the current text, the citations and bench strength before relying on them.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**Drafting a review application that survives Rule 4**

1. Open by identifying the **precise ground** under Order XLVII Rule 1 — one of the three, named.
2. If the ground is newly discovered evidence, devote a full paragraph to **due diligence**: what was searched, when, why the material surfaced only now. Support by affidavit.
3. If the ground is error apparent, state it in **one sentence** and point to the two places on the record that contradict each other. If it takes a page to explain, it is not apparent.
4. State expressly whether an **appeal has been preferred** — Order XLVII Rule 1(2) makes this material, and concealing it is fatal.
5. Address **limitation** under Article 124 in the body, or file a s. 5 application.
6. Confine the prayer to review of the specific finding or direction; do not pray for the whole judgment to be recalled unless that is genuinely the consequence.

::: danger Five reasons review applications are dismissed
1. **It is an appeal in disguise** — the grounds re-argue the evidence.
2. **No due diligence pleaded** for the "newly discovered" material.
3. **The error is not apparent** — it takes argument to establish.
4. **An appeal is already pending** on the same grounds, attracting Order XLVII Rule 1(2).
5. **Reliance on a later judgment** in another case, barred by the Explanation to Rule 1.
:::

## Section 115 with Article 227 — revision

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **115. Revision.** — (1) The High Court may call for the record of any case which has been decided by any Court subordinate to such High Court and in which no appeal lies thereto, and if such subordinate Court appears —
>
> (a) to have **exercised a jurisdiction not vested in it by law**, or
>
> (b) to have **failed to exercise a jurisdiction so vested**, or
>
> (c) to have acted in the exercise of its jurisdiction **illegally or with material irregularity**,
>
> the High Court may make such order in the case as it thinks fit:
>
> **Provided that the High Court shall not, under this section, vary or reverse any order made, or any order deciding an issue, in the course of a suit or other proceeding, except where the order, if it had been made in favour of the party applying for revision, would have finally disposed of the suit or other proceeding.**
>
> (2) The High Court shall not, under this section, vary or reverse any decree or order against which an appeal lies either to the High Court or to any Court subordinate thereto.
>
> (3) A revision shall not operate as a stay of suit or other proceeding before the Court except where such suit or other proceeding is stayed by the High Court.
>
> *Explanation.* — In this section, the expression "any case which has been decided" includes any order made, or any order deciding an issue, in the course of a suit or other proceeding.

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| s. 115(1) — "**The High Court may call for the record**" | **Discretionary supervisory power** | Revision is not a right of the litigant; it is a power of the court, invoked by application |
| s. 115(1) — "**in which no appeal lies thereto**" | **Condition precedent** | Where an appeal lies to the High Court, revision is excluded |
| (a), (b), (c) — jurisdictional triad | **Exhaustive grounds** | Only these three. An error of fact, or even of law, within jurisdiction is outside s. 115 |
| Proviso (post-1999) — "**would have finally disposed of the suit**" | **Mandatory limitation** | This is the amendment that gutted s. 115. Unless a decision the other way would have ended the suit, revision does not lie |
| s. 115(2) — "**shall not… where an appeal lies**" | **Absolute bar** | Revision cannot be an alternative to an available appeal |
| s. 115(3) — "**shall not operate as a stay**" | **Default rule** | The suit continues below unless the High Court expressly stays it |

::: danger The 1999 amendment changed the map
Before 1999 the proviso to s. 115 allowed revision where the order would have finally disposed of the suit **or** where it would occasion a failure of justice or irreparable injury. The second limb was deleted with effect from 1 July 2002. The result is that most interlocutory orders are now outside s. 115 altogether, and the practical supervisory work has migrated to **Article 227 of the Constitution**. Any authority predating the amendment must be read with this in mind.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

#### 2.1 The three grounds, precisely

**(a) Exercised a jurisdiction not vested in it by law.** The court did something it had no power to do — decided a matter within the exclusive jurisdiction of a tribunal, entertained a suit beyond its pecuniary limits, passed an order against a person not before it.

**(b) Failed to exercise a jurisdiction so vested.** The court declined to act when it was bound to — refused to entertain an application on a mistaken view that it had no power, declined to decide an issue it was obliged to decide.

**(c) Acted illegally or with material irregularity in the exercise of jurisdiction.** This is the most litigated and the most misunderstood limb. It looks at **how** the jurisdiction was exercised, not at whether the conclusion was right. Deciding without hearing a party, deciding on evidence taken behind a party's back, ignoring a mandatory statutory procedure — these are material irregularities. Reaching a wrong conclusion after a proper hearing is not.

The classical formulation: a court has jurisdiction to decide rightly and also to decide wrongly. If it has jurisdiction, an erroneous decision within it is not revisable.

#### 2.2 The proviso — the finality filter

After 1999, an interlocutory order is revisable only if the order, **had it been made the other way**, would have finally disposed of the suit. This is a counterfactual test and it is applied strictly.

- An order **rejecting** an application under Order VII Rule 11: had it been allowed, the plaint would stand rejected and the suit would be over. The proviso is satisfied — revision lies.
- An order **rejecting** an application under Order VI Rule 17 for amendment: had it been allowed, the suit would have continued with an amended plaint. The suit would not have been disposed of. Revision does **not** lie.
- An order refusing to frame an additional issue: the suit continues either way. Revision does not lie.

This test is where most revisions are lost, and it must be addressed in the opening paragraphs of the petition.

#### 2.3 Section 115 and Article 227 — the current position

Article 227 confers on every High Court superintendence over all courts and tribunals within its territorial jurisdiction. It is a constitutional power and cannot be curtailed by an amendment to the Code. So where s. 115 no longer reaches, Article 227 may — but on its own, equally narrow, terms.

The position after *Radhey Shyam v. Chhabi Nath* (2015):

- **Certiorari under Article 226 does not lie against orders of civil courts.** A civil court is not a tribunal amenable to certiorari; the judicial orders of civil courts are amenable only to the supervisory jurisdiction under Article 227. To this extent *Surya Dev Rai* stands overruled.
- **Article 227 remains available**, to be exercised sparingly, to keep subordinate courts within the bounds of their authority — not to correct errors of fact, and not as a substitute for an appeal.

The drafting consequence is precise: a petition against a civil court's order should be styled under **Article 227**, not Article 226, and must plead a jurisdictional error or a patent perversity, not merely an erroneous view.

#### 2.4 What revision is not

- Not an appeal. No re-appreciation of evidence.
- Not available where an appeal lies to the High Court — s. 115(2).
- Not a stay. The trial continues below unless the High Court orders otherwise — s. 115(3).
- Not a remedy for delay in appealing. An expired appeal does not convert into a revision.
- Not available against orders of a High Court itself; s. 115 speaks of courts *subordinate* to the High Court.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

::: tip Illustration 1 — the proviso satisfied
A defendant's application under Order VII Rule 11(d) contending that the suit is barred by a statutory bar is rejected. Had it been allowed, the plaint would have been rejected and the suit ended. The proviso is satisfied, and a revision under s. 115 is maintainable — provided the ground pleaded is jurisdictional or a material irregularity, not merely that the trial court took a wrong view of limitation.
:::

::: tip Illustration 2 — the proviso not satisfied
An application to summon a witness is refused. The suit continues whichever way the application is decided. Section 115 is unavailable. If the refusal is so unreasonable as to deny a fair trial, Article 227 may be invoked; otherwise, preserve it under s. 105(1) for the appeal from the decree.
:::

::: tip Illustration 3 — sword: jurisdiction not vested
A Munsif's court with a pecuniary ceiling of ₹2,00,000 proceeds with a suit valued at ₹8,00,000 after refusing the defendant's objection. This is limb (a) — a jurisdiction not vested in it by law — and the order refusing the objection, had it been allowed, would have taken the suit out of that court. Revision lies, and the objection must also be preserved under s. 21 for the eventual appeal.
:::

::: tip Illustration 4 — shield: resisting a revision
For the respondent, the first line of defence is almost always the proviso. Show that whichever way the impugned application had been decided, the suit would have continued. The second line is limb (c): show that the trial court heard both sides, applied its mind and reached a conclusion — and that a wrong conclusion within jurisdiction is not a material irregularity.
:::

::: tip Illustration 5 — Article 227 correctly invoked
A trial court proceeds to decree a suit after striking off the defence, without notice of the application on which the defence was struck. This is a denial of hearing — a jurisdictional error going to the root. Whether or not the proviso to s. 115 is satisfied, a petition under Article 227 pleading breach of natural justice is the correct and available remedy.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

#### D.L.F. Housing & Construction Co. (P) Ltd. v. Sarup Singh, (1969) 3 SCC 807

**Bench strength:** Three Judges.

*Facts:* A High Court in revision interfered with a subordinate court's conclusion on the merits of an award proceeding.

**Ratio decidendi:** The words "illegally" and "with material irregularity" in s. 115(1)(c) do not cover an error of fact or of law; they refer to the *manner* in which the jurisdiction is exercised — the procedure adopted or the failure to comply with a mandatory requirement. Where the subordinate court has jurisdiction and has decided after hearing the parties, its conclusion, right or wrong, is not revisable.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Clause (c) of section 115(1) is directed to the manner of exercise of jurisdiction and not to the correctness of the conclusion — *D.L.F. Housing v. Sarup Singh*."
:::

#### Baldevdas Shivlal v. Filmistan Distributors (India) (P) Ltd., (1969) 2 SCC 201

**Bench strength:** Three Judges.

*Facts:* Whether an interlocutory order in a pending suit was a "case which has been decided" for the purposes of s. 115.

**Ratio decidendi:** A case may be said to be decided if the court adjudicates for the purposes of the suit some right or obligation of the parties in controversy. Not every interlocutory direction qualifies. *(The Explanation later inserted in s. 115 now defines the expression to include an order deciding an issue in the course of a suit — read the decision alongside the current statutory Explanation.)*

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The impugned order adjudicates a right in controversy and is therefore a 'case decided' within the Explanation to section 115."
:::

#### Surya Dev Rai v. Ram Chander Rai, (2003) 6 SCC 675

**Bench strength:** Two Judges.

*Facts:* The availability of Articles 226 and 227 against civil court orders after the curtailment of s. 115.

**Ratio decidendi:** Held that both certiorari under Article 226 and supervision under Article 227 were available against civil court orders, to be used sparingly. **The certiorari limb was subsequently overruled** in *Radhey Shyam*; the Article 227 limb and the caution about sparing use survive.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
Cite only for the surviving proposition: "the amendment of section 115 does not curtail the constitutional jurisdiction under Article 227, which remains available but is to be exercised sparingly."
:::

#### Radhey Shyam v. Chhabi Nath, (2015) 5 SCC 423

**Bench strength:** Three Judges.

*Facts:* A reference to resolve whether the judicial orders of civil courts could be challenged by a writ of certiorari under Article 226.

**Ratio decidendi:** Judicial orders of civil courts are **not** amenable to a writ of certiorari under Article 226. Such orders can be challenged only under Article 227, the supervisory jurisdiction. *Surya Dev Rai* is overruled to the extent it held otherwise.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Following *Radhey Shyam v. Chhabi Nath*, the judicial order of a civil court is challengeable only under Article 227 and not by certiorari under Article 226; this petition is accordingly styled under Article 227."
:::

#### Shalini Shyam Shetty v. Rajendra Shankar Patil, (2010) 8 SCC 329

**Bench strength:** Two Judges.

*Facts:* A High Court exercised Article 227 jurisdiction in a manner amounting to an appellate reappraisal of a civil dispute.

**Ratio decidendi:** Article 227 is a power of superintendence, to be exercised most sparingly and only in appropriate cases to keep subordinate courts within the bounds of their authority. It is not appellate; the High Court cannot, under Article 227, review or reweigh the evidence, and a petition under Article 227 must be styled as such and not as a writ petition.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Article 227 confers a power of superintendence and not of appeal; interference is confined to keeping the subordinate court within the bounds of its authority — *Shalini Shyam Shetty*."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Section 115 has been amended and several States have their own versions materially different from the central text. Verify the applicable text, the current Limitation Act article, and all citations before filing.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**Structuring a revision petition**

1. **Maintainability first.** Paragraph one: no appeal lies to this Hon'ble Court against the impugned order (s. 115(2) answered). Paragraph two: had the application been allowed, the suit would have stood finally disposed of (the proviso answered). If you cannot write paragraph two honestly, file under Article 227 instead.
2. **Name the limb.** State whether the case is (a), (b) or (c), and plead the facts that constitute it. Do not plead all three indiscriminately.
3. **Do not argue the merits.** Every sentence inviting the High Court to prefer your view of the evidence weakens the petition.
4. **Apply for stay separately** — s. 115(3).
5. **Limitation** — ninety days under Article 131; plead the date of knowledge and the date of the certified copy.

**Choosing between section 115 and Article 227**

| Feature | Section 115 | Article 227 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source | Statute — can be and has been curtailed | Constitution — cannot be curtailed by statute |
| Finality filter | Yes — the proviso | No, but sparing use is the governing discipline |
| Grounds | Jurisdiction: exceeded, declined, or irregularly exercised | Jurisdictional error, patent perversity, breach of natural justice |
| Against whom | Courts subordinate to the High Court | All courts and tribunals within the territory |
| Merits review | None | None |

::: danger Five ways a revision is thrown out at admission
1. **An appeal lay** — s. 115(2) bars the revision outright.
2. **The proviso is not satisfied** — the suit would have continued either way.
3. **The petition argues the merits** rather than jurisdiction.
4. **Wrong label** — a writ petition under Article 226 against a civil court's judicial order, contrary to *Radhey Shyam*.
5. **No stay applied for**, so the trial concludes below and the revision is rendered infructuous.
:::

## Consolidating the chapter

| Situation | Remedy | Governing provision | Scope of scrutiny |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Decree of a court of original jurisdiction | First appeal | **s. 96** with O. XLI | Facts and law — a full rehearing |
| Consent decree | **No appeal** | **s. 96(3)** | Challenge only by a suit or an application under the O. XXIII R. 3 proviso |
| Preliminary decree unappealed | Barred at the final decree stage | **s. 97** | — |
| Ex parte decree | First appeal **or** O. IX R. 13 | **s. 96(2)** | Both doors are open; the choice has consequences |
| Decree of a first appellate court | Second appeal | **s. 100** | **Substantial question of law only** |
| Decision of a single Judge in appeal | **No further appeal** | **s. 100A** | Supreme Court alone |
| Order listed in O. XLIII R. 1 | Appeal from order | **s. 104(1)(i)** | One appeal only — s. 104(2) |
| Order not listed | Preserve and carry forward | **s. 105(1)** | Must have affected the decision |
| Remand order not appealed | **Preclusion** | **s. 105(2)** | Point lost permanently |
| Error apparent on the record | Review | **s. 114**, O. XLVII | Patent error only, before the same court |
| Clerical or arithmetical slip | Correction | **s. 152** | No limitation; often better than review |
| Jurisdictional error, no appeal lying | Revision | **s. 115** | Subject to the finality proviso |
| Jurisdictional error outside s. 115 | Supervision | **Article 227** | Sparing; never appellate |

::: tip The seven sentences worth memorising
1. **There is no natural right of appeal** — every appellate remedy is a creature of statute and exists only to the extent the statute allows.
2. **The first appellate court is the final court of fact.** Second appeal reaches only a substantial question of law, and s. 100(4) formulation is jurisdictional.
3. **Section 96(3) bars an appeal from a consent decree** — the challenge lies before the court that recorded it, under the proviso to Order XXIII Rule 3.
4. **Order XLIII Rule 1 is exhaustive.** An order not in the list carries no independent appeal; s. 105(1) preserves it for the appeal from the decree.
5. **A remand order must be appealed** — s. 105(2) precludes the point forever if it is not.
6. **Review corrects a patent error, not an erroneous decision.** If it takes argument to establish, it is not apparent on the face of the record.
7. **Revision looks at jurisdiction, never at correctness** — and since 1999, only where a contrary order would have finally disposed of the suit.
:::

::: warning Before you file any appellate proceeding
1. Classify the decision — **decree or order** — and, if a decree, whether preliminary or final.
2. Locate the **enabling provision** by number: s. 96, s. 100, s. 104(1)(i) with the O. XLIII R. 1 clause, s. 114, or s. 115.
3. Compute **limitation** from the correct date, with the s. 12(2) exclusion for the time taken to obtain copies, and file a s. 5 application if there is any delay.
4. File a **separate application for stay** — no appeal or revision stays execution or the trial by its own force.
5. In a second appeal, draft the **substantial question of law first**; the grounds follow from it.
6. In a review, plead the **specific ground** and, for newly discovered evidence, the **due diligence**.
7. In a revision, answer the **proviso** in the opening paragraphs, or file under Article 227 instead.
8. Verify every citation, the current text of the section, and any **State amendment** — particularly to ss. 100 and 115 — before filing.
:::
