# Sections 36 to 74 — Execution

A decree is a declaration of entitlement. It is not money in the bank, possession of the land, or an injunction anyone has obeyed. Part II of the Code is the machinery that converts the declaration into the thing itself — and it is where the largest share of civil litigation quietly dies. A suit decreed in year eleven can sit unexecuted through year twenty, and the decree-holder's real adversary by then is not the judgment-debtor's defence but the procedural architecture of execution itself.

This Part governs three questions in sequence: **which court** executes, **what that court may decide**, and **by what modes** it may compel. Everything else is detail hanging off those three.

| Provision | One-line function |
| --- | --- |
| s. 36 | Applies the execution provisions to orders as well as decrees |
| s. 37 | Defines "the Court which passed a decree" — wider than it first appears |
| s. 38 | A decree is executed by the court that passed it, **or** the court to which it is sent |
| s. 39 | Transfer of a decree to another court, and the limits on that power |
| s. 42 | The transferee court has the same powers as if it had passed the decree |
| s. 47 | **All questions relating to execution are decided by the executing court, not by fresh suit** |
| s. 51 | The menu of modes: delivery, attachment and sale, arrest, receiver, "such other manner" |
| ss. 52–54 | Execution against legal representatives; partition of estates |
| ss. 55–59 | Arrest and detention in civil prison |
| s. 60 | Property liable to attachment — and the exempted classes in the proviso |
| s. 64 | Private alienation after attachment is void against claims under the attachment |
| s. 73 | Rateable distribution among decree-holders |
| s. 74 | Resistance to execution |

::: info The architecture in one breath
Section 38 tells you **where** to file. Section 39 lets you move the decree to where the assets or the judgment-debtor actually are. Section 47 then makes that court the *exclusive* forum for every dispute about the decree's execution, discharge or satisfaction — barring a separate suit. Section 51 hands that court its instruments, with the proviso to clause (b) imposing a condition precedent before the harshest of them. Section 60 defines what can be seized, section 64 stops the judgment-debtor from selling ahead of the attachment, and section 73 divides the proceeds when several decree-holders are chasing the same fund.
:::

::: warning A structural point most students miss
Part II is the *skeleton*. Order XXI — 106 rules, the longest Order in the Code — is the flesh. Sections 36 to 74 confer the powers; Order XXI prescribes how each is invoked, by what application, on what notice, and subject to what objection. Neither is usable without the other, and an execution petition drafted from the sections alone will fail on procedure.
:::

## Sections 36 to 39 and 42 — which court executes

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **36. Application to orders.** — The provisions of this Code relating to the execution of decrees (including provisions relating to payment under a decree) shall, so far as they are applicable, be deemed to apply to the execution of orders (including payment under an order).

> **37. Definition of Court which passed a decree.** — The expression "Court which passed a decree", or words to that effect, shall, in relation to the execution of decrees, unless there is anything repugnant in the subject or context, be deemed to include, —
> (a) where the decree to be executed has been passed in the exercise of appellate jurisdiction, the Court of first instance, and
> (b) where the Court of first instance has ceased to exist or to have jurisdiction to execute it, the Court which, if the suit wherein the decree was passed was instituted at the time of making the application for the execution of the decree, would have jurisdiction to try such suit.
>
> *Explanation.* — The Court of first instance does not cease to have jurisdiction to execute a decree merely on the ground that after the institution of the suit wherein the decree was passed or after the passing of the decree, any area has been transferred from the jurisdiction of that Court to the jurisdiction of any other Court; but, in every such case, such other Court shall also have jurisdiction to execute the decree, if at the time of making the application for execution of the decree it would have jurisdiction to try the said suit.

> **38. Court by which decree may be executed.** — A decree may be executed either by the Court which passed it, or by the Court to which it is sent for execution.

> **39. Transfer of decree.** — (1) The Court which passed a decree may, on the application of the decree-holder, send it for execution to another Court of competent jurisdiction, —
> (a) if the person against whom the decree is passed actually and voluntarily resides or carries on business, or personally works for gain, within the local limits of the jurisdiction of such other Court, or
> (b) if such person has not property within the local limits of the jurisdiction of the Court which passed the decree sufficient to satisfy such decree and has property within the local limits of the jurisdiction of such other Court, or
> (c) if the decree directs the sale or delivery of immovable property situate outside the local limits of the jurisdiction of the Court which passed it, or
> (d) if the Court which passed the decree considers for any other reason, which it shall record in writing, that the decree should be executed by such other Court.
>
> (2) The Court which passed a decree may of its own motion send it for execution to any subordinate Court of competent jurisdiction.
>
> (3) For the purposes of this section, a Court shall be deemed to be a Court of competent jurisdiction if, at the time of making the application for the transfer of decree to it, such Court would have jurisdiction to try the suit in which such decree was passed.
>
> (4) Nothing in this section shall be deemed to authorise the Court which passed a decree to execute such decree against any person or property outside the local limits of the jurisdiction of such Court.

> **42. Powers of Court in executing transferred decree.** — (1) The Court executing a decree sent to it shall have the same powers in executing such decree as if it had been passed by itself. All persons disobeying or obstructing the execution of the decree shall be punishable by such Court in the same manner as if it had passed the decree. And its order in executing such decree shall be subject to the same rules in respect of appeal as if the decree had been passed by itself.

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| s. 36 — "shall … be deemed to apply" | **Mandatory, deeming** | Orders (including interlocutory ones awarding costs or maintenance) are executable exactly as decrees |
| s. 37 — "shall … be deemed to include" | **Deeming, inclusive** | Expands the forum; the appellate decree is executed by the *trial* court, not the appellate court |
| s. 38 — "either … or" | **Enabling, disjunctive** | Two competent forums exist simultaneously; the decree-holder chooses |
| s. 39(1) — "may, on the application" | **Discretionary, and party-driven** | The court does not transfer suo motu under sub-s. (1); the decree-holder must apply |
| s. 39(1)(d) — "which it shall record in writing" | **Mandatory condition on the discretion** | A residuary transfer without recorded reasons is vulnerable |
| s. 39(4) — "Nothing … shall be deemed to authorise" | **Absolute jurisdictional bar** | The transferring court cannot itself execute against person or property outside its limits |
| s. 42(1) — "same powers … as if it had been passed by itself" | **Full conferral** | The transferee court is not a mere agent; it exercises original execution powers |

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — why section 37 exists at all.** When a suit is dismissed at trial and decreed on appeal, the operative decree is the appellate decree. A literal reading would send the decree-holder to the High Court to execute. Section 37(a) prevents that absurdity: the decree of the appellate court is executed by the **court of first instance**. Appellate courts decide; trial courts execute.

Section 37(b) and the Explanation deal with the passage of time. Courts are abolished, districts are reorganised, pecuniary limits are revised. The provision keeps the decree alive by transferring executability to whichever court *would today* have jurisdiction to try the suit. Note the Explanation's careful effect: territorial reorganisation does **not** strip the original court of its power — it *adds* a second competent court. The decree-holder gains an option; the judgment-debtor gains no objection.

**Sub-module B — section 38 gives you a choice, and the choice matters.** The decree-holder may proceed in the court that passed the decree, or have it transferred. This is a tactical decision, not a formality. Executing where the decree was passed is faster to start — no transfer application, no transmission delay — but is useless if the debtor and his assets sit three states away. Transfer costs weeks at the front end and buys the ability to actually attach something.

**Sub-module C — the four gateways to transfer under section 39(1).** Clauses (a) to (c) are objective and factual: the debtor *resides or carries on business* elsewhere; there is *insufficient property* here and property there; the decree concerns *immovable property situate* elsewhere. Clause (d) is the residuary — any other reason, but only with reasons recorded in writing. Draft your transfer application to fit (a), (b) or (c) if the facts allow; a (d) application invites the court to weigh convenience and invites the debtor to contest.

**Sub-module D — section 39(4), the trap.** The sub-section was inserted to settle a conflict, and it is categorical: the court which passed the decree cannot execute *against person or property outside its local limits*. Read together with sub-s. (1), the scheme is coherent — if the assets are elsewhere, you do not ask the decreeing court to reach out; you ask it to **send the decree** to the court where the assets are. Attempting the former produces an order without jurisdiction.

**Sub-module E — section 42 and what the transferee court can do.** The transferee court is not a postbox. It has the same powers as if it had passed the decree: it can attach, sell, arrest, appoint a receiver, punish obstruction, and its orders carry the same appeal rights. But its powers are powers *of execution*. It cannot review the decree, cannot alter it, and cannot entertain what is in substance a challenge to the correctness of the adjudication — that is the domain of section 47, and even there the limits are severe.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — the appellate decree.** A suit for recovery of ₹40 lakhs is dismissed by the Civil Judge, Senior Division, Pune. The District Court allows the appeal and decrees the suit. The decree-holder files execution in the District Court. It is returned. Under s. 37(a), execution lies before the **Civil Judge, Senior Division, Pune** — the court of first instance — notwithstanding that the operative decree is the appellate one.

**Illustration 2 — assets in another state.** A money decree is passed at Delhi. The judgment-debtor's only substantial asset is a flat in Bengaluru. *As sword:* the decree-holder applies under s. 39(1)(b), pleading — with specifics — that the debtor has no sufficient property within Delhi's limits and owns identified immovable property within the jurisdiction of the City Civil Court, Bengaluru. The decree is sent; the Bengaluru court attaches and sells under s. 42. *The error to avoid:* filing at Delhi and asking the Delhi court to attach the Bengaluru flat. Section 39(4) bars it outright.

**Illustration 3 — reorganisation of districts.** A decree is passed by the Civil Judge, Gurgaon, in 2014. In 2018 the area where the debtor resides is carved into a new district. *As shield*, the judgment-debtor objects that the Gurgaon court is now coram non judice. The objection fails: the Explanation to s. 37 preserves the original court's jurisdiction and merely adds the new court as an alternative forum.

**Illustration 4 — executing an interlocutory order.** An order under s. 151 directs the defendant to pay ₹25,000 costs. He does not. *As sword:* s. 36 makes the execution provisions applicable to orders, so the costs order is executed as a decree — no separate suit, no fresh cause of action.

**Illustration 5 — decree for delivery of land outside the limits.** A decree for possession of agricultural land in District B is passed by a court in District A (which had jurisdiction because the defendant resided there and leave was obtained). Execution must go to District B under s. 39(1)(c); the District A court cannot deliver possession of land beyond its territorial reach.

### 4. Procedural Interlocking Matrix

| Substantive provision | Corresponding Order / Rule | Practical trigger |
| --- | --- | --- |
| s. 38 (choice of forum) | O. XXI R. 10 — application for execution; R. 11 — its contents | The execution petition must state the mode sought and the relief |
| s. 39 (transfer) | O. XXI RR. 5–9; R. 6 — what the transferring court sends | Certified copy of decree, certificate of non-satisfaction, order of costs |
| s. 42 (powers of transferee court) | O. XXI R. 8 | Execution proceeds as if the decree were its own |
| s. 37 (court of first instance) | O. XXI R. 10 | Filing in the appellate court is a return, not a dismissal |
| Limitation to execute | **Article 136, Limitation Act, 1963 — twelve years** from when the decree becomes enforceable | Runs from the decree; for a mandatory injunction, from date of default |
| Limitation for a transfer application | Governed by the twelve-year outer limit; each execution application must be within it | A transfer sought in year thirteen is futile |
| Court fee | Execution applications bear a fixed fee under the applicable **State** Court Fees Act | Verify locally; this is not uniform across States |

::: danger The twelve-year rule is a hard stop, not a soft one
Article 136 gives twelve years from the date the decree becomes enforceable. Unlike most limitation articles, there is no provision for a fresh period on part-satisfaction or acknowledgment in the ordinary case. Diarise it from the date of the decree. A decree-holder who allows twelve years to run has, in practical terms, lost the litigation he won.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Ghanshyam Das v. Anant Kumar Sinha, (1991) 4 SCC 379** — Bench of two.
*Facts:* A dispute over whether a claim relating to execution had to be agitated by suit or before the executing court.
**Ratio decidendi:** The Code provides a complete and self-contained machinery for the adjudication of questions arising in execution; the scheme of s. 47 read with Order XXI is designed to avoid the multiplicity of a separate suit, and that machinery must be used.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The Code furnishes a complete code of execution, and the objection now raised is one this Court is bound to determine under section 47, not one to be relegated to a fresh suit."
:::

**Rahul S. Shah v. Jinendra Kumar Gandhi, (2021) 6 SCC 418** — Bench of three.
*Facts:* The Court confronted the systemic problem of execution petitions pending for years after decrees in suits for property.
**Ratio decidendi:** Execution courts must not permit re-agitation of concluded questions; the Court issued binding directions requiring executing courts to dispose of execution proceedings within six months and restricting repeated objections designed to delay.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The directions in *Rahul S. Shah* require this Court to conclude execution within six months, and the objection now raised is precisely the kind of successive obstruction those directions were framed to prevent."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Citations, bench strength and paragraph numbers must be checked against the current reports before use in court. Execution law is also heavily overlaid by **State amendments** to Order XXI, and by the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 where the decree arises from a commercial dispute of Specified Value.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**Choosing the forum — a four-question sequence before you file:**

1. Was the decree passed on appeal? → File before the **court of first instance** (s. 37(a)).
2. Does the court of first instance still exist and have jurisdiction? → If not, identify the court which would today try the suit (s. 37(b)).
3. Are the debtor and his assets within that court's limits? → If yes, file there and proceed.
4. If no → do **not** ask that court to reach out. Apply under s. 39(1) to send the decree to the court where the person or property is.

::: danger Four ways an execution petition is thrown out at the threshold
1. **Filed in the appellate court.** Section 37(a) sends you to the trial court.
2. **Seeking attachment of property outside the court's limits.** Section 39(4) is an absolute bar — the answer is transfer, not reach.
3. **Beyond twelve years.** Article 136 of the Limitation Act, with no ordinary saving.
4. **Vague identification of property.** An execution petition that asks for attachment of "such property as may be found" invites dismissal; the property must be described with the particularity that would allow a bailiff to identify it.
:::

**Model prayer — application under section 39(1)(b):**

> It is therefore most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to —
> (a) send the decree dated ______ passed in Suit No. ______ of ______ for execution to the Court of ______ at ______, being a Court of competent jurisdiction within the local limits of whose jurisdiction the judgment-debtor owns the immovable property described in the Schedule hereto;
> (b) direct issuance of the certificate of non-satisfaction under Order XXI Rule 6 along with a certified copy of the decree;
> (c) pass such further orders as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit.

## Section 47 — the executing court's exclusive jurisdiction

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **47. Questions to be determined by the Court executing decree.** — (1) All questions arising between the parties to the suit in which the decree was passed, or their representatives, and relating to the execution, discharge or satisfaction of the decree, shall be determined by the Court executing the decree and not by a separate suit.
>
> (2) [*Omitted by Act 104 of 1976.*]
>
> (3) Where a question arises as to whether any person is or is not the representative of a party, such question shall, for the purposes of this section, be determined by the Court.
>
> *Explanation I.* — For the purposes of this section, a plaintiff whose suit has been dismissed and a defendant against whom a suit has been dismissed are parties to the suit.
>
> *Explanation II.* — (a) For the purposes of this section, a purchaser of property at a sale in execution of a decree shall be deemed to be a party to the suit in which the decree is passed; and (b) all questions relating to the delivery of possession of such property to such purchaser or his representative shall be deemed to be questions relating to the execution, discharge or satisfaction of the decree within the meaning of this section.

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "**All** questions" | **Exhaustive** | No carve-out for difficult or mixed questions of law and fact |
| "between the parties … or their representatives" | **Limiting** | A **stranger** to the decree is outside s. 47 — his remedy lies elsewhere |
| "relating to the execution, discharge or satisfaction" | **Subject-matter test** | The controlling question: does this dispute concern *executing* the decree, or something else? |
| "**shall** be determined by the Court executing the decree" | **Mandatory** | The executing court cannot decline and relegate the party to a suit |
| "**and not by a separate suit**" | **Express bar on jurisdiction** | A suit raising such a question is barred — and liable to rejection under O. VII R. 11(d) |
| Explanation II — auction-purchaser "deemed to be a party" | **Deeming** | Brings delivery disputes with the auction-purchaser inside s. 47 |

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — the two-part test.** A question falls under s. 47 only if **both** limbs are satisfied: (i) it arises *between the parties to the suit or their representatives*; and (ii) it *relates to execution, discharge or satisfaction* of the decree. Fail either limb and s. 47 does not apply — which usually means a separate suit is competent, and sometimes means an application under Order XXI instead.

**Sub-module B — the bar cuts both ways, and that is the point.** Section 47 is not merely a convenience for decree-holders. It is equally the judgment-debtor's forum. A debtor who says the decree has been satisfied out of court, or that the decree-holder has taken more than the decree permits, or that the decree is being executed against property it never covered, raises those objections **here** — by application in the execution proceeding, not by an independent suit for declaration or injunction. Filing that separate suit is a standard and expensive error.

**Sub-module C — "the executing court cannot go behind the decree."** This is the single most quoted proposition in execution law, and it is usually stated too broadly. The rule is that the executing court must take the decree as it finds it: it cannot question the correctness of the adjudication, cannot re-open findings, cannot entertain a plea that the suit was badly decided, and cannot modify relief.

**Sub-module D — the nullity exception, and its narrow gate.** There is one genuine exception. A decree passed by a court **wholly without inherent jurisdiction** — over the subject-matter — is a nullity, and its invalidity may be set up whenever and wherever it is sought to be enforced, including at the execution stage and even in collateral proceedings. But the gate is narrow in two ways:

1. It applies to want of **inherent** jurisdiction over the subject-matter. Defects of *territorial* or *pecuniary* jurisdiction stand differently: section 21 requires them to be taken at the earliest opportunity in the trial court and to have occasioned a failure of justice, and they do not render the decree a nullity.
2. The nullity must ordinarily appear on the **face of the record**. The executing court will not embark on a roving inquiry, take evidence, and decide for itself that the decreeing court lacked jurisdiction where that turns on contested facts.

**Sub-module E — what section 47 does *not* cover: the stranger.** A person who is neither a party nor a representative of a party is outside s. 47 entirely. If a third party is in possession and resists delivery, the machinery is **Order XXI Rules 97 to 101**, which requires the executing court to adjudicate that resistance, and gives its determination the force of a decree. This is the practical answer to the tenant, the subsequent purchaser or the family member found on the property at the time of delivery.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — out-of-court satisfaction.** A money decree for ₹18 lakhs is passed. The debtor pays ₹12 lakhs by bank transfer and the parties record a settlement, but nothing is certified to the court. The decree-holder executes for the full ₹18 lakhs. *As shield:* the judgment-debtor's objection is filed **under s. 47 in the execution proceeding** — not as a suit for declaration. (Note the trap: Order XXI Rule 2 requires payments outside court to be certified; an uncertified payment cannot be *recognised* by the executing court, which is why the debtor's real protection is to insist on certification at the time of payment.)

**Illustration 2 — the decree covers less than is being taken.** A decree grants possession of Plot A. In execution the decree-holder seeks delivery of Plots A and B, claiming B was always part of the same holding. *As shield:* this is squarely a s. 47 question — the scope of what the decree directs — and is decided by the executing court on the decree and the plaint schedule, not by a fresh title suit.

**Illustration 3 — the nullity plea that works.** A civil court decrees a suit that, on the face of the plaint, sought relief exclusively within the jurisdiction of a statutory tribunal from which civil jurisdiction is expressly barred. *As shield:* the decree is a nullity for want of inherent jurisdiction, and the objection is available at execution.

**Illustration 4 — the nullity plea that fails.** The debtor argues at execution that the suit was undervalued and should have gone to a higher court. *Result:* rejected. Pecuniary objections are governed by s. 21 and had to be taken at the earliest opportunity before the trial court; they do not make the decree a nullity.

**Illustration 5 — the stranger in possession.** On delivery of possession, a person claiming to be a tenant since before the suit resists. *Result:* not a s. 47 question, because he is not a party or representative. The executing court must adjudicate his obstruction under **O. XXI RR. 97–101**, and its order is appealable as a decree.

### 4. Procedural Interlocking Matrix

| Question | Provision to invoke | Forum |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Decree satisfied out of court | s. 47 objection + **O. XXI R. 2** certification | Executing court |
| Scope of the decree disputed | s. 47 | Executing court |
| Decree a nullity for want of inherent jurisdiction | s. 47 | Executing court |
| Territorial / pecuniary defect | **s. 21** — must be taken early in the trial court | Trial court, not execution |
| Stranger resists possession | **O. XXI RR. 97–101** | Executing court, adjudicated as a decree |
| Third-party claim to attached property | **O. XXI R. 58** | Executing court; order appealable |
| Separate suit filed on a s. 47 question | **O. VII R. 11(d)** — barred by law | Rejection of plaint |
| Appeal from a s. 47 determination | Determination is a **decree** under **s. 2(2)**; appeal under s. 96 | Appellate court |

::: warning The single most valuable consequence of section 47
Because a determination under s. 47 falls within the definition of "decree" in **section 2(2)**, it carries a **first appeal as of right under section 96** — not a revision. Practitioners who treat an adverse execution order as revisable under s. 115 routinely file in the wrong forum and lose the limitation period while they discover the error.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Kiran Singh v. Chaman Paswan, AIR 1954 SC 340** — Constitution Bench.
*Facts:* A decree was challenged on the ground that the court which passed it lacked pecuniary jurisdiction.
**Ratio decidendi:** A defect of jurisdiction over the subject-matter strikes at the authority of the court and renders the decree a nullity, which may be set up at any stage including execution; but a mere defect of pecuniary or territorial jurisdiction is governed by s. 21 and does not have that effect unless a failure of justice is shown.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The objection is not one of inherent jurisdiction over the subject-matter but of valuation, and on *Kiran Singh* it is answerable only under section 21 — which required it to be taken at the earliest opportunity in the trial court, and it was not."
:::

**Vasudev Dhanjibhai Modi v. Rajabhai Abdul Rehman, (1970) 1 SCC 670** — Bench of three.
*Facts:* The judgment-debtor sought to establish at the execution stage that the decreeing court lacked jurisdiction, on facts requiring investigation.
**Ratio decidendi:** An executing court may treat a decree as a nullity only where the want of jurisdiction is **apparent on the face of the record**; it cannot embark on an inquiry into contested facts to determine whether the decreeing court had jurisdiction.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The nullity now alleged does not appear on the face of the record and could be established, if at all, only by evidence — which on *Vasudev Dhanjibhai Modi* this executing Court is precluded from receiving."
:::

**Merla Ramanna v. Nallaparaju, AIR 1956 SC 87** — Bench of three.
*Facts:* Concerned the extent to which an executing court may examine the decree it is asked to enforce.
**Ratio decidendi:** The executing court must take the decree as it stands and cannot go behind it to question its correctness; its function is to give effect to the decree, construing it where necessary but never modifying it.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"This Court's function is to execute the decree as it stands. What the judgment-debtor invites is a re-examination of the adjudication itself, which *Merla Ramanna* forbids."
:::

**Brahmdeo Chaudhary v. Rishikesh Prasad Jaiswal, (1997) 3 SCC 694** — Bench of two.
*Facts:* A stranger to the decree resisted delivery of possession and sought adjudication of his independent right.
**Ratio decidendi:** A third party in possession claiming an independent right is entitled to have his objection adjudicated under Order XXI Rules 97 to 101 before being dispossessed; he cannot be evicted first and left to sue afterwards.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"My client is a stranger to this decree in settled possession under an independent claim, and *Brahmdeo Chaudhary* entitles him to adjudication under Rules 97 to 101 before any warrant of delivery issues."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Check the current reports for bench strength, paragraph numbers and subsequent history before relying on any of the above. Order XXI is extensively amended by several States, and the position on third-party obstruction in particular should be confirmed against the local amendment.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**The diagnostic — three questions that place any execution dispute:**

1. **Who is raising it?** A party or representative → potentially s. 47. A stranger → Order XXI Rules 97–101 (obstruction) or Rule 58 (claim to attached property).
2. **Does it concern executing the decree, or attacking it?** Execution, discharge or satisfaction → s. 47. Correctness of the adjudication → not available at all, absent inherent nullity on the face of the record.
3. **What is the remedy against the order?** A s. 47 determination is a decree under s. 2(2) → **first appeal under s. 96**. Get this right; it decides your forum and your limitation.

::: danger Five errors that lose an execution objection
1. **Filing a separate suit** on a question that s. 47 commits to the executing court — met with rejection under O. VII R. 11(d).
2. **Paying without certification.** Order XXI Rule 2 makes an uncertified out-of-court payment unrecognisable by the executing court. Insist on certification contemporaneously.
3. **Pleading "nullity" loosely** where the real complaint is pecuniary or territorial — governed by s. 21, and long since waived.
4. **Filing a revision under s. 115** against a s. 47 determination instead of a first appeal, and discovering the error after limitation has run.
5. **Raising objections successively.** After *Rahul S. Shah*, staggered objections designed to delay attract adverse orders and costs.
:::

**Model prayer — objection under section 47:**

> It is therefore most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to —
> (a) hold and declare that the decree dated ______ in Suit No. ______ of ______ stands satisfied to the extent of ₹______, and that the present execution is maintainable, if at all, only for the balance;
> (b) recall the warrant of attachment dated ______ issued in respect of the property described in the Schedule, the same not being covered by the decree;
> (c) stay further proceedings in the present execution pending determination of the questions raised herein;
> (d) pass such further orders as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit and proper.

## Sections 51 to 59 — the modes of execution, and arrest

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **51. Powers of Court to enforce execution.** — Subject to such conditions and limitations as may be prescribed, the Court may, on the application of the decree-holder, order execution of the decree —
> (a) by delivery of any property specifically decreed;
> (b) by attachment and sale or by sale without attachment of any property;
> (c) by arrest and detention in prison for such period not exceeding the period specified in section 58, where arrest and detention is permissible under that section;
> (d) by appointing a receiver; or
> (e) in such other manner as the nature of the relief granted may require:
>
> **Provided that**, where the decree is for the payment of money, execution by detention in prison shall not be ordered unless, after giving the judgment-debtor an opportunity of showing cause why he should not be committed to prison, the Court, for reasons recorded in writing, is satisfied —
> (a) that the judgment-debtor, with the object or effect of obstructing or delaying the execution of the decree, —
>   (i) is likely to abscond or leave the local limits of the jurisdiction of the Court, or
>   (ii) has, after the institution of the suit in which the decree was passed, dishonestly transferred, concealed, or removed any part of his property, or committed any other act of bad faith in relation to his property, or
> (b) that the judgment-debtor has, or has had since the date of the decree, the means to pay the amount of the decree or some substantial part thereof and refuses or neglects or has refused or neglected to pay the same, or
> (c) that the decree is for a sum for which the judgment-debtor was bound in a fiduciary capacity to account.
>
> *Explanation.* — In the calculation of the means of the judgment-debtor for the purposes of clause (b), there shall be left out of account any property which, by or under any law or custom having the force of law for the time being in force, is exempt from attachment in execution of the decree.

> **58. Detention and release.** — (1) Every person detained in the civil prison in execution of a decree shall be so detained, —
> (a) where the decree is for the payment of a sum of money exceeding five thousand rupees, for a period not exceeding three months, and
> (b) where the decree is for the payment of a sum of money exceeding two thousand rupees, but not exceeding five thousand rupees, for a period not exceeding six weeks:
>
> Provided that he shall be released from such detention before the expiration of the said period of detention — (a) on the amount mentioned in the warrant for his detention being paid to the officer in charge of the civil prison, or (b) on the decree against him being otherwise fully satisfied, or (c) on the request of the person on whose application he has been so detained, or (d) on the omission by the person, on whose application he has been so detained, to pay subsistence allowance …
>
> (2) A judgment-debtor released from detention under this section shall not merely by reason of his release be discharged from his debt, but he shall not be liable to be re-arrested under the decree in execution of which he was detained in the civil prison.

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| s. 51 — "the Court **may**, on the application" | **Discretionary and party-driven** | The mode is chosen by the decree-holder and allowed by the court; no mode is automatic |
| s. 51(e) — "in such other manner as the nature of the relief … may require" | **Residuary** | Gives the executing court flexibility where (a)–(d) do not fit |
| Proviso — "**shall not** be ordered unless" | **Prohibitory condition precedent** | Detention is barred until the conditions are satisfied |
| Proviso — "after giving … an opportunity of showing cause" | **Mandatory natural justice** | Show-cause notice is a jurisdictional prerequisite, not a formality |
| Proviso — "for reasons recorded in writing" | **Mandatory** | An order of detention without recorded satisfaction is unsustainable |
| Proviso (b) — "**has, or has had since the date of the decree**, the means … **and refuses or neglects**" | **Conjunctive: means + refusal** | Inability to pay is a complete answer; poverty is not contempt |
| s. 58(2) — "shall not … be discharged from his debt" | **Preservative** | Detention does not extinguish the debt; but re-arrest on the same decree is barred |

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — the menu, and how to choose.** Section 51 lists five modes. In practice the choice is strategic:

| Mode | When it is the right instrument |
| --- | --- |
| **(a) Delivery of property specifically decreed** | Decrees for possession or for delivery of identified movables |
| **(b) Attachment and sale** | The workhorse for money decrees; the default first move |
| **(c) Arrest and detention** | Only where the debtor *can* pay and won't — a coercive lever, not a recovery method |
| **(d) Receiver** | Where the asset generates income, or needs preservation and management pending realisation |
| **(e) Such other manner** | Mandatory injunctions, specific performance requiring execution of documents, and the residue |

**Sub-module B — the proviso to section 51 is the whole of arrest law in miniature.** Read it as a sequence of gates, all of which must be passed before a money-decree debtor can be committed:

1. **Show cause.** The debtor must be given an opportunity to be heard on why he should not be committed. This is jurisdictional.
2. **Recorded satisfaction.** The court must record its reasons in writing. A mechanical order does not survive challenge.
3. **One of three grounds must be made out:** (a) conduct designed to obstruct or delay — absconding, dishonest transfer, concealment, bad faith; **or** (b) means plus refusal or neglect; **or** (c) a sum for which the debtor was bound in a **fiduciary** capacity.

**Sub-module C — "means" is not "once had money".** Clause (b) requires that the debtor *has, or has had since the date of the decree*, the means to pay, **and** refuses or neglects. Both limbs are essential. A debtor who is genuinely destitute has an absolute answer, and the Explanation reinforces it by excluding exempt property from the computation of means. Civil detention in India is a sanction for **dishonest refusal**, not for poverty.

**Sub-module D — detention does not pay the debt.** Section 58(2) makes two points at once: release does not discharge the debt, so the decree survives and other modes remain available; but the debtor cannot be **re-arrested** under the same decree. Arrest is therefore a single-use lever. Spending it early, before attachment has been attempted, is usually a strategic error.

**Sub-module E — who cannot be arrested.** Section 56 prohibits the arrest or detention of a **woman** in execution of a decree for the payment of money. Section 55 regulates the manner of arrest, including the protection that a dwelling-house shall not be entered after sunset or before sunrise, and that an outer door shall not be broken open unless the house is in the debtor's occupancy and he refuses access.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — arrest sought as a first move.** A decree-holder files execution and immediately seeks the debtor's arrest. *As shield:* the debtor shows he owns an unencumbered flat, offers it for attachment, and demonstrates no concealment or absconding. The application fails — none of the three grounds in the proviso is made out, and the existence of attachable property negates the suggestion of bad faith.

**Illustration 2 — means plus refusal, properly proved.** The debtor draws a salary of ₹3.5 lakhs a month, has paid nothing in four years against a decree for ₹9 lakhs, and has transferred his only immovable property to his brother for nominal consideration after the suit was filed. *As sword:* the decree-holder pleads the transfer under clause (a)(ii) **and** means with refusal under clause (b), supported by salary records and the registered sale deed. Detention is available.

**Illustration 3 — the fiduciary case.** An agent decreed to account for sale proceeds he collected on the principal's behalf pleads inability to pay. *Result:* clause (c) applies independently of means — the sum is one for which he was bound in a fiduciary capacity to account.

**Illustration 4 — the receiver as the better instrument.** A decree for ₹60 lakhs against a debtor whose principal asset is a tenanted commercial building. Attachment and sale is slow and will realise a distress price. *As sword:* the decree-holder applies under s. 51(d) for a receiver to collect rents and remit them towards the decree — realisation without destroying the asset.

**Illustration 5 — the woman judgment-debtor.** A money decree is passed against a woman. Execution by arrest is not available at all: section 56 bars it. The decree-holder must proceed by attachment, sale or receiver.

::: danger Order XXI Rule 37 is where arrest applications are actually lost
Rule 37 requires that, instead of issuing a warrant at once, the court **shall** ordinarily issue a notice calling on the judgment-debtor to show cause why he should not be committed. A decree-holder who obtains a warrant without that notice, in a case where the exception does not apply, hands the debtor a clean ground to have the arrest set aside — and invites a claim for compensation.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Jolly George Varghese v. Bank of Cochin, (1980) 2 SCC 360** — Bench of two.
*Facts:* A judgment-debtor was ordered to be detained in civil prison for non-payment of a money decree, without a finding that he had the means to pay.
**Ratio decidendi:** Read with Article 21 of the Constitution and Article 11 of the ICCPR, the proviso to section 51 permits detention only where there is proof of **means coupled with refusal or neglect**, or of dishonest conduct; mere inability to discharge a debt cannot justify imprisonment. To imprison for poverty is constitutionally impermissible.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"*Jolly George Varghese* holds that no one may be detained merely because he is unable to pay. There is no finding — and no material capable of supporting a finding — that my client has the means and refuses; the application must therefore fail."
:::

**Shyam Singh v. Collector, District Hamirpur** and the line of authority on the **civil prison** — *see the caution below.*

Bench strength and paragraph numbers of all cases cited must likewise be verified against the current reports.

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**Sequencing execution — the order that actually works:**

1. **Order XXI Rule 41 examination first.** Bring the debtor to court and examine him on oath as to his assets. This produces admissions that later support both attachment and, if needed, the "means" finding under the proviso.
2. **Attach and sell.** The primary route for money decrees.
3. **Receiver**, where the asset yields income or would be destroyed by forced sale.
4. **Arrest last** — and only with clause (a), (b) or (c) material actually assembled. It is a one-shot lever under s. 58(2), and it recovers nothing by itself.

::: danger Six ways an arrest application collapses
1. **No show-cause notice** under O. XXI R. 37.
2. **No recorded reasons** — the proviso requires written satisfaction.
3. **Means not proved.** Assertion is not evidence; produce salary slips, bank statements, the registered transfer deed.
4. **Exempt property counted as means** — the Explanation excludes it.
5. **Woman judgment-debtor** — barred outright by s. 56.
6. **Subsistence allowance not deposited** — s. 57 and O. XXI R. 39 secure release regardless of the merits.
:::

**Model prayer — execution seeking attachment and sale:**

> It is therefore most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to —
> (a) order execution of the decree dated ______ in Suit No. ______ of ______ by attachment and sale of the immovable property of the judgment-debtor described in the Schedule hereto, under section 51(b) read with Order XXI Rules 54 and 64;
> (b) direct the judgment-debtor to appear and be examined on oath as to his property under Order XXI Rule 41;
> (c) restrain the judgment-debtor from alienating, encumbering or otherwise dealing with the said property pending realisation;
> (d) pass such further orders as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit and proper.

## Sections 60 to 64 and 73 — attachment, alienation and distribution

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **60. Property liable to attachment and sale in execution of decree.** — (1) The following property is liable to attachment and sale in execution of a decree, namely, lands, houses or other buildings, goods, money, bank-notes, cheques, bills of exchange, hundis, promissory notes, Government securities, bonds or other securities for money, debts, shares in a corporation and, save as hereinafter mentioned, all other saleable property, movable or immovable, belonging to the judgment-debtor, or over which, or the profits of which, he has a disposing power which he may exercise for his own benefit, whether the same be held in the name of the judgment-debtor or by another person in trust for him or on his behalf:
>
> **Provided that the following particulars shall not be liable to such attachment or sale**, namely: —
> (a) the necessary wearing-apparel, cooking vessels, beds and bedding of the judgment-debtor, his wife and children, and such personal ornaments as, in accordance with religious usage, cannot be parted with by any woman;
> (b) tools of artisans, and, where the judgment-debtor is an agriculturist, his implements of husbandry and such cattle and seed-grain as may, in the opinion of the Court, be necessary to enable him to earn his livelihood as such …;
> (c) houses and other buildings (with the materials and the sites thereof and the land immediately appurtenant thereto and necessary for their enjoyment) belonging to an agriculturist or a labourer or a domestic servant and occupied by him;
> …
> (g) stipends and gratuities allowed to pensioners of the Government …;
> (h) the wages of labourers and domestic servants, whether payable in money or in kind;
> (i) salary to the extent of the first one thousand rupees and two-thirds of the remainder …;
> …
> (n) any allowance declared by any Indian law to be exempt from liability to attachment or sale in execution of a decree; …

> **64. Private alienation of property after attachment to be void.** — (1) Where an attachment has been made, any private transfer or delivery of the property attached or of any interest therein and any payment to the judgment-debtor of any debt, dividend or other monies contrary to such attachment, shall be void as against all claims enforceable under the attachment.
>
> (2) Nothing in this section shall apply to any private transfer or delivery of the property attached or of any interest therein, made in pursuance of any contract for such transfer or delivery entered into and registered before the attachment.

> **73. Proceeds of execution-sale to be rateably distributed among decree-holders.** — (1) Where assets are held by a Court and more persons than one have, before the receipt of such assets, made application to the Court for the execution of decrees for the payment of money passed against the same judgment-debtor and have not obtained satisfaction thereof, the assets, after deducting the costs of realization, shall be rateably distributed among all such persons …

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| s. 60(1) — "all other saleable property … belonging to the judgment-debtor" | **Inclusive and wide** | The default is attachability; exemption must be established |
| "or over which … he has a **disposing power** … for his own benefit" | **Substance over form** | Benami holdings and property held by another on his behalf are reachable |
| Proviso — "**shall not** be liable" | **Absolute exemption** | Not discretionary; the court cannot attach exempt property even by consent of the debtor |
| s. 60 proviso (i) — salary formula | **Quantified partial exemption** | Attachment of salary is permitted only above the statutory floor |
| s. 64(1) — "**shall be void** as against all claims enforceable under the attachment" | **Limited avoidance, not absolute nullity** | The transfer is void *only* against the attaching claims — it remains good between transferor and transferee |
| s. 64(2) — registered pre-attachment contract | **Saving** | Protects the honest prior purchaser who registered his contract before attachment |
| s. 73(1) — "**shall** be rateably distributed" | **Mandatory** | Not first-past-the-post; qualifying decree-holders share |

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — the presumption runs in the decree-holder's favour.** Section 60(1) casts the net wide: everything saleable and belonging to the debtor, plus anything over which he has a disposing power exercisable for his own benefit. The last clause matters enormously in practice — property standing in a relative's name but in substance the debtor's is attachable, though it must be pleaded and proved, not merely asserted.

**Sub-module B — the exemptions are a floor of human dignity, not a loophole.** The proviso protects the debtor's clothing and bedding, a woman's religiously inalienable ornaments, an artisan's tools, an agriculturist's implements and cattle, the agriculturist's or labourer's dwelling-house, wages, and salary below the statutory threshold. These exemptions cannot be waived: an agreement by the debtor to allow attachment of exempt property is ineffective, because the exemption protects a public interest in not reducing people to destitution, not merely a private right.

**Sub-module C — section 64 in one sentence.** Once property is attached, the debtor cannot sell it out from under the attachment. But read sub-s. (1) carefully: the transfer is void **"as against all claims enforceable under the attachment"** — not void altogether. As between the debtor and his buyer the sale stands; it simply cannot defeat the decree-holder. And sub-s. (2), inserted by the 1976 amendment, protects the buyer who had a **registered** contract predating the attachment.

**Sub-module D — rateable distribution under section 73, and its three conditions.** Section 73 displaces the instinct that the first decree-holder to attach takes everything. Where assets are held by the court, the money is shared rateably among decree-holders who satisfy three conditions:

1. They hold decrees for the **payment of money** (not decrees for possession or injunction);
2. Against the **same judgment-debtor**;
3. And they applied for execution **before the receipt of the assets** by the court.

The third condition is the one that decides cases. A decree-holder who files his execution application after the sale proceeds have reached the court is too late, however meritorious his decree.

**Sub-module E — what section 73 does not touch.** Secured creditors stand outside this scheme: a mortgagee enforcing his security is not sharing rateably with unsecured decree-holders, and claims under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 or the SARFAESI Act, 2002 operate on their own priorities. Section 73 governs the distribution of assets *held by the civil court*, among *money decree-holders*.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — the benami flat.** The judgment-debtor lives in a flat registered in his adult son's name; the consideration came from the debtor's account and he pays the maintenance. *As sword:* the decree-holder attaches it under the "disposing power … for his own benefit" limb of s. 60(1), pleading the money trail with bank records. *As shield:* the son objects under **O. XXI R. 58**, and the executing court adjudicates his claim.

**Illustration 2 — the salary attachment.** A decree-holder seeks attachment of the debtor's entire monthly salary. *As shield:* the debtor invokes proviso (i) — the first tranche is wholly exempt, and only a fraction of the remainder is attachable. The court moulds the attachment accordingly.

**Illustration 3 — the sale one day after attachment.** Attachment is effected on 3 March. On 4 March the debtor executes a sale deed in favour of a purchaser who pays full value in good faith. *Result:* under s. 64(1) the sale is void against the decree-holder's claim — the property may still be sold in execution. The purchaser's remedy lies against the debtor, not against the decree-holder.

**Illustration 4 — the registered agreement that saves the buyer.** The same facts, except the purchaser had a **registered** agreement to sell dated the previous November, before the attachment. *Result:* section 64(2) protects him; the attachment does not defeat the transfer made in pursuance of that registered contract.

**Illustration 5 — the race to the fund.** Three decree-holders hold money decrees against the same debtor. A's execution realises ₹30 lakhs, which reaches the court on 10 June. B had applied for execution on 2 June; C applies on 15 June. *Result:* A and B share rateably; **C takes nothing** from this fund, having applied after receipt of the assets.

**Illustration 6 — the agriculturist's house.** A money decree-holder attaches the debtor's dwelling-house in a village. The debtor establishes he is an agriculturist occupying the house. *Result:* proviso (c) exempts it absolutely, and no consent or undertaking by the debtor can render it attachable.

::: warning Attachment of immovable property is not physical seizure
Order XXI Rule 54 effects attachment by an **order prohibiting the judgment-debtor from transferring or charging the property**, proclaimed by beat of drum and affixed on a conspicuous part of the property and the court-house. The date on which those formalities are completed fixes the moment from which section 64 operates. Practitioners who assume attachment runs from the date of the order, rather than from compliance with Rule 54, misdate the very fact on which the avoidance of an intervening sale depends.
:::

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Rahul S. Shah v. Jinendra Kumar Gandhi, (2021) 6 SCC 418** — Bench of three.
*Facts:* Execution of decrees in property suits had been frustrated for years by successive objections and obstruction.
**Ratio decidendi:** Executing courts must curtail repeated and successive objections; the Court laid down directions, including that execution proceedings be concluded within six months, that courts insist on disclosure of assets, and that obstruction be dealt with firmly.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The directions in *Rahul S. Shah* require disclosure on affidavit of the judgment-debtor's assets and the conclusion of execution within six months; I press for both."
:::

**Ghanshyam Das v. Anant Kumar Sinha, (1991) 4 SCC 379** — Bench of two.
**Ratio decidendi:** *(as above)* — the Code provides a complete machinery for questions arising in execution, and parties must use it rather than resorting to independent suits.

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**For the decree-holder — building an attachment that survives objection:**

| Step | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Examine the debtor under **O. XXI R. 41** and obtain sworn admissions about his assets |
| 2 | Identify property with **title-document precision** — survey number, boundaries, area, registration particulars |
| 3 | Where the property stands in another's name, plead the **money trail** for the disposing-power limb of s. 60(1) |
| 4 | Ensure **Rule 54 formalities** are completed and documented — this fixes the s. 64 date |
| 5 | Apply for execution **before** any fund reaches the court, to qualify under s. 73 |

**For the judgment-debtor — the legitimate defences, in order of strength:**

1. **Statutory exemption** under the proviso to s. 60 — absolute, and unwaivable.
2. **Salary formula** under clause (i) — reduces, rather than defeats, the attachment.
3. **Registered pre-attachment contract** under s. 64(2), where a transferee is affected.
4. **Deposit and set aside the sale** under O. XXI R. 89, within the prescribed period.

::: danger Three misconceptions that cost clients property
1. **"Section 64 makes the sale void."** It makes it void *only against claims enforceable under the attachment*. The buyer still has rights against the seller — and, crucially, a buyer with a registered pre-attachment contract is protected by sub-s. (2).
2. **"First to attach takes all."** Section 73 requires rateable distribution among money decree-holders who applied before the assets were received.
3. **"The debtor can consent to attachment of his exempt house."** He cannot. The exemptions in the proviso to s. 60 are not waivable.
:::

**Model prayer — objection under Order XXI Rule 58 to attachment:**

> It is therefore most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to —
> (a) raise the attachment dated ______ levied upon the property described in the Schedule hereto, the said property belonging to the objector and not to the judgment-debtor;
> (b) in the alternative, hold that the said property is exempt from attachment under clause ____ of the proviso to section 60 of the Code;
> (c) stay the sale of the said property pending adjudication of this objection;
> (d) pass such further orders as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit and proper.

## Chapter consolidation — the life of an execution

| Stage | Provision | The question it answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Choosing the court | ss. 37, 38 | Trial court if the decree came on appeal; original court otherwise |
| 2. Moving the decree | s. 39, and the bar in s. 39(4) | Send the decree to the assets — never reach beyond your limits |
| 3. Filing | O. XXI RR. 10, 11 | The petition's contents decide whether it survives scrutiny |
| 4. Finding assets | O. XXI R. 41 | Examination of the debtor on oath |
| 5. Choosing the mode | s. 51(a)–(e) | Delivery, attachment and sale, arrest, receiver, or residuary |
| 6. Attaching | s. 60; O. XXI RR. 54, 58 | What may be seized, and how third parties object |
| 7. Protecting the attachment | s. 64 | Alienation after attachment is void against the decree |
| 8. Distributing | s. 73 | Rateable sharing among qualifying money decree-holders |
| 9. Resolving disputes | **s. 47** | Everything between the parties, decided here — and appealable as a decree |

::: tip The five sentences worth memorising from this chapter
1. **The appellate decree is executed by the court of first instance** — section 37(a).
2. **If the assets are elsewhere, send the decree; do not reach for them** — section 39(4) is an absolute bar.
3. **All questions between the parties relating to execution, discharge or satisfaction go to the executing court and not by separate suit** — section 47, and its determination is a **decree** carrying a first appeal under section 96.
4. **The executing court cannot go behind the decree**, save where want of inherent jurisdiction appears on the face of the record.
5. **No one is imprisoned for poverty** — the proviso to section 51 requires means *plus* refusal, or dishonest conduct, or a fiduciary obligation, after show-cause and on recorded reasons.
:::

::: warning Before you file an execution petition
- Confirm the **twelve-year** period under Article 136 has not expired, and diarise it from the date the decree became enforceable.
- Verify you are in the correct court under **sections 37 and 38** before drafting anything else.
- Describe the property with the precision a bailiff could act on; vague schedules are returned.
- Check the **State amendments** to Order XXI and the current exemption figures under section 60 — both vary materially between States.
- If the decree arises from a commercial dispute of Specified Value, check whether the **Commercial Courts Act, 2015** alters the forum and the timelines.
- Sequence your modes: examination under Rule 41, then attachment, then receiver — and keep **arrest last**, since section 58(2) makes it available only once.
:::
