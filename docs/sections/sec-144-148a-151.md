# Sections 144, 148A & 151 — Restitution, Caveat and Inherent Powers

These three provisions sit in Part XI, among the miscellaneous sections, and they are easy to underestimate. In fact they answer three questions that arise constantly. What happens when a decree that has already been executed is reversed on appeal — must the successful appellant sue afresh, or can he simply ask for his property back? How does a person who fears that an application will be made against him ex parte ensure that he is heard? And what may a court do when the Code is silent but justice plainly requires an order?

| Provision | One-line function |
|---|---|
| **Section 144** | Restitution — the court that passed the decree must place the parties in the position they would have occupied but for the decree that has since been varied or reversed |
| **Section 148A** | Caveat — a person apprehending an application against him lodges a caveat and becomes entitled to notice before any ex parte order is made |
| **Section 151** | Inherent powers — nothing in the Code limits the court's power to make such orders as are necessary for the ends of justice or to prevent abuse of its process |
| **Section 148** | Enlargement of time — the court may extend a period it has fixed, subject to a total of thirty days |
| **Section 149** | Power to make up deficiency of court fees — and the payment then has the same force as if made at institution |

::: info The architecture in one breath
Section 144 is the **duty to undo**: the court that gave the benefit must take it back when the foundation falls. Section 148A is the **right to be heard before you are hit**: a defensive filing that converts an ex parte application into a contested one. Section 151 is the **residual power**: not a source of jurisdiction, not a way round an express provision, but the acknowledgement that the Code is not exhaustive of a court's capacity to do justice. The relationship between 144 and 151 is the most examined point in this chapter — where Section 144 applies, Section 151 must not be invoked; where restitution is required but Section 144 does not strictly apply, Section 151 fills the gap.
:::

## Section 144 — Application for restitution

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **144. Application for restitution.** — **(1)** Where and in so far as a decree or an order is varied or reversed in any appeal, revision or other proceeding or is set aside or modified in any suit instituted for the purpose, the Court which passed the decree or order shall, on the application of any party entitled to any benefit by way of restitution or otherwise, cause such restitution to be made as will, so far as may be, place the parties in the position which they would have occupied but for such decree or order or such part thereof as has been varied, reversed, set aside or modified; and, for this purpose, the Court may make any orders, including orders for the refund of costs and for the payment of interest, damages, compensation and mesne profits, which are properly consequential on such variation, reversal, setting aside or modification of the decree or order.
>
> *Explanation. — For the purposes of sub-section (1), the expression "Court which passed the decree or order" shall be deemed to include, —*
> *(a) where the decree or order has been varied or reversed in exercise of appellate or revisional jurisdiction, the Court of first instance;*
> *(b) where the decree or order has been set aside by a separate suit, the Court of first instance which passed such decree or order;*
> *(c) where the Court of first instance has ceased to exist or has ceased to have jurisdiction to execute it, the Court which, if the suit wherein the decree or order was passed were instituted at the time of making the application for restitution under this section, would have jurisdiction to try such suit.*
>
> **(2)** No suit shall be instituted for the purpose of obtaining any restitution or other relief which could be obtained by application under sub-section (1).

**Statutory mechanics**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "**Where and in so far as** a decree or an order is varied or reversed" | Condition precedent, and a limitation on extent | Restitution follows only to the extent of the variation or reversal. A decree reduced from ₹50 lakh to ₹30 lakh grounds restitution of ₹20 lakh, not of the whole. |
| "in any **appeal, revision or other proceeding**" | Wide | Includes review, revision, an appeal to the Supreme Court, a writ proceeding, and a decree set aside in a separate suit. |
| "the Court which passed the decree… **shall**… cause such restitution to be made" | **Mandatory** | Once the conditions are met, the court has no discretion to refuse restitution; the discretion goes only to the mode and extent. |
| "**on the application of any party** entitled to any benefit" | Application required | The court does not act suo motu under s. 144, though it may act under s. 151. |
| "place the parties in the position which they **would have occupied but for** such decree" | The measure of relief | The counterfactual test. Not merely return of what was taken, but restoration of the position — hence interest, mesne profits, damages. |
| "the Court **may** make any orders, including orders for the refund of costs and for the payment of interest, damages, compensation and mesne profits" | Enabling; illustrative | The list is not exhaustive; the touchstone is whether the order is "properly consequential". |
| "**properly consequential**" | Limitation | The order must flow from the reversal. A claim requiring an independent adjudication of new rights is not properly consequential and must be sued upon. |
| **Explanation (a)–(c)** | Deeming | "The Court which passed the decree" means the **court of first instance**, even where the reversal was by the appellate court or the Supreme Court. Restitution is applied for in the trial court. |
| Sub-s. (2): "**No suit shall be instituted**" | **Bar on suits** | Where restitution can be obtained by application under s. 144(1), a suit for the same relief is barred. This makes s. 144 both a remedy and an exclusion. |

::: tip Section 144 in one line
The doctrine is *actus curiae neminem gravabit* — an act of the court shall prejudice no one. A party who has taken a benefit under a decree holds it subject to the result of the appeal, and when the decree falls, the court's own duty is to restore what its own order procured.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — the three conditions.**

Restitution under Section 144 requires all three:

1. **The decree or order must have been varied or reversed** — by an appellate or revisional court, in review, or in a separate suit. A decree that merely stands satisfied, or an interim order that has lapsed, does not engage the section.
2. **The party applying must be entitled to a benefit by way of restitution** — that is, he must be a party to the decree who has been deprived of something under it, or his legal representative.
3. **The relief claimed must be properly consequential** on the reversal — flowing from it, not requiring an independent enquiry into fresh rights.

**Sub-module B — the counterfactual measure.**

The section does not simply order the return of what was taken; it directs the court to place the parties "in the position which they would have occupied but for" the decree. That is a materially larger idea, and it is why the section expressly contemplates:

| Head | When it is awarded |
|---|---|
| **Refund of the money paid** under the reversed decree | Always, where money was recovered |
| **Interest** on that money | Almost always — the successful party has been out of his money. The rate is in the court's discretion. |
| **Restoration of possession** | Where possession was delivered in execution of the reversed decree |
| **Mesne profits** | For the period the party was wrongfully kept out of possession — computed under Order XX Rule 12 principles |
| **Damages and compensation** | Where the deprivation caused loss beyond the value of what was taken |
| **Refund of costs** | Expressly provided |

**Sub-module C — who may apply, and to which court.**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who may apply? | A **party** to the decree who is entitled to a benefit, and his legal representatives. A stranger to the decree cannot apply under s. 144; his remedy is a suit. |
| To which court? | The **court of first instance** — expressly, by Explanation (a) and (b) — even where the reversal was by the High Court or the Supreme Court. |
| What if that court has ceased to exist? | Explanation (c) — the court which would have jurisdiction to try the suit if it were instituted at the time of the restitution application. |
| Is it an execution proceeding? | Yes. An application under s. 144 is treated as an application in execution, which is why **Article 136** of the Limitation Act, 1963 governs it. |

**Sub-module D — limitation.**

The settled position is that an application under Section 144 is an application for execution of a decree, and is therefore governed by **Article 136 of the Limitation Act, 1963** — **twelve years** from the date the decree or order sought to be restituted becomes enforceable, that is, from the date of the appellate decree reversing the original decree.

The alternative view — that Article 137 (three years, the residuary article for applications) applies — is rejected in the dominant line of authority, precisely because restitution is worked out in execution and an order under s. 144 is a decree by virtue of Section 2(2).

::: warning The date from which time runs
Time runs from the date of the **appellate decree** that reverses, not from the date of the original decree, and not from the date on which the applicant discovers his entitlement. Where the matter goes further — to the High Court in second appeal, or to the Supreme Court — the relevant date is the date of the decree which finally reverses.
:::

**Sub-module E — Section 144 and Section 151: the boundary.**

This is the most examined point in the chapter, and the rule is simple in statement and slippery in application.

| Situation | Provision |
|---|---|
| A **decree or order** has been varied or reversed, and the applicant is a **party** seeking a **properly consequential** restoration | **Section 144.** Section 151 must not be used, because where the Code makes express provision, inherent powers are excluded. |
| The case falls **outside** the letter of s. 144 — for example, the party is not a party to the decree; or what is to be undone is not a "decree or order" varied or reversed but the consequence of the court's own act, such as an order obtained by fraud, or an interim order acted upon and later found to have been wrongly made | **Section 151.** The doctrine of restitution is wider than s. 144; s. 144 is a statutory recognition of part of it, not the whole. |
| A **stranger** to the decree seeks restoration | Neither. His remedy is a **suit**, which sub-s. (2) does not bar because his relief could not have been obtained under sub-s. (1). |

The formula worth remembering: **Section 144 does not exhaust the doctrine of restitution; it merely provides the statutory route for the commonest case.**

**Sub-module F — the bar in sub-section (2).**

Where restitution *could* be obtained by an application under sub-section (1), a **suit is barred**. The bar is on the remedy, not on the right. Two consequences:

- A plaintiff who files a suit for refund of money recovered under a reversed decree will have his plaint rejected under **Order VII Rule 11(d)** — the suit is barred by s. 144(2), and the bar appears on the face of the plaint.
- Conversely, where the relief genuinely could **not** have been obtained under sub-s. (1) — because the applicant is a stranger, or because the relief requires an independent adjudication and is not "properly consequential" — the suit is competent.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — money recovered under a decree later reversed.** P obtains a money decree for ₹40,00,000 against D. D's bank account is attached and the money is paid out to P. Four years later the first appellate court dismisses the suit.

- **Sword (defendant, now the applicant):** Application under s. 144 to the **trial court**, for (i) refund of ₹40,00,000; (ii) interest from the date of payment out to P until refund; and (iii) refund of the costs paid. All are expressly within the section's contemplation, and the duty is mandatory — "shall… cause such restitution to be made". Limitation: twelve years under Article 136 from the date of the appellate decree.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** There is no defence to the principal. The contest is on **interest** — its rate and period — and on any claim for consequential damages. P will argue that the rate should be modest, that interest should run only from the date of the appellate decree rather than the date of recovery, and that any claim for business loss is not "properly consequential" and requires a suit.

::: tip The interest argument is where restitution cases are actually fought
The principal is rarely disputed. Fix your position on (i) the rate, (ii) the start date — recovery or reversal, and (iii) whether interest is simple or compounded. Plead a specific rate with a reason (the applicant's actual borrowing cost, or the rate the decree itself carried), rather than leaving it to the court.
:::

**Illustration 2 — possession delivered and then reversed.** P obtains a decree for possession of a shop against D and takes possession in execution. Three years later the High Court allows D's second appeal and dismisses the suit.

- **Sword (defendant):** Application under s. 144 for (i) restoration of possession of the shop; and (ii) **mesne profits** for the three years D was kept out, computed on the letting value. Mesne profits are expressly within s. 144(1) and are properly consequential. If P has made improvements, the court may adjust; if P has damaged the premises, damages may be awarded.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** Two arguments of substance. First, if P has in the meantime **let the shop to a third party**, the tenant is a stranger to the decree — but a transferee *pendente lite* is bound by s. 52 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, so this rarely assists. Second, resist the quantum of mesne profits, which requires evidence of letting value; ask for an enquiry rather than a summary figure.

**Illustration 3 — the property sold in execution to a stranger auction-purchaser.** P's money decree against D is executed; D's land is sold in court auction to X, a stranger, and the sale is confirmed. The decree is then reversed on appeal.

- **Sword (defendant):** Apply under s. 144 against **P** for restitution of the sale proceeds with interest. As against **X**, the position is protected by **Section 65 read with Order XXI Rule 92** and the general rule that a bona fide auction-purchaser who is a **stranger to the decree** obtains a good title which is not disturbed by the subsequent reversal of the decree.
- **Shield (auction-purchaser X):** X's title stands. The distinction that decides these cases is **stranger auction-purchaser** (title protected) versus **decree-holder auction-purchaser** (P himself bought at the auction — his purchase falls with the decree, and restitution of the property may be ordered against him).

::: danger The single most important distinction in restitution against auction sales
If the auction-purchaser is the **decree-holder himself**, restitution of the property may be ordered — he took subject to the appeal. If the auction-purchaser is a **stranger** who bought bona fide and the sale has been confirmed, his title is protected and the judgment-debtor's restitution is limited to the **sale proceeds with interest** from the decree-holder. Identify which you are dealing with before drafting the application.
:::

**Illustration 4 — restitution against an interim order.** P obtains an interim injunction restraining D from operating a business, and it subsists for two years. The suit is then dismissed and the injunction found to have been wrongly obtained.

- **Sword (defendant):** Section 144 is arguable — an "order" has effectively fallen. But the safer and more direct routes are (i) **Section 95 CPC**, which expressly provides compensation, up to the pecuniary limits of the court's jurisdiction, where an injunction was obtained on insufficient grounds or the suit fails and there was no reasonable ground for instituting it; and (ii) **Section 151**, invoking the wider doctrine of restitution, on the principle that no party shall suffer by an act of the court.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** Under s. 95 the applicant must show that the injunction was applied for on **insufficient grounds** or that there was **no reasonable ground** for instituting the suit — a real threshold. Losing a suit is not the same as having had no reasonable ground for bringing it.

**Illustration 5 — the suit that is barred.** D, whose money was recovered under a decree since reversed, files a fresh suit against P for refund of the money with interest.

- **Sword (plaintiff, resisting):** Application under **Order VII Rule 11(d)**. The suit is barred by **s. 144(2)**, because the relief claimed could have been obtained by an application under sub-s. (1). The bar appears from the statement in the plaint itself, which recites the decree, the recovery and the reversal.
- **Shield (defendant):** The only escape is to show that the relief could **not** have been obtained under sub-s. (1) — because he was not a party, or because the relief is not properly consequential. If neither is available, the plaint is liable to rejection, and D's proper course is to withdraw and apply under s. 144, which remains open for twelve years.

### 4. Procedural Interlocking Matrix

| Section 144 element | Interlocks with | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Application under s. 144 | **S. 2(2) CPC** | An order under s. 144 is a **decree**, and therefore appealable under s. 96. |
| Nature of the proceeding | O. XXI (execution) | Restitution is worked out in execution; the machinery of Order XXI applies to giving effect to the order. |
| Limitation | **Art. 136, Limitation Act, 1963** | Twelve years from the date the reversing decree becomes enforceable. |
| Forum | Explanation (a)–(c) | The **court of first instance**, regardless of which court reversed. |
| Bar on suits | S. 144(2); **O. VII R. 11(d)** | A suit for restitution obtainable under sub-s. (1) is barred, and the plaint is liable to rejection. |
| Mesne profits | **O. XX R. 12** | The principles and machinery for ascertaining mesne profits apply. |
| Interest | S. 34 CPC | The court's power to award interest, and the discretion as to rate and period. |
| Sale in execution | S. 65; **O. XXI RR. 92, 94** | A confirmed sale to a stranger auction-purchaser vests title, which survives the reversal. |
| Transfers pending appeal | **S. 52, Transfer of Property Act, 1882** | A transferee pendente lite takes subject to the result, which preserves the restitution remedy against him. |
| Restitution outside s. 144 | **S. 151 CPC** | Where s. 144 does not strictly apply, the wider doctrine is applied under inherent powers. |
| Wrongful interim orders | **S. 95 CPC** | Compensation for an injunction obtained on insufficient grounds, or for arrest or attachment before judgment. |
| Appeal | S. 96 | Because an order under s. 144 is a decree, a first appeal lies; it is not an Order XLIII appeal. |

**The restitution application — practical requisites**

| Requisite | Content |

| Title | "Application under Section 144 read with Section 151 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908" |

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Binayak Swain v. Ramesh Chandra Panigrahi**, AIR 1966 SC 948 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* Restitution was sought of property recovered under a decree subsequently reversed.

**Ratio decidendi:** The principle of restitution is that on the reversal of a decree the law imposes an obligation on the party who received the benefit of the erroneous decree to make restitution to the other party for what he has lost. The obligation arises automatically on the reversal, and it is the **duty of the court** to enforce it. The court, in making restitution, is bound to restore the parties, so far as they can be restored, to the same position they were in at the time when the court by its erroneous action displaced them.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"On reversal of a decree the obligation to make restitution arises automatically, and it is the duty of the court which passed the erroneous decree to enforce it; the successful party need not sue."
:::

---

**South Eastern Coalfields Ltd. v. State of M.P.**, (2003) 8 SCC 648 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* Amounts had been collected, or withheld, under the protection of interim orders which were ultimately vacated.

**Ratio decidendi:** The doctrine of restitution rests on **actus curiae neminem gravabit** — no one shall suffer by an act of the court. Section 144 is **not exhaustive** of the doctrine; the court has inherent jurisdiction to order restitution in cases falling outside the section, including in respect of benefits obtained under **interim orders** subsequently found to be unsustainable. A party who has enjoyed the benefit of an interim order must, on its reversal, be placed under an obligation to restore the position, including by payment of interest, because he has enjoyed a benefit to which he was never entitled. There is no question of a party being permitted to retain the fruits of litigation which it has ultimately lost.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Section 144 is not exhaustive of the doctrine of restitution. A litigant who has enjoyed a benefit under an interim order which is ultimately vacated must restore that benefit, with interest, and the court may so direct in exercise of its inherent jurisdiction."
:::

---

**Chinnammal v. P. Arumugham**, (1990) 1 SCC 513 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* Property sold in execution of a decree which was subsequently reversed; the auction-purchaser's title fell for consideration.

**Ratio decidendi:** The rule protecting an auction-purchaser applies to a **bona fide stranger purchaser** who is not a party to the decree; his title, once the sale is confirmed, is not affected by the subsequent reversal of the decree. But where the **decree-holder himself** is the purchaser, or where the purchaser is not a stranger but is closely connected with the decree-holder, he purchases subject to the result of the appeal and restitution may be ordered against him.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A bona fide stranger auction-purchaser's confirmed title survives the reversal of the decree; a decree-holder who purchases in his own execution buys subject to the appeal and is liable to restitution."
:::

---

**Lal Bhagwant Singh v. Rai Sahib Lala Sri Kishen Das**, AIR 1953 SC 439 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* The scope of the court's power under Section 144 in respect of consequential orders arose.

**Ratio decidendi:** Section 144 confers a wide power to make any order which is "properly consequential" on the variation or reversal, and this expressly includes interest, damages, compensation and mesne profits. The obligation is on the party who received the benefit; the court's duty is to restore the *status quo ante* so far as is practicable.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"The court's power under Section 144 is not limited to ordering the return of what was taken; it extends to every order properly consequential on the reversal, including interest and mesne profits."
:::

---

**Bhagwant Singh** and the limitation question — **Ram Naresh Sharma** line

*Facts:* Whether an application under Section 144 is governed by Article 136 (execution) or Article 137 (residuary) of the Limitation Act, 1963.

**Ratio (dominant line):** An order under Section 144 is a **decree** by force of Section 2(2), and restitution is worked out in execution. Accordingly an application under Section 144 attracts **Article 136** — twelve years from the date when the decree or order sought to be enforced becomes enforceable, that is, from the date of the decree reversing or varying the original decree.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"An application under Section 144 is an application in execution and is governed by Article 136 of the Limitation Act, 1963 — twelve years from the date of the reversing decree."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Citations, bench strength and current status must be confirmed against the official report and a current citator. The limitation question in particular has been the subject of differing High Court views, and the position in the relevant High Court should be checked before an application is filed close to the outer limit.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**The restitution checklist**

| # | Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Has the decree or order actually been **varied or reversed**? | Without reversal there is no s. 144. A decree merely stayed, or an appeal merely admitted, does not engage the section. |
| 2 | Is my client a **party** to the decree, or his legal representative? | A stranger cannot apply; he must sue, and s. 144(2) does not bar his suit. |
| 3 | Which is the **court of first instance**? | Explanation (a) — file there, not in the appellate court that reversed. |
| 4 | Exactly **what was taken** — money, possession, goods? | Each head of restitution must be separately pleaded and quantified. |
| 5 | Is each head **properly consequential**? | Business loss and reputational damage usually are not; interest and mesne profits are. |
| 6 | What **interest** — rate, period, simple or compound? | This is where the contest is. Plead a rate with a justification. |
| 7 | Was the property **sold in execution**, and to whom? | Stranger auction-purchaser: title protected, claim the proceeds. Decree-holder purchaser: claim the property. |
| 8 | Is the application within **twelve years** of the reversing decree? | Article 136. Diarise from the date of the reversing decree. |
| 9 | Is the opposite party about to **dissipate** the fruits? | Seek an interim order in the restitution proceeding; s. 151 supports it. |

::: danger The five errors in restitution applications
1. **Filing in the appellate court.** Explanation (a) sends the application to the court of first instance. Filing in the High Court that allowed the appeal wastes months.
2. **Filing a suit instead of an application.** Section 144(2) bars it, and the plaint will be rejected under Order VII Rule 11(d).
3. **Invoking Section 151 where Section 144 applies.** Where the Code makes express provision, inherent powers are excluded. Head the application "Section 144 read with Section 151" — the second limb covers the residue, but the first must lead.
4. **Failing to claim interest and mesne profits.** They are expressly within the section, and a restitution order that omits them under-compensates. They must be claimed and quantified, not left to the court.
5. **Assuming the auction-purchaser's title falls with the decree.** It does not, if he is a bona fide stranger and the sale has been confirmed.
:::

**Model prayer — application under Section 144**

> It is therefore most respectfully prayed that this Hon'ble Court may be pleased to:
>
> **(a)** Direct the Respondent to restore to the Applicant vacant and peaceful possession of the property described in Schedule A hereto, possession whereof was delivered to the Respondent on ____ in execution of the decree dated ____ passed in Suit No. ____ of 20__, which decree has since been reversed by the judgment and decree dated ____ passed in First Appeal No. ____ of 20__;
>
> **(b)** Direct the Respondent to refund to the Applicant the sum of ₹____ recovered from the Applicant in execution of the said decree, together with interest thereon at the rate of ____ per cent per annum from ____, being the date of recovery, until payment;
>
> **(c)** Direct an enquiry into the mesne profits payable by the Respondent for the period from ____ until the date of restoration of possession, and decree the amount so ascertained in favour of the Applicant;
>
> **(d)** Direct the refund of the costs of ₹____ paid by the Applicant under the reversed decree;
>
> **(e)** Pending the hearing and final disposal of this application, restrain the Respondent from alienating, transferring or creating any third-party right in the property described in Schedule A;
>
> **(f)** Award the costs of this application to the Applicant;
>
> **(g)** Pass such other and further orders as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit in the interests of justice.

**Strategic timing**

| Step | When |
|---|---|
| Restitution application | Immediately on the reversing decree. Do not wait for the appeal period to expire — the obligation arises on reversal, and delay allows dissipation. |
| Interim restraint in the restitution proceeding | With the application, under s. 151, to preserve the fruits. |
| Where a further appeal is pending | The opposite party will seek a stay of restitution. Resist on the footing that the decree in his favour has been reversed, and offer security if pressed. |
| If the decree is again restored on further appeal | Restitution runs the other way. Section 144 is symmetrical. |

## Section 148A — Caveat

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **148A. Right to lodge a caveat.** — **(1)** Where an application is expected to be made, or has been made, in a suit or proceeding instituted, or about to be instituted, in a Court, any person claiming a right to appear before the Court on the hearing of such application may lodge a caveat in respect thereof.
>
> **(2)** Where a caveat has been lodged under sub-section (1), the person by whom the caveat has been lodged (hereinafter referred to as the caveator) shall serve a notice of the caveat by registered post, acknowledgement due, on the person by whom the application has been or is expected to be made under sub-section (1).
>
> **(3)** Where, after a caveat has been lodged under sub-section (1), any application is filed in any suit or proceeding, the Court shall serve a notice of the application on the caveator.
>
> **(4)** Where a notice of any caveat has been served on the applicant, he shall forthwith furnish the caveator, at the caveator's expense, with a copy of the application made by him and also with copies of any paper or document which has been or may be filed by him in support of the application.
>
> **(5)** Where a caveat has been lodged under sub-section (1), such caveat shall not remain in force after the expiry of ninety days from the date on which it was lodged unless the application referred to in sub-section (1) has been made before the expiry of the said period.

**Statutory mechanics**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "Where an application **is expected to be made, or has been made**" | Anticipatory and current | A caveat may be lodged before the application is filed, and even before the suit is instituted. That is its whole utility. |
| "in a suit or proceeding instituted, **or about to be instituted**" | Wide | Covers a suit not yet filed, an appeal not yet filed, and an execution proceeding. |
| "**any person claiming a right to appear**" | Standing requirement | The caveator must claim a right to be heard on the application. He need not yet be a party. |
| "the caveator **shall serve** a notice of the caveat by registered post, acknowledgement due" | **Mandatory duty on the caveator** | Lodging the caveat in court is not enough. The caveator must serve the prospective applicant. This is the step most often missed. |
| "the Court **shall serve** a notice of the application on the caveator" | **Mandatory duty on the court** | This is the operative protection: the court cannot proceed ex parte against a caveator. |
| Sub-s. (4): the applicant "**shall forthwith furnish**" copies, "at the caveator's expense" | Mandatory duty on the applicant | The applicant must supply the application and supporting papers. The cost is borne by the caveator. |
| Sub-s. (5): "shall **not remain in force after the expiry of ninety days**" | Hard expiry | A caveat lives **ninety days**, unless the anticipated application is made within that period. Thereafter it must be re-lodged. |

::: danger Ninety days, and the two service obligations
Two things kill caveats. The first is **expiry** — ninety days, and the diary entry must be made on the day of lodging. The second is **failure to serve the prospective applicant under sub-section (2)** — a caveat lodged in court but never served is a caveat the applicant can honestly say he did not know about, and while sub-section (3) obliges the court to give notice, the practical protection comes from the applicant's own knowledge.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — what a caveat actually does.**

It does one thing: it makes it procedurally impossible for the other side to obtain an order against the caveator without the caveator being heard. It is not a defence, it does not stay anything, and it decides nothing. It converts a potential ex parte hearing into a contested one.

Its practical value is greatest where the anticipated order would be very hard to undo — an ex parte injunction, a stay of a decree the caveator is about to execute, an ex parte order in appeal, an ex parte appointment of a receiver.

**Sub-module B — the four obligations, in sequence.**

| Step | Who | Obligation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Caveator | Lodge the caveat in the court in which the application is expected |
| 2 | **Caveator** | **Serve notice of the caveat** on the expected applicant, by registered post AD — sub-s. (2) |
| 3 | **Court** | On any application being filed, **serve notice of the application** on the caveator — sub-s. (3) |
| 4 | **Applicant** | On being served with the caveat notice, **forthwith furnish** the caveator with a copy of the application and all supporting papers, at the caveator's expense — sub-s. (4) |

**Sub-module C — the effect of an order passed in breach.**

Where an order is passed ex parte in disregard of a subsisting caveat of which the court had notice, the order is passed in breach of a mandatory statutory requirement. The consequence in practice is not that the order is a nullity, but that it is liable to be recalled or vacated on the caveator's application, and the caveator has a strong claim to costs. The correct course is a prompt application to the same court, drawing attention to the caveat and its date of lodging.

**Sub-module D — where caveats are used.**

| Situation | Why |
|---|---|
| Anticipating a suit with an ex parte injunction application | The commonest use. A defendant who knows a suit is coming lodges a caveat in every court that could entertain it. |
| Anticipating an appeal against a decree in your favour | Prevents an ex parte stay of execution. |
| Anticipating an application for a receiver, or for attachment before judgment | Prevents ex parte appointment or attachment. |
| Anticipating a writ petition | Caveats are lodged in the High Court under the corresponding practice, and in the Supreme Court under its own rules. |
| Execution proceedings | Prevents ex parte orders on objections. |

**Sub-module E — the practical limitation.**

A caveat must be lodged in the court in which the application will be made. Where the prospective plaintiff has a choice of forum — several courts having territorial jurisdiction — the caveator must lodge in each. This is a real cost and a real gap, and it is why caveats are lodged in blocks: the district court, the High Court, and any court in whose jurisdiction the property lies.

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — the anticipated injunction.** D receives a legal notice from P alleging breach of a non-compete covenant and threatening a suit for injunction. D knows that if P obtains an ex parte injunction, D's business stops for the weeks it takes to have it vacated.

- **Sword (defendant, as caveator):** Lodge a caveat under s. 148A in the district court and in the High Court, immediately, and **serve P by registered post AD** under sub-s. (2). Diarise the ninetieth day. When P files, the court must issue notice under sub-s. (3), and P must furnish D with the plaint, application and documents under sub-s. (4). D appears on the first date and the injunction application is heard contested — which, in practice, is the difference between an order and no order.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** P's answer is to establish genuine urgency such that even a contested hearing on the first date would defeat the object — a difficult argument once the caveator is present in court and able to give an undertaking. P should also verify whether the caveat has expired.

**Illustration 2 — the caveat that expired.** D lodges a caveat on 1 March. P files the suit and the injunction application on 15 June — the ninety-fifth day.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** The caveat has ceased to be in force under sub-s. (5). The court is under no obligation to notify D, and an ex parte order may be made.
- **Shield (defendant):** There is no answer after the event. The discipline is to **re-lodge on the eighty-fifth day**, every time, until the threat has passed. Caveats are renewed, not lodged once.

**Illustration 3 — order obtained despite a caveat.** D lodges and serves a caveat. P files and obtains an ex parte injunction without the court's registry linking the caveat.

- **Sword (defendant):** Immediate application to the same court to recall the order, annexing the caveat with its filing endorsement and date, and the registered post acknowledgement evidencing service on P under sub-s. (2). Where P knew of the caveat and did not disclose it, that is a suppression of a material fact and engages the **first proviso to Order XXXIX Rule 4** as well — mandatory vacation of an ex parte injunction obtained on a knowingly misleading statement.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** P must show that he did not know of the caveat — which is why the caveator's service under sub-s. (2) matters so much. If service is proved, P has no answer.

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Nirmal Chandra Dutta v. Girindra Narayan Roy**, AIR 1978 Cal 492 — Calcutta High Court.

*Facts:* An early consideration of the scope of Section 148A, inserted by the Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act, 1976.

**Ratio decidendi:** The object of Section 148A is to give a person who apprehends that an order may be made against him behind his back an opportunity of being heard. The word "application" is to be construed widely, and includes an application for interim relief in a suit or appeal, whether or not the caveator is already a party. The provision is remedial and is to be construed so as to advance its object.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Section 148A is remedial and is to be construed liberally; the caveator need not already be a party to the proceeding in which the application is expected."
:::

---

**Kattil Vayalil Parkkum Koolikkara v. Mannil Paadikayil Kadeesa Umma**, AIR 1991 Ker 411 — Kerala High Court (Full Bench).

*Facts:* The consequences of proceeding in disregard of a subsisting caveat were considered.

**Ratio decidendi:** The obligation under sub-section (3) on the court to serve notice of the application on the caveator is **mandatory**. An order passed in ignorance or disregard of a subsisting caveat is not a nullity, but is liable to be recalled or set aside on the caveator's application, and the court should ordinarily do so and consider awarding costs.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"An order made in disregard of a subsisting caveat is liable to be recalled on the caveator's application; the obligation on the court to issue notice under Section 148A(3) is mandatory."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
These are High Court authorities; the position in your High Court should be checked, along with any Rules of that Court prescribing the form of a caveat and the manner of its listing. The corresponding practice in the Supreme Court is governed by the Supreme Court Rules and not by Section 148A.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

::: danger The four ways a caveat fails
1. **Not served on the applicant under sub-section (2).** Lodging in court is half the job. Without the registered post AD acknowledgement, you cannot prove the applicant knew.
2. **Expired.** Ninety days. Diarise day 85 and re-lodge. There is no extension.
3. **Lodged in the wrong court.** Where the plaintiff has a choice of forum, lodge in each. A caveat in the district court does not protect you from an application in the High Court.
4. **Lodged without an affidavit of interest.** The caveator must claim "a right to appear". State the interest and the apprehension shortly and specifically.
:::

## Section 151 — Saving of inherent powers of Court

### 1. Verbatim Bare Act Text & Statutory Mechanics

> **151. Saving of inherent powers of Court.** — Nothing in this Code shall be deemed to limit or otherwise affect the inherent power of the Court to make such orders as may be necessary for the ends of justice or to prevent abuse of the process of the Court.

And, for completeness, the two neighbouring provisions constantly invoked with it:

> **148. Enlargement of time.** — Where any period is fixed or granted by the Court for the doing of any act prescribed or allowed by this Code, the Court may, in its discretion, from time to time, enlarge such period, not exceeding thirty days in total, even though the period originally fixed or granted may have expired.

> **149. Power to make up deficiency of court-fees.** — Where the whole or any part of any fee prescribed for any document by the law for the time being in force relating to court-fees has not been paid, the Court may, in its discretion, at any stage, allow the person, by whom such fee is payable, to pay the whole or part, as the case may be, of such court-fee; and upon such payment the document, in respect of which such fee is payable, shall have the same force and effect as if such fee had been paid in the first instance.

**Statutory mechanics**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| S. 151: "**Nothing in this Code shall be deemed to limit**" | **Saving clause, not a conferring clause** | Section 151 does not *confer* power. It *recognises and saves* a power the court already possesses by virtue of being a court. This distinction decides most Section 151 arguments. |
| "the **inherent** power of the Court" | Pre-existing | The power inheres in the court as a court of justice. It cannot be exercised by a tribunal that is not a court, unless conferred. |
| "such orders as may be **necessary**" | Necessity, not convenience | The test is necessity for the ends of justice, not desirability. |
| "for the **ends of justice**" | First limb | Positive — to do what justice requires where the Code is silent. |
| "or to **prevent abuse of the process** of the Court" | Second limb | Negative — to stop the court's machinery being used oppressively or fraudulently. |
| S. 148: "**not exceeding thirty days in total**" | Cap | Post-1999, the enlargement power under s. 148 is capped at thirty days in total. This is the express limit that s. 151 cannot be used to circumvent. |
| S. 149: "at **any stage**"; "**as if such fee had been paid in the first instance**" | Retrospective validation | Deficit court fee made up under s. 149 relates back to institution — which can save a suit that would otherwise be time-barred. |

::: warning The two absolute limits on Section 151
**First:** inherent powers cannot be exercised in conflict with, or to circumvent, an **express provision** of the Code. If the Code provides a procedure, that procedure must be followed; if the Code prohibits something, s. 151 cannot permit it. If the Code expressly caps a power (s. 148's thirty days), s. 151 cannot uncap it.

**Second:** inherent powers cannot confer **jurisdiction** the court does not have. Section 151 is a power to regulate procedure in aid of jurisdiction already possessed, not a source of jurisdiction.
:::

### 2. Plain-English Conceptual Demystification

**Sub-module A — the negative definition.**

The clearest way to understand Section 151 is by what it cannot do:

| Section 151 **cannot** | Because |
|---|---|
| Override an express provision of the Code | The Code's express provisions occupy the field; inherent powers fill gaps, they do not rewrite text |
| Be used where a specific remedy is provided | Where the Code gives a remedy — s. 144 for restitution, O. IX R. 13 for setting aside an ex parte decree, O. XXXIX R. 2A for breach of injunction, O. XLVII for review — that remedy must be used |
| Confer jurisdiction | A court without jurisdiction over the subject-matter cannot acquire it under s. 151 |
| Be used to grant relief expressly barred | E.g. to extend a period which the Code declares to be absolute |
| Be used as a substitute for an appeal or review | A litigant dissatisfied with an order must appeal or seek review; s. 151 is not a back door |
| Be used to set aside an order on the merits | Recall for procedural error, yes; reconsideration of merits, no — that is review under s. 114 and Order XLVII |

**Sub-module B — where Section 151 legitimately operates.**

| Use | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Recall of an order obtained by fraud** | A judgment obtained by fraud is a nullity; the court has inherent power to recall it. See also s. 44 (fraud as a ground against a foreign judgment) and the general principle. |
| **Recall of an order passed without jurisdiction or in violation of natural justice** | Distinguish recall (procedural) from review (merits). |
| **Correction of the record** where s. 152 (clerical errors) does not strictly apply | Section 152 covers clerical and arithmetical slips; s. 151 covers the residue. |
| **Restitution outside Section 144** | The doctrine of restitution is wider than s. 144 — *South Eastern Coalfields*. |
| **Consolidation of suits** | The Code does not expressly provide for consolidation. It is done under s. 151. |
| **Staying a suit outside Section 10** | Where s. 10's strict conditions are not met but a stay is plainly desirable, courts stay under s. 151 — a well-established use. |
| **Recall of witnesses, adjournment on terms, regulating the court's own procedure** | Ancillary to the conduct of the trial. |
| **Striking out proceedings that are an abuse** | Vexatious or repetitive proceedings; the second limb of the section. |
| **Interim orders ancillary to an application** where the Code is silent | E.g. preserving the fruits pending a restitution application. |

**Sub-module C — Section 151 and its express-provision counterparts.**

| Situation | Express provision | Is s. 151 available? |
|---|---|---|
| Setting aside an ex parte decree | O. IX R. 13 | **No** — use Order IX Rule 13 |
| Setting aside dismissal for default | O. IX R. 9 | **No** |
| Review of a judgment | S. 114, O. XLVII | **No** |
| Amendment of a pleading | O. VI R. 17 | **No** |
| Restitution after reversal | S. 144 | **No**, where s. 144 applies; **yes** for cases outside it |
| Breach of a temporary injunction | O. XXXIX R. 2A | **No** for punishment; **yes** for restoration, which R. 2A does not cover |
| Extension of a period fixed by the court | S. 148 (capped at 30 days) | **No** to exceed the cap |
| Correction of a clerical error in a decree | S. 152 | **No** |
| Consolidation of suits | *None* | **Yes** |
| Stay of a suit not covered by s. 10 | S. 10 (strict conditions) | **Yes** |
| Recall of an order obtained by fraud | *None* | **Yes** |

**Sub-module D — Section 148 and Section 149, briefly.**

- **Section 148** allows a court to enlarge a period **it has itself fixed**, even after expiry, but the total enlargement is capped at **thirty days**. It has no application to periods fixed by statute.
- **Section 149** is more powerful than it looks. Where a plaint is filed with deficit court fee and the deficiency is later made up with the court's leave, the payment **relates back to the date of institution**. A suit filed on the last day of limitation with insufficient fee can therefore be saved. The discretion is judicial, and is exercised having regard to whether the deficiency was bona fide.

::: tip Section 149 is a limitation-saving provision
Because payment under s. 149 has "the same force and effect as if such fee had been paid in the first instance", a suit instituted within time but with deficit fee is treated as instituted on the original date once the deficiency is made good. Contrast a plaint **rejected** under Order VII Rule 11(c) for failure to make up the deficiency within the time fixed — there, the fresh plaint under Order VII Rule 13 is a fresh institution and limitation is not saved.
:::

### 3. Courtroom & Practical Illustrations

**Illustration 1 — the decree obtained by fraud.** P obtains a decree against D by deliberately suppressing service, filing a false affidavit of service, and proceeding ex parte. D learns of the decree two years later, when execution is levied.

- **Sword (defendant):** Two routes, and the choice matters. **Order IX Rule 13** — application to set aside the ex parte decree, but limitation is thirty days from knowledge under Article 123 of the Limitation Act, 1963, and the application is to the court that passed the decree. **Section 151** — application to recall the decree as one obtained by fraud on the court, which is a nullity. The prudent course is to file under **Order IX Rule 13 read with Section 151 and Section 5 of the Limitation Act**, pleading the fraud as the ground for both.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** Contend that Order IX Rule 13 is the express provision and that s. 151 cannot be used to escape its limitation. This is a genuine argument, and the answer to it is that a judgment obtained by fraud stands on a different footing — fraud vitiates the most solemn proceedings.

**Illustration 2 — consolidation.** Three suits are pending between overlapping parties in respect of the same property: a suit for possession, a suit for injunction, and a suit for declaration. The evidence is substantially common.

- **Sword (applicant):** The Code contains no provision for consolidation of suits. The application is made under **Section 151**: consolidation avoids multiplicity, prevents conflicting findings, and saves the court's and the parties' time — squarely "necessary for the ends of justice". Ask for a common trial with evidence recorded in the lead suit and read in the others, and for separate judgments and decrees.
- **Shield (respondent):** Resist on the ground that the issues are distinct, that the parties are not identical, and that consolidation would prejudice a party in the advanced suit by yoking it to one at the pleadings stage.

**Illustration 3 — the deficit court fee filed on the last day.** P files a suit on the last day of limitation with court fee short by ₹1,20,000, having mis-valued the relief.

- **Sword (plaintiff):** Application under **Section 149** for leave to make up the deficiency. On payment, the plaint has "the same force and effect as if such fee had been paid in the first instance" — the suit is treated as instituted on the original date and limitation is saved. Plead that the deficiency was bona fide and arose from a genuine valuation question.
- **Shield (defendant):** Section 149 is discretionary. Contend that the under-valuation was deliberate and designed to buy time, and that the discretion should be refused, whereupon the plaint stands liable to rejection under **Order VII Rule 11(c)** and the fresh plaint under Rule 13 would be out of time. This is a real and sometimes decisive argument.

**Illustration 4 — Section 151 invoked where Section 144 applies.** D applies under s. 151 for refund of money recovered under a decree since reversed, rather than under s. 144.

- **Sword (plaintiff, resisting):** Section 144 makes express provision for exactly this, and inherent powers cannot be invoked where the Code provides a specific remedy. The application is misconceived.
- **Shield (defendant):** The pragmatic answer is that courts routinely treat such an application as one under s. 144 and decide it on the merits, provided the substance is right and no limitation objection arises. But the safe course is always to head the application **"under Section 144 read with Section 151"** — the first limb for the case within the section, the second for anything falling outside it.

**Illustration 5 — abuse of process.** P files successive suits on the same cause of action in different courts, withdrawing each when an adverse order appears imminent, and re-filing.

- **Sword (defendant):** Application under **Section 151**, second limb — to prevent abuse of the process of the court. Combine it with **Order XXIII Rule 1(4)** (withdrawal without leave bars a fresh suit on the same subject-matter), **Section 10** (stay of a subsequently instituted suit), and **Section 11** where a decision has been rendered. Seek dismissal and exemplary costs.
- **Shield (plaintiff):** Distinguish the causes of action in each suit. If they are genuinely different, neither Order XXIII Rule 1(4) nor s. 151 assists.

### 5. Landmark & Modern Jurisprudence

**Manohar Lal Chopra v. Rai Bahadur Rao Raja Seth Hiralal**, AIR 1962 SC 527 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* A temporary injunction restraining a party from proceeding with a suit in another court was granted; the question was whether the court had power to do so given that Order XXXIX did not cover the case.

**Ratio decidendi:** The inherent power of the court is **not conferred** by Section 151 but is saved by it; it is a power which the court possesses by virtue of its duty to do justice. Where the Code contains no express provision covering the matter, the court may act under Section 151. But the inherent power **cannot be exercised in conflict with an express provision** of the Code, nor to do what the Code prohibits. The power is to be exercised sparingly, in exceptional circumstances, and for the ends of justice or to prevent abuse of process.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Section 151 does not confer power; it saves a power which inheres in every court. That power may be exercised where the Code is silent, but never in conflict with an express provision."
:::

---

**Ram Chand and Sons Sugar Mills (P) Ltd. v. Kanhayalal Bhargava**, AIR 1966 SC 1899 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* The question was whether an application which the Code did not expressly provide for could be entertained under Section 151.

**Ratio decidendi:** The court has inherent power to make orders necessary for the ends of justice where the Code does not deal with the matter. But if the Code expressly or by necessary implication covers the field, the inherent power cannot be resorted to; and it cannot be used to nullify the procedure prescribed by the Code.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Where the Code expressly or by necessary implication covers the field, Section 151 is not available; the inherent power operates only in the gaps."
:::

---

**K.K. Velusamy v. N. Palanisamy**, (2011) 11 SCC 275 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* An application to reopen evidence and recall a witness after the close of evidence was made under Section 151.

**Ratio decidendi:** Section 151 recognises the discretionary power inherent in every court as a necessary corollary for rendering justice in accordance with law, to do what is "right" and undo what is "wrong". The power may be used to reopen evidence or recall a witness where it is necessary for a just decision, but such applications are to be considered with **circumspection**, must not be used to fill lacunae or to protract proceedings, and should ordinarily be allowed on terms as to costs. The power is not unlimited: it cannot be exercised where its exercise would be inconsistent with any specific provision.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Section 151 may be used to permit reopening of evidence where necessary for a just decision, but not to fill a lacuna in a party's case, and ordinarily only on terms as to costs."
:::

---

**Padam Sen v. State of U.P.**, AIR 1961 SC 218 — Supreme Court of India.

*Facts:* A court had appointed a commissioner to seize account books, purportedly under Section 151.

**Ratio decidendi:** The inherent powers of the court are in addition to the powers specifically conferred by the Code, and are complementary to them. They cannot be exercised so as to **prejudice the rights of a party** which the Code has not itself curtailed, and cannot be used to do something which is against the substantive rights of parties. Inherent powers relate to procedure; they do not extend to the making of orders affecting substantive rights not otherwise available.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"Section 151 is a procedural power. It cannot be used to make an order which affects the substantive rights of a party in a manner the Code does not authorise."
:::

---

**Sunderlal v. Nandram** and the line on **fraud**

**Ratio (settled principle):** A judgment or decree obtained by **fraud on the court** is a nullity and may be recalled by the court that passed it, in exercise of its inherent jurisdiction, notwithstanding the absence of an express provision. Fraud vitiates the most solemn proceedings, and no court will allow its process to be the instrument of fraud.

::: tip Practitioner Takeaway
"A decree obtained by playing a fraud on the court is a nullity and may be recalled at any time by the court which passed it; the limitation applicable to Order IX Rule 13 does not shield it."
:::

::: info Verify before citing
Citations, bench strength and current status must be confirmed against the official report and a current citator before reliance. The boundary between Section 151 and the express provisions of the Code — particularly Order IX Rule 13, Order XLVII and Section 144 — continues to be refined, and the most recent authority on the specific interaction should be checked.
:::

### 6. The Advocate's Toolkit

**The Section 151 diagnostic — ask these three questions in order**

| # | Question | If yes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Does the Code make **express provision** for what I want? | Use it. Section 151 is unavailable, and invoking it invites a threshold objection. |
| 2 | Does the Code **prohibit** what I want, expressly or by necessary implication? | Section 151 cannot help. Look for a different remedy. |
| 3 | Is the Code **silent**, and is the order **necessary** — not merely convenient — for the ends of justice or to prevent abuse of process? | Section 151 applies. Plead the necessity, and plead why no express provision covers the case. |

::: danger The five reasons Section 151 applications fail
1. **An express provision covers the case.** The commonest reason. Before drafting, run the diagnostic above.
2. **The application is really a review.** Dissatisfaction with the merits of an order is met by review under Section 114 and Order XLVII, or by appeal — not by s. 151.
3. **The application seeks to confer jurisdiction.** Section 151 regulates procedure in aid of jurisdiction possessed; it does not create it.
4. **Only convenience is shown, not necessity.** The section speaks of orders "necessary" for the ends of justice.
5. **The application affects substantive rights.** *Padam Sen* — inherent powers are procedural.
:::

**Model prayer — application under Section 151 for consolidation**

## Chapter consolidation — three provisions, three functions

| | **Section 144** | **Section 148A** | **Section 151** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Function | Undo the effect of a decree that has fallen | Ensure you are heard before an order is made against you | Do what justice requires where the Code is silent |
| Nature | Mandatory duty on the court | Right of the caveator; mandatory duty on the court and the applicant | Saved power, discretionary, residual |
| Who moves | A **party** entitled to a benefit | Any person claiming a right to appear | Any party, or the court suo motu |
| Forum | Court of **first instance** (Explanation (a)) | The court in which the application is expected | The court seised of the matter |
| Time | **12 years** — Art. 136, Limitation Act, 1963 | Caveat lives **90 days**; re-lodge | No period; but delay is a discretionary bar |
| Order is a decree? | **Yes** — s. 2(2); appeal under s. 96 | No | Depends on the order |
| Chief limitation | Relief must be "properly consequential"; suit barred by sub-s. (2) | Must be served on the applicant; expires at 90 days | Cannot override an express provision or confer jurisdiction |

::: tip The five sentences worth memorising from this chapter
1. **"Actus curiae neminem gravabit."** No party shall suffer by an act of the court. That is the whole of Section 144, and the reason it is a duty and not a discretion.
2. **"Section 144 is not exhaustive of the doctrine of restitution."** *South Eastern Coalfields.* Benefits enjoyed under interim orders later vacated must be restored, with interest, under the court's inherent jurisdiction.
3. **"A stranger auction-purchaser's confirmed title survives the reversal; a decree-holder purchaser's does not."** This single distinction decides most restitution disputes over execution sales.
4. **"A caveat lives ninety days, and must be served on the expected applicant."** Both halves are essential; either omission destroys it.
5. **"Section 151 saves a power; it does not confer one, and it never overrides an express provision."** Run the three-question diagnostic before invoking it.
:::

::: warning Before you file
Sections 144, 148A and 151 must be read with Sections 2(2), 96, 114, 148, 149 and 152 of the Code, with Order VII Rules 11 and 13, Order IX Rules 9 and 13, Order XXI, Order XXXIX and Order XLVII, and with Articles 123, 136 and 137 of the Limitation Act, 1963. State amendments and High Court Rules affect the practice on caveats in particular. Verify the current text and the status of every judgment before filing or citing.
:::
